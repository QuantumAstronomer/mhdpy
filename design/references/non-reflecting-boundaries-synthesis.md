# Synthesis of non-reflecting boundaries

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on boundary conditions that let waves leave a computational domain, above all where the flow crosses the boundary subsonically, kept in `reference-works/Non-Reflecting Boundaries/Docs`. The collection is gathered around the review of [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, [doi:10.1146/annurev.fluid.36.050802.121930](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.fluid.36.050802.121930)) on artificial boundary conditions for compressible flow (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §2.3, eq. 8), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Works that the papers cite are cited as the papers cite them. Four classical papers have no free copy: those of [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), [Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) (1980) and [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987, [1990](#thompson-1990)). They are known here only as the papers of the collection restate them, and each citation of them names the paper that restates it.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004) wrote the review at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena. It appeared in volume 36 of the Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics, on pp. 315–345. The local copy is the published version, which the repository CaltechAUTHORS holds with the permission of Annual Reviews. Its 33 pages hold the text in six sections up to p. 341, with five figures and nineteen numbered equations, the literature cited up to p. 345, and two pages of the table of contents of the volume. The key words are non-reflecting, absorbing and direct numerical simulation. Colonius thanks Jeff Eldredge and Tom Hagstrom for their comments on a draft. Much of the review was written during a sabbatical at the University of Cambridge, where he thanks Ann Dowling among others (acknowledgements).

### 1.2 Aims and scope

The review treats artificial boundary conditions for inflow, outflow and far-field, or radiation, problems, with an emphasis on techniques for compressible turbulent shear flows and the sound they generate ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §1). It addresses the unsteady compressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, and its techniques largely carry over to large-eddy simulation and to the unsteady Reynolds-averaged equations. Acoustic waves matter even where the radiated sound does not, as in instabilities of combustion and in the receptivity of shear layers. A boundary that encloses the region of interest closely also saves effort, provided that it does not damage the solution.

Two situations are distinguished. Where the flow near the boundary consists of small fluctuations about a uniform state, analytical solutions of the problem outside the domain yield the boundary conditions. The solution should not depend on where the domain is truncated, which is equivalent to requiring that no waves enter through the boundary. These non-reflecting boundary conditions have been studied for some thirty years. Their conceptual issues are largely solved, although demanding extensions and details of implementation remain, and earlier reviews cover them ([Givoli](#givoli-1991) 1991, [Tsynkov](#tsynkov-1998) 1998, [Hagstrom](#hagstrom-1999) 1999). Where the flow near the boundary is not a small disturbance of a uniform state, as in turbulent shear flows and in flows through pipes, nozzles, diffusers and turbomachinery, little theory exists. Truncating the domain then ignores the interaction of the region with its surroundings, often with unrealistic effects. A truncated mixing layer, for instance, can oscillate by itself through feedback from downstream ([Buell and Huerre](#buell-huerre-1988) 1988). Practical computations therefore use boundary conditions of low order. For better accuracy they sometimes add sacrificial regions next to the boundary, in which the equations are modified, called absorbing layers, fringe regions, buffer zones or sponges. Their free parameters must be tuned anew for each flow.

The review explores the gap between the largely mathematical theory of non-reflecting conditions and the largely empirical practice of crude conditions with buffer regions. It stresses that mathematics alone cannot fill this gap, which it calls an open problem of modelling, no less challenging, and arguably no less important, than the modelling of turbulence below the grid scale. §2 treats boundary conditions linearised about a uniform flow, §3 the effects of discretisation, §4 nonlinear characteristic conditions and §5 absorbing layers, and §6 summarises.

The summary finds the theory largely settled where the flow near the boundary can be linearised: local and non-local techniques give both accuracy and stability (§6). Stubborn problems remain: large gradients of the base flow, compatibility at the corners and edges of Cartesian domains, and places on a boundary where the velocity changes sign or the flow becomes sonic. Throughout, the continuous conditions interact subtly with the closures of the discrete equations, which have more degrees of freedom and so reflect additional, spurious modes, and this ties the well-posedness of the continuous problem to the stability of its discretisation. Where nonlinear effects near the boundary are significant, chiefly at the outflow of turbulent flow, little theory guides the conditions. Crude one-dimensional characteristic conditions used to be the rule, and ad hoc absorbing layers have lately improved the accuracy, but all of them need several parameters and blending functions, tuned by trial and error. The review traces this to a close analogy between artificial boundary conditions and the modelling of turbulence below the grid scale: one deals with scales too large for the domain, the other with scales too small for the mesh. It calls for new approaches to this largely unexplored problem of modelling.

## 2. Numerical methods

The methods fall into eight subjects: 2.1 characteristics and the number of boundary conditions, 2.2 absorbing conditions for waves at oblique incidence, 2.3 characteristic boundary conditions, 2.4 relaxation towards the far field at subsonic outflow and inflow, 2.5 absorbing layers, 2.6 characteristic conditions in finite-volume codes with ghost cells, 2.7 magnetohydrodynamics, and 2.8 open boundaries in astrophysical simulations of clouds and winds.

### 2.1 Characteristics and the number of boundary conditions

#### 2.1.1 Waves of a uniform flow at a planar boundary

Most artificial boundary conditions for compressible flow linearise the equations about a nearly uniform ambient state, the base flow ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2, fig. 1). A source region, which may be nonlinear and may hold bodies and forces, is surrounded by a region in which its disturbances have decayed, by geometric spreading or viscous diffusion, so far that they are linear. The aim is to simulate this linear region as if it extended to infinity, either by stretching the grid without end (§5.3) or by truncating the domain at an artificial boundary. The names absorbing, non-reflecting, radiation, characteristic, artificial and transparent all denote this aim, although they sometimes imply different techniques. For an inviscid gas without heat conduction and with constant specific heats, the linearised equations decouple into an advection equation for the vorticity, an advection equation for the entropy and a convected wave equation for the pressure. These couple only through boundary conditions or nonlinear interaction. Boundary conditions for the two advection equations are almost trivial: the fluctuations of vorticity and entropy are prescribed wherever the base flow enters the domain. Codes, however, solve for the velocity, which superposes acoustic and vortical motion, and splitting it into its irrotational and solenoidal parts requires the solution of Poisson equations, which is expensive.

At a locally planar boundary, a Fourier transform along the boundary and a Laplace transform in time decouple the linearised equations (§2.1). In two dimensions, with the boundary normal to $x$, a base flow $(u_o, v_o)$ with $u_o > 0$ and fluctuations $(u', v')$ of the velocity and $p'$ of the pressure, the equations read $\mathbf{q}_t + A\mathbf{q}_x + (B + v_oI)\mathbf{q}_y = 0$ for $\mathbf{q} = (v', u' + p', u' - p')^\mathrm{T}$ (eqs. 1 and 2). Here $A = \mathrm{diag}(u_o, u_o + 1, u_o - 1)$, and $B$ has the first row $(0, 1/2, -1/2)$ and the first column $(0, 1, -1)^\mathrm{T}$, its other entries being zero. The scales make the ambient density and speed of sound 1 and the ambient pressure $1/\gamma$. The entropy fluctuation $s' = p' - \rho'$ obeys an advection equation of its own and is set aside. With the variables $k$ dual to $y$ and $s$ dual to $t$, the transformed equations become $\hat{\mathbf{q}}_x = -(s + iv_ok)M(z)\hat{\mathbf{q}}$, with $z = ik/(s + iv_ok)$ and $M(z) = A^{-1}(I + zB)$ (eq. 3). The eigenvalues of $M$ are $\lambda_1 = 1/u_o$ for the vorticity wave and $\lambda_{2,3} = (u_o \mp \mu)/(u_o^2 - 1)$ for the two acoustic waves, with $\mu(z) = [1 - z^2(1 - u_o^2)]^{1/2}$ on the standard branch. Where $\mu$ is real, the solutions are propagating waves. Waves that meet the boundary at normal incidence, $k = z = 0$, recover the one-dimensional eigenvalues $1/u_o$, $1/(u_o + 1)$ and $1/(u_o - 1)$.

With $u_o > 0$, the boundary on the left is an inflow and that on the right an outflow. In supersonic flow, $u_o > 1$, all waves enter at the left and leave at the right. In subsonic flow, $0 < u_o < 1$, the vorticity wave and one acoustic wave travel to the right and enter at the inflow, while the other acoustic wave travels to the left, enters at the outflow and leaves at the inflow. A non-reflecting condition sets the amplitudes of the entering waves to zero. Solutions that do not propagate, where $\mu$ is imaginary, are labelled as right- or left-going algebraically, as in the theory of well-posedness ([Higdon](#higdon-1986) 1986). If $M$ can be block-diagonalised, as it can for the Euler equations, a matrix $Q$ with the blocks $Q^\mathrm{R}$ and $Q^\mathrm{L}$ of the right- and left-going solutions brings it into the form $QMQ^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(\Lambda^\mathrm{R}, \Lambda^\mathrm{L})$ (eq. 4). The entropy wave, which the analysis sets aside, travels with the flow like the vorticity wave. Counted with it, a subsonic inflow in two dimensions takes three conditions and a subsonic outflow one. If $u_o = 0$, the boundary is called characteristic, a degenerate case that needs a separate but straightforward treatment (§2.2). In turbulent shear flows, a small flow of entrainment usually keeps $u_o$ away from zero at the boundaries.

#### 2.1.2 Well-posedness

Boundary conditions must leave the problem well posed ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.2). The uniform Kreiss condition is sufficient ([Kreiss](#kreiss-1970) 1970, [Higdon](#higdon-1986) 1986), but stricter than necessary: it suffices that the reflection matrices of 2.2.1 stay bounded for all complex $z$ (Higdon 1986). Accuracy further demands that they be small on the imaginary axis of $z$, where the solutions are waves. As [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, [1990](#giles-1990)) first noted, the most direct approximation of the exact inflow condition is always ill posed. Relaxing the requirements on the matrix of the condition cures this, and yields a family of inflow conditions with three parameters ([Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) 2000), of which only a few have been tried.

#### 2.1.3 Characteristics in nonlinear and viscous flow

In one dimension, the classical method of characteristics identifies and separates the waves that travel to the left and to the right even in nonlinear flow ([Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) 1979, as restated by [Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). The three characteristic curves in the $x$–$t$ plane have the slopes $u$, $u + a$ and $u - a$, with the speed of sound $a$. A boundary condition is needed for every characteristic that enters the domain, by the sign of its slope. The quantities carried along the characteristics are the Riemann invariants, and conditions on them are called Riemann-invariant or characteristic conditions. Linearised about a uniform flow, they reduce to the one-dimensional conditions of 2.2.2 (eq. 8). In nonlinear flow, the incoming characteristics must carry information about the whole flow outside the domain, not merely about disturbances of a base state, and whether a characteristic enters or leaves can change along a boundary and in time. Only rarely does a solution of the exterior problem supply the incoming information, which must usually be set from little knowledge of the exterior.

Viscous flow takes a different number of conditions at inflow and outflow than inviscid flow ([Strikwerda](#strikwerda-1977) 1977, [Oliger and Sundström](#oliger-sundstrom-1978) 1978). The additional conditions usually set certain gradients, or combinations of them, to zero. Formulations so far seek a well-posed continuous problem and a stable discrete one, and avoid viscous boundary layers at the inflow and outflow by requiring that the inviscid conditions be recovered smoothly as the viscosity vanishes. Non-reflection of viscous conditions has been analysed only in one dimension, even when linearised. There, non-reflecting conditions exist that are strongly well posed, remain strongly stable when discretised, recover the inviscid conditions at infinite Reynolds number, and are maximally dissipative: without forcing at the boundary, they drive the energy down faster than any other one-dimensional condition of their general form. Many codes simply apply inviscid characteristic conditions and close the viscous derivatives with one-sided differences, which is probably justified only at high Reynolds numbers. In magnetohydrodynamics, the number of incoming modes ranges from none to eight with the normal velocity relative to the slow, Alfvén and fast speeds (2.7.2).

#### 2.1.4 Physical and soft conditions

[Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992, Table I) give the number of physical conditions that well-posedness requires in three dimensions: five at a supersonic inflow for the Euler and the Navier–Stokes equations alike, four and five at a subsonic inflow, none and four at a supersonic outflow, and one and four at a subsonic outflow. They distinguish these physical conditions, which state the known behaviour of the flow at a boundary independently of the numerical method, from soft conditions, which the method needs for the variables that no physical condition fixes (§2.1). Such variables are often extrapolated from the interior, but extrapolation acts as an additional physical condition of zero gradient, which over-specifies the boundary and whose compatibility with the physical conditions is unclear. They instead advance these variables at the boundary with the conservation equations themselves, as in the interior. One-sided differences then serve at the boundary, which costs no overall accuracy if their order is one less than that of the scheme (§2.1, after [Gustafsson](#gustafsson-1975) 1975). The waves that enter cannot be computed from the interior, since that would need differences against the direction of propagation, which are unstable. Their amplitudes must come from the physical conditions, expressed through those of the outgoing waves. For the Navier–Stokes equations, the waves are taken from the hyperbolic part alone and those of diffusion are neglected, an approximation that is probably well justified at the high Reynolds numbers of direct simulations.

### 2.2 Absorbing conditions for waves at oblique incidence

#### 2.2.1 Exact conditions and their non-locality

Exact non-reflecting conditions set the amplitudes of all incoming waves to zero: $Q^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ at the inflow and $Q^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ at the outflow ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.2, eq. 5). A forcing on the right-hand sides would instead excite chosen entropic, vortical and acoustic waves. Since the block-diagonalisation of $M$ is not unique, eq. 5 is a family of exact conditions, whose matrices [Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) (2000) and [Hagstrom and Goodrich](#hagstrom-goodrich-2002) (2002) give in full. Approximate matrices $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}$ and $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}$ let some reflection through, measured by reflection coefficients that give the amplitudes of the incoming waves in terms of those of the outgoing ones. With the matrix $T(z)$ of the eigenvectors of $M$, the decoupled amplitudes are $\hat{\mathbf{f}} = T^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ (eq. 6). A subsonic outflow has the two coefficients $\rho_{\Omega \to a^-}$ and $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-}$, of the upstream acoustic wave $a^-$ produced by the outgoing vorticity wave $\Omega$ and by the outgoing downstream acoustic wave $a^+$. A subsonic inflow has $\rho_{a^- \to \Omega}$ and $\rho_{a^- \to a^+}$, of the incoming waves produced by the outgoing upstream acoustic wave (eq. 7).

**[GK]** Inserting $\hat{\mathbf{q}} = T^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{R} + T^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{L}$ into the approximate outflow condition $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{L} = -(\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}T^\mathrm{L})^{-1}\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}T^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{R}$, and the inflow condition $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{R} = -(\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}T^\mathrm{R})^{-1}\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}T^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{L}$. Eq. 6 states these matrices without the minus sign, which leaves the size of a reflection unchanged. It names the first $\varrho^\mathrm{L}$, as belonging to the outflow, whereas the text and eq. 7 give the name $\varrho^\mathrm{L}$ to the reflection at the inflow.

The exact conditions are non-local. Through $\mu(z)$ they involve convolution integrals along the boundary and in time, which must be discretised or further approximated ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.2). The wave equation poses the same problem. Efficient evaluation of the convolutions depends on the scale invariance of the boundary, which planar, spherical, cylindrical and conical boundaries possess ([Hagstrom](#hagstrom-1999) 1999). For the wave equation, the review lists non-local conditions from the Kirchhoff integral, which suit any smooth boundary, conditions in spherical harmonics on spheres, maps from Dirichlet to Neumann data for time-harmonic problems, and the sharp rear front of waves in odd numbers of dimensions, which bounds the memory in time. For the linearised Euler equations, fewer such techniques exist. For steady flows, the method of difference potentials uses non-local boundary operators with good results ([Tsynkov](#tsynkov-1998) 1998). For unsteady flows, the convolution kernel can be approximated by a sum of exponentials, which turns the convolution into recurrences that are local in time. This was done for the linearised equations of magnetohydrodynamics at a planar boundary ([Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2001) 2001).

#### 2.2.2 Local approximations

Polynomial or rational approximations of $\mu(z)$ in eq. 5 turn the exact conditions into local differential equations at each point of the boundary ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.3). Hierarchies of such conditions, of increasing order of accuracy, were first developed for the wave equation ([Lindman](#lindman-1975) 1975, [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) 1977, [1979](#engquist-majda-1979), [Bayliss and Turkel](#bayliss-turkel-1980) 1980). Locality keeps an advantage even where efficient non-local methods now exist: a local condition can follow a non-uniform base flow by taking the density, pressure and velocity of the base flow at each point of the boundary (2.2.3). A Taylor series of $\mu(z)$ about $z = 0$ expands about normal incidence, the only incidence in one dimension. Its crudest form, $z = 0$, gives the one-dimensional conditions $(v', u' + p')^\mathrm{T} = 0$ at the inflow and $u' - p' = 0$ at the outflow (eq. 8). These are the linearised quantities that are constant along characteristics, whose nonlinear counterparts are the characteristic conditions of 2.3.

[Trefethen and Halpern](#trefethen-halpern-1986) (1986) proved that rational approximations $\mu \approx p_m(z)/q_n(z)$ of the Padé, Chebyshev and least-squares kinds give well-posed conditions provided that $m = n$ or $m = n + 2$, so that Taylor series beyond the second order are not admissible. [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, [1990](#giles-1990)) derived local conditions for the Euler equations from Taylor expansions of low order. They are strongly well posed, cause no reflection between the vorticity wave and the acoustic waves, $\rho_{a^- \to \Omega} = \rho_{\Omega \to a^-} = 0$, and reflect acoustic waves with $\rho_{a^- \to a^+} = O(z^4)$ and $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-} = O(z^2)$. [Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) (2000) obtained strongly well-posed conditions of any order under the same conditions on the rational approximation. The Padé approximant of degrees $(m, n)$ again causes no reflection between vorticity and sound, while $\rho_{a^- \to a^+} = O(z^{m+n+4})$ and $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-} = O(z^{m+n+2})$. The degrees $m = n = 0$ recover the conditions of Giles, and [Hagstrom and Goodrich](#hagstrom-goodrich-2002) (2002) derived similar conditions. The conditions of high order contain mixed derivatives in $y$ and $t$, which are discretised as systems of first-order equations with auxiliary variables.

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, fig. 2) compares the reflection coefficients of local conditions for $u_o = 0.5$, against the angle between the $x$-axis and the acoustic wave that causes the reflection, or the upstream acoustic wave that the vorticity wave generates. The Padé approximants of higher order are better at every angle. The approximant of degrees $(4, 4)$ reflects less than 1 per cent of acoustic waves that meet the outflow at angles below $100^\circ$ and the inflow at angles below $45^\circ$, whereas the one-dimensional conditions keep reflections to that level only below $20^\circ$ and $5^\circ$. Every well-posed local condition reflects completely the waves whose group velocity is tangent to the boundary, where $\mu = 0$. With periodic boundaries or walls across the non-reflecting direction, this degrades long runs, since after a while only such tangent waves remain.

In domains that are not periodic, the edges and corners of a rectangular domain pose a more serious problem, since compatibility conditions must blend the conditions of the boundaries that meet. Stable treatments of corners are known only for the conditions of lowest order, although compatibility conditions exist for the wave equation. One treatment writes the conditions as closures for the derivatives normal to each boundary, using the full equations, and imposes the conditions of both boundaries at a corner. No proof of its stability exists, but experiments suggest that it is stable for the Padé approximants of degrees $(0, 0)$, $(2, 0)$ and $(2, 2)$ ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.3, §5.1). Smooth boundaries, such as circles and spheres, would avoid corners, but the normal velocity of the base flow then changes sign along the boundary, and with it the labelling of the waves. Corners and boundaries with both inflow and outflow remain open problems.

Asymptotic expansions of the solution at large distance from the source give another kind of local condition ([Bayliss and Turkel](#bayliss-turkel-1982) 1982). Their first term is the Sommerfeld radiation condition, applied at a finite distance. For the linearised Euler equations they give conditions for outflow and for the characteristic boundary, $u_o = 0$, and [Tam and Webb](#tam-webb-1993) (1993) extended the first term to inflow boundaries. The expansion converges slowly unless the source lies at the centre of the boundary, so that a single term is inaccurate in general.

#### 2.2.3 Non-uniform base flows

A local condition can follow a non-uniform base flow through its local density, pressure and velocity, provided that the base flow varies over lengths long compared with the disturbances ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.4). At the inflow and outflow of turbulent shear flows this approximation of high frequency fails: the largest vortices are as large as the variations of the mean flow, and sound radiates most strongly at long wavelengths. Exact or high-order conditions then gain little, and computations of mixing layers and jets have used conditions of low order, namely one-dimensional ones, those of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990) and the first term of the asymptotic expansion. Several of them add an absorbing buffer near the outflow (2.5). In arbitrary gradients of the mean flow, acoustic, vortical and entropic waves lose their separate identities. Exact non-reflecting conditions exist for a quiescent base state stratified normal to the boundary ([Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2001) 2001). Conditions for parallel sheared flows may be tractable through a third-order wave equation for the pressure, which also governs the linear instability of such flows. For turbulent flows, the nonlinear effects of 2.3 may matter as much, especially at the outflow.

#### 2.2.4 The hierarchy of Engquist and Majda

[Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977) derive perfectly absorbing boundary conditions from the theory of the reflection of singularities, and show that they are necessarily non-local in space and time: advancing one point by one step needs all earlier times along the whole boundary (introduction, §1.B). They therefore build a hierarchy of local conditions that approximate them, under two requirements: locality, for the cost, and a well-posed mixed problem, without which no stable difference approximation exists. For the wave equation $w_{tt} = w_{xx} + w_{yy}$ in $x > 0$, waves leaving through $x = 0$ satisfy $\partial_xw = (\partial_t^2 - \partial_y^2)^{1/2}w$, a pseudo-differential condition whose symbol $d/dx - i\xi(1 - \omega^2/\xi^2)^{1/2}$, with $\xi$ and $\omega$ dual to $t$ and $y$, is expanded about normal incidence, $\omega = 0$ (eqs. 1.6 and 1.8). The first approximation is $w_x - w_t = 0$, exact in one dimension, the second, from the first Taylor or Padé term, $w_{xt} - w_{tt} + \frac{1}{2}w_{yy} = 0$, and the third, from the second Padé approximant, $w_{xtt} - w_{ttt} - \frac{1}{4}w_{xyy} + \frac{3}{4}w_{tyy} = 0$ (eqs. 1 to 3, 1.9, 1.10, 1.13). With $\omega/\xi = \sin\theta$ for waves incident at the angle $\theta$, they reflect amplitudes of $|(\cos\theta - 1)/(\cos\theta + 1)|$ to the first, second and third power: 17, 3 and 0.5 per cent at $45^\circ$. All absorb perfectly at normal incidence. All reflect completely near glancing incidence, which is inherent in the problem but harmless, since the reflected waves move only slowly away from the boundary.

The first condition is maximally dissipative and so well posed. The second and third are well posed by the normal-mode analysis of [Kreiss](#kreiss-1970) (1970), which excludes solutions $\exp[-(s^2 + \omega^2)^{1/2}x + st + i\omega y]$ with $\mathrm{Re}\,s > 0$ that satisfy the condition (eqs. 1.11, 1.12 and 1.14). The condition from the second Taylor approximation, by contrast, has such a solution and is strongly ill posed, which shows that the local approximation must be chosen with care (eqs. 1.15 to 1.17). Where the data reach the boundary, the condition must also be made compatible with them, through an inhomogeneous right-hand side, or it introduces a discontinuity. For variable coefficients and curved boundaries, pseudo-differential operators factor the wave operator into incoming and outgoing parts, whose expansion gives local conditions of the same kind (§1.C). On a circle of radius $a$, the first is $(\partial_r + \partial_t + 1/(2a))w = 0$, which annihilates outgoing cylindrical waves $f(r - t)/r^{1/2}$ (eq. 1.27), and the second also compensates for their dependence on angle (eq. 1.28).

For strictly hyperbolic symmetric systems $\mathbf{u}_t = A_1\mathbf{u}_x + A_2\mathbf{u}_y + B\mathbf{u}$, the first approximation projects onto the characteristics that enter the domain in the one-dimensional system, and is maximally dissipative and always well posed (§2.B). The second reflects to $O(|\omega/\xi|^2 + 1/|\xi|^2)$, and the authors conjecture that it is always well posed, which [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988) later disproved for the Euler equations (2.2.6). Under conditions that the inflow of the linearised shallow-water equations meets, and which Engquist and Majda expect to hold often in fluid dynamics, a special second approximation reaches the reflection of the third with a condition of first order only, much easier to discretise stably (§2.C, §3). They doubt that approximations beyond the second are well posed in general.

The experiments of [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977, §4) confirm the gains. On an explicit scheme of second order for the wave equation, with $\Delta x = \Delta y = 2.5\Delta t$, an expanding circular wave meets the boundary $x = 0$ within 100 steps. Dirichlet and Neumann conditions reflect it completely, the first approximation reflects about 5 per cent of its amplitude in the $L_2$ norm, and the second less than 1 per cent. For the linearised shallow-water equations at a subsonic inflow, with velocities $a = -1$ and $b = 2$ and a wave speed $c = 12$, on a mesh of $30 \times 50$ points with a Lax–Wendroff scheme, the norms of the reflected velocities and height after 30 steps lie between 0.62 and 0.70 for a reflecting wall, between 0.05 and 0.10 for the first approximation and between 0.013 and 0.024 for the second. The second approximation thus improves on the first by a factor of about 3.5.

#### 2.2.5 The unified theory of Giles

[Giles](#giles-1988) (1988) sets out non-reflecting conditions for the Euler equations in a report for researchers in computational fluid dynamics who wish to implement them and to understand their theory (§1.3). The motivation is the flow through turbomachinery, whose far-field boundaries lie less than a chord from the blades (§1.1). The steady far field there holds several spatial wavenumbers, and where the flow is supersonic along its direction but subsonic along the axis, shocks propagate indefinitely and are reflected by improper conditions. Unsteadiness, moreover, enters as shocks and wakes from upstream rows of blades, which rules out the coarse far-field grids that dissipate outgoing waves around isolated airfoils. The report therefore seeks steady conditions that do not reflect steady waves such as shocks, and accurate conditions for unsteady waves. Turbomachinery also simplifies the problem: the inflow and the outflow are separate boundaries of trivial geometry, normal to the axial coordinate $x$, and the flow is periodic in the circumferential coordinate $y$, which permits a Fourier decomposition about a uniform flow. Four kinds of conditions are derived: quasi-one-dimensional ones, exact ones for a single frequency, exact steady ones and approximate unsteady ones (§1.3). Exact refers to the linear problem, whose own errors are of second order in the amplitude of the disturbances at the boundaries. Giles counts the theory a condensed restatement of earlier work, and the analysis of well-posedness its one original contribution, since the Euler equations have a multiple root with distinct eigenvectors and, at one complex frequency, a coalescence of eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The journal paper of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990) summarises this report and a second one on the implementation in a code for turbomachinery, and omits the analysis of well-posedness.

By the history of the report (§1.2), the firm foundation of the field was the analysis by [Kreiss](#kreiss-1970) (1970) of the well-posedness of initial-boundary-value problems for hyperbolic systems in several dimensions, which [Higdon](#higdon-1986) (1986) reviewed with an interpretation in terms of waves. For one-dimensional flow, characteristic theory gave the correct non-reflecting conditions early: [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), as Giles restates him, derived their linear and nonlinear forms for the Euler equations with the approach through eigenvectors that the report follows. Applied in two and three dimensions by an analysis normal to the boundary that ignores all tangential derivatives, they are the quasi-one-dimensional, or normal one-dimensional, conditions, also called the method of characteristics, and remain the most common conditions in unsteady computations. [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977) derived a hierarchy of approximate conditions in several dimensions, whose first member is the one-dimensional condition. Written for mathematicians, their paper had been little taken up in computational fluid dynamics, and its conditions of higher order had been implemented only for acoustic and elastic waves and for the scalar unsteady potential equation. For linearised harmonic flows, exact conditions can be built. For steady flow past isolated airfoils, a vortex correction for the lift, and then source and doublet terms for the drag, brought the far-field boundary in from many chords to a few. The same followed from the limit of zero frequency of the ideal non-reflecting conditions, the view that unites the steady, single-frequency and unsteady conditions of the report.

The general analysis treats $\mathbf{U}_t + A\mathbf{U}_x + B\mathbf{U}_y = 0$ for a vector $\mathbf{U}$ of $N$ components and constant matrices $A$ and $B$ ([Giles](#giles-1988) 1988, §2). Waves $\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u}e^{i(kx + ly - \omega t)}$ obey the dispersion relation $\det(-\omega I + kA + lB) = 0$, a polynomial of degree $N$ in $k$ and in $l$ (§2.1). For a growing complex frequency, $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega > 0$, the waves that travel to the right have $\mathrm{Im}\,k > 0$, and for real $\omega$ and $k$ a wave travels in the direction of its group velocity $(\partial\omega/\partial k, \partial\omega/\partial l)$. At the boundary, a solution is a sum of Fourier modes in $y$ and $t$, each a group of waves with common $\omega$ and $l$ and different $k$. Their amplitudes are measured not by the usual left eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{L}$ of $kA + lB$, which are orthogonal across different $\omega$, but by the left eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}$ of $A^{-1}(\omega I - lB)$, which are orthogonal to the right eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{R}$ of the other waves with the same $\omega$ and $l$ (§2.2). The ideal condition sets $\mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}_n\mathbf{U} = 0$ for every incoming wave $n$ (§2.3). It needs a Fourier transform in $y$ and a Laplace transform in $t$, which is difficult and expensive. Since $k/\omega$ and all eigenvectors depend only on $\lambda = l/\omega$, simpler conditions follow from assumptions on $\lambda$:

- The one-dimensional conditions take $\lambda = 0$ (§2.4). With the right and left eigenvectors $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{R}_n$ and $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_n$ of $A$, normalised so that $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_m\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{R}_n = \delta_{mn}$, the characteristic variables $c_n = \mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_n\mathbf{U}$ give $\mathbf{U} = \sum_n c_n\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{R}_n$, and the conditions set $c_n = 0$ for the incoming waves, those whose eigenvalue of $A$ points into the domain. Codes usually also extrapolate the outgoing characteristic variables from the interior, which completes the state on the boundary.
- The exact conditions for a single frequency apply the ideal condition to each Fourier mode along a boundary on which the solution is periodic in $y$, as an algebraic equation in the Fourier domain (§2.5). Their implementation depends on the interior method, and a principal difficulty is to decide which waves enter.
- The exact steady conditions are their limit as $\omega \to 0$ (§2.6). For the mean of the solution along the boundary, the mode $l = 0$, they reduce to the one-dimensional conditions, whose right-hand sides the user may set to prescribe the mean incoming characteristics, such as the mean inlet flow angle, stagnation enthalpy and entropy (2.4.5).
- The approximate unsteady conditions expand $\mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}_n$ in a Taylor series in $\lambda$ (§2.7). The first term gives the one-dimensional conditions. The second, after multiplying by $\omega$ and replacing $\omega$ and $l$ by derivatives in $t$ and $y$, gives a local condition with first derivatives in $t$ and $y$, which needs no Fourier decomposition and which the interior method can probably also integrate.

The one-dimensional conditions are always well posed (§2.8). After a transformation that makes $A$ and $B$ symmetric, the energy, the integral of $|\mathbf{u}|^2$ over the domain, changes only through a boundary integral of $\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{T}A\mathbf{u} = \sum_n \lambda_n c_n^2$, with the eigenvalues $\lambda_n$ of $A$. Once the conditions remove the incoming terms, the remaining ones all carry energy out, so that the energy cannot grow. For the approximate conditions, the theory of Kreiss requires instead that no incoming mode satisfy the conditions: the matrix $C_{mn} = \bar{\mathbf{v}}^\mathrm{L}_m\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{R}_n$ of the approximate left and exact right eigenvectors of the incoming waves must be non-singular for all real $l$ and all complex $\omega$ with $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega > 0$. Where right eigenvectors coalesce at a critical frequency, Giles extends the condition: the determinant may vanish there, but no faster than the coalescence permits, and he conjectures a general rule for several coalescing eigenvectors. [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977) had conjectured that the approximation of second order is always well posed, which Giles disproves for the Euler equations (§3.7). In his analysis, a general solution of given $\omega$ and $l$ splits into incoming and outgoing modes, and the approximate conditions give the incoming amplitudes as $-C^{-1}D$ times the outgoing ones, with a matrix $D$ of the approximate left eigenvectors and the right eigenvectors of the outgoing waves (§2.9). Since $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_m - \mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}_m = O(l/\omega)$, the one-dimensional conditions reflect waves to $O(l/\omega)$, and the approximate conditions of second order to $O((l/\omega)^2)$.

#### 2.2.6 The conditions of Giles for the Euler equations

For the Euler equations, the perturbations $\mathbf{U} = (\delta\rho, \delta u, \delta v, \delta p)^\mathrm{T}$ of a uniform flow obey the linear system of 2.2.5, with $A$ and $B$ built from that flow ([Giles](#giles-1988) 1988, §3.1). Scaled by its density and speed of sound, $A$ has the rows $(u, 1, 0, 0)$, $(0, u, 0, 1)$, $(0, 0, u, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0, u)$, and $B$ the rows $(v, 0, 1, 0)$, $(0, v, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, v, 1)$ and $(0, 0, 1, v)$, where $u$ and $v$ are now the Mach numbers along $x$ and $y$ (eq. 63). Linearising introduces errors. In steady flow they are proportional to the square of the steady perturbation at the boundaries, and small except for an oblique shock at the outflow. In unsteady flow they are proportional to the square of the unsteady perturbation, and to its product with the non-uniformity of the steady flow, since $A$ and $B$ must be built from a mean of the steady state. The last is negligible at the inflow, but may dominate at the outflow, behind wakes and shock losses. All these errors are of second order, much smaller than the first-order gains of the conditions.

The dispersion relation $(uk + vl - \omega)^2[(uk + vl - \omega)^2 - k^2 - l^2] = 0$ has the double root $k_{1,2} = (\omega - vl)/u$ of the entropy and vorticity waves, and the acoustic roots $k_{3,4} = (\omega - vl)(-u \pm S)/(1 - u^2)$, with $S = [1 - (1 - u^2)l^2/(\omega - vl)^2]^{1/2}$ (§3.2, eqs. 64 to 70). In the domain $0 \le x \le 1$ with $0 < u < 1$, the inflow at $x = 0$ has three incoming waves, the entropy, vorticity and downstream acoustic waves, and the outflow at $x = 1$ one, the upstream acoustic wave (§3.4). The branch of $S$ must be chosen with care. For real $\omega$ and real positive $S$ the third wave runs to the right and the fourth to the left, and for complex $\omega$ or $S$ the third root is the one with positive imaginary part, so that it remains the right-running wave (§3.2). The report assumes the flow axially subsonic, as in almost all turbomachines. The eigenvectors identify the waves (§3.3). The entropy wave changes the density alone, at constant pressure and without vorticity, the vorticity wave carries vorticity at uniform entropy and pressure, and the acoustic waves are isentropic and irrotational. The eigenvectors of the double root are not unique, and Giles chooses those that separate entropy from vorticity, as in fluid dynamics, which simplifies the later algebra.

In dimensional variables, with the speed of sound $c$, the one-dimensional characteristic variables are $c_1 = -c^2\delta\rho + \delta p$ for the entropy wave, $c_2 = \rho c\,\delta v$ for the vorticity wave, $c_3 = \rho c\,\delta u + \delta p$ for the downstream and $c_4 = -\rho c\,\delta u + \delta p$ for the upstream acoustic wave (§3.8, eq. 162). The one-dimensional conditions set $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = 0$ at the inflow and $c_4 = 0$ at the outflow (eqs. 164 and 165). The outgoing characteristics are computed or extrapolated from the interior, and the state on the boundary follows from all four (§3.4).

The approximate conditions of second order are exact for the entropy and vorticity waves at the inflow, since their only approximation, $S \approx 1$, enters the acoustic waves alone (§3.7.1). At the outflow they read $\partial c_4/\partial t + u\,\partial c_2/\partial y + v\,\partial c_4/\partial y = 0$ (eq. 174), a local equation for the incoming characteristic that can be integrated in time like the interior equations. Before use, they must be checked for well-posedness, without which any implementation diverges on a fine enough grid (§3.7.2). In a frame that moves with the tangential velocity $v$, which leaves well-posedness unchanged, the inflow conditions of second order admit an incoming mode at the complex frequency $\omega = iu|l|$, where two eigenvectors coalesce, and are ill posed. The outflow condition is well posed. Giles cures the inflow by relaxing its third condition (§3.7.3). Since the first two already remove the entropy and vorticity waves, its left eigenvector need not be orthogonal to theirs, and a correction proportional to $\lambda$ is chosen that makes the reflection of fourth order and the problem well posed. In dimensional variables this modified inflow condition reads $\partial(c_1, c_2, c_3)^\mathrm{T}/\partial t + M_\mathrm{in}\,\partial(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4)^\mathrm{T}/\partial y = 0$, where $M_\mathrm{in}$ has the rows $(v, 0, 0, 0)$, $(0, v, (c + u)/2, (c - u)/2)$ and $(0, (c - u)/2, v, 0)$ (eq. 173). A modified outflow condition, $\partial c_4/\partial t + \frac{1}{2}(c + u)\,\partial c_2/\partial y + v\,\partial c_4/\partial y = 0$, is well posed as well (eq. 175).

The reflection coefficients show the trade-offs (§3.7.4, eqs. 159 to 161). At the modified inflow, the outgoing pressure wave reflects no entropy or vorticity, and the pressure wave it reflects has an amplitude of $O((l/\omega)^4)$. At the outflow of second order, outgoing entropy and vorticity cause no reflection, and the pressure wave a reflection of second order, with the coefficient $[(1 + u)/(1 - u)][S - 1 + u(1 - u)\lambda^2]/[S + 1 - u(1 - u)\lambda^2]$ in the moving frame. At the cut-off frequency, where $\lambda^2 = 1/(1 - u^2)$ and the group velocity has no component along $x$, the coefficients are $-(1 - u)/(1 + u)$ at the inflow and $-(1 + u)/(1 - u)$ at the outflow. Their product is 1, as it must be, since two pressure waves without group velocity along $x$ cannot be told apart. The modified outflow reflects the pressure wave only to fourth order, but outgoing vorticity to first order. It therefore suits only boundaries without outgoing vorticity, such as the far field of an oscillating transonic airfoil away from its wake, since only a shock and the unsteady Kutta condition create vorticity there (§3.7.4).

### 2.3 Characteristic boundary conditions

#### 2.3.1 The form of Thompson

The one-dimensional characteristic conditions are exact only in one dimension, but serve as an approximation in several ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987, [1990](#thompson-1990)), as Colonius restates him, writes the conservation laws at a boundary normal to $x$ as $\mathbf{q}_t + P(\mathbf{u})S(\mathbf{u})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})_y = 0$ (eq. 12). Here $\mathbf{q}$ are the conserved variables, $\mathbf{g}$ their fluxes along the boundary, $\mathbf{u} = (\rho, u, v, p)^\mathrm{T}$ the primitive variables, $P = \partial\mathbf{q}/\partial\mathbf{u}$ the Jacobian between the two sets, and $S$ the matrix of the right eigenvectors of the Jacobian in $x$. The vector $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ has the components $\mathcal{L}_1 = \lambda_1v_x$, $\mathcal{L}_2 = \lambda_1(p_x - a^2\rho_x)$, $\mathcal{L}_3 = \lambda_2[u_x + (\rho a)^{-1}p_x]$ and $\mathcal{L}_4 = \lambda_3[u_x - (\rho a)^{-1}p_x]$, with $\lambda_1 = u$, $\lambda_2 = u + a$ and $\lambda_3 = u - a$ (eq. 13). Each is the slope of a characteristic times the derivative in $x$ of its Riemann invariant: those of the shear wave, the entropy wave and the two acoustic waves. Where a characteristic leaves the domain, its component is computed from the interior with one-sided differences. Where it enters, its component is prescribed.

Linearised, the conditions of Thompson differ from eq. 8, since eq. 12 keeps the derivatives along the boundary. Fig. 2 shows their reflection coefficients for $u_o = a_o/2$. At a subsonic outflow they reflect outgoing sound, $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-}$, less than the one-dimensional conditions. Plane acoustic waves that travel along the boundary, which differ from the tangent waves of 2.2.2 unless $u_o = 0$, are not reflected at the outflow at all, since all derivatives in $x$ in eq. 13 then vanish. Outgoing vorticity waves, however, generate more sound than under the one-dimensional conditions, and the acoustic reflections at the inflow exceed theirs. Except where $u_o = 0$, the linearised conditions are not strongly well posed. Their reflection coefficient at the inflow becomes infinite for plane acoustic waves that travel along the boundary. Since $s$ is then purely imaginary, the ill-posedness is weak, and without other dissipation the computation is marginally stable. The same property helps at the corners of a domain that is not periodic, since a plane wave along one boundary is absorbed completely by the boundary across its path. The conditions at a corner are compatible: they are imposed on $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ and on the corresponding transformation of the fluxes in $y$ at once. This is robust in tests and in practice, although with large errors near the corner (Thompson 1990, [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) 1992, as cited by Colonius).

#### 2.3.2 Nonlinear outflow

At a subsonic outflow, the inviscid equations take one condition, on the sole incoming characteristic $\mathcal{L}_4$ ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). The other variables follow from the analysis of [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987), as Colonius restates it, or, equivalently, from the locally one-dimensional inviscid (LODI) relations of [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992). If the flow at the boundary could be linearised about a uniform state, which it seldom can at an outflow, a non-reflecting condition of 2.2 would set the fluctuation $\mathcal{L}_4'$, and the linearised conditions of Thompson take $\mathcal{L}_4' = 0$, with the reflections that Colonius shows in fig. 2. Usually the fluctuations are not small, and the mean flow is not known. A steady Reynolds-averaged solution has been proposed for it ([Morris et al.](#morris-et-al-1997) 1997), but nonlinearity still prevents accurate conditions.

A vortex without circulation that a uniform flow carries through the outflow boundary shows the size of the nonlinear errors ([Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) 1993). For small viscosity, the vortex should leave unaltered at constant speed. [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, fig. 4) compares two linearised outflow conditions for it ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002): that of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990), under which outgoing vorticity produces no incoming sound in the linear limit, and a modified condition of Giles, under which it produces incoming sound in proportion to the amplitude of the vortex. Once the velocity that the vortex induces is a sizeable fraction of the speed of the flow, the nonlinear reflections of the first condition are as large as the linear reflections of the second. Shear at the boundary would likely make this worse. A vortex strong enough can even reverse the sign of $\lambda_1$ for a time, turning the outflow into an inflow, and the linearised treatment then fails. Prescribing the incoming wave is thus a matter of modelling, which analysis alone cannot settle, and buffer techniques (2.5) are at present the most accurate at outflow boundaries.

#### 2.3.3 Extensions

By [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §4), the characteristic conditions of [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987) and [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992) have proved robust, and are therefore widely used and extended. [Thompson](#thompson-1990) (1990), as Colonius restates him, applies the same framework to walls, both inviscid and without slip, to boundaries at constant pressure, and to a force-free boundary. The last sets to zero all forces on the fluid normal to the boundary and lets the fluid be carried at its own velocity, much like the convective condition of incompressible flow. Poinsot and Lele (1992) generalise their LODI relations alike. [Okong'o and Bellan](#okongo-bellan-2002) (2002) extend the analysis to mixtures of real gases, and [Kim and Lee](#kim-lee-2000) (2000) to curvilinear coordinates.

#### 2.3.4 The characteristic conditions of Poinsot and Lele

[Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992) build characteristic conditions for the Navier–Stokes equations (NSCBC) that reduce to conditions for the Euler equations as the viscosity vanishes, avoid all extrapolation, and use the number of physical conditions that theory requires (§1). At a boundary normal to $x_1$, the conservation equations of mass, energy and momentum are recast so that the derivatives normal to the boundary enter only through a vector $\mathbf{d}$, while derivatives along it and viscous terms keep their usual form (§2.2, eqs. 9 to 14). With $d_1 = [\mathcal{L}_2 + \frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1)]/c^2$, $d_2 = \frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1)$, $d_3 = (\mathcal{L}_5 - \mathcal{L}_1)/(2\rho c)$, $d_4 = \mathcal{L}_3$ and $d_5 = \mathcal{L}_4$, the amplitude variations are $\mathcal{L}_1 = \lambda_1(\partial p/\partial x_1 - \rho c\,\partial u_1/\partial x_1)$, $\mathcal{L}_2 = \lambda_2(c^2\partial\rho/\partial x_1 - \partial p/\partial x_1)$, $\mathcal{L}_3 = \lambda_3\partial u_2/\partial x_1$, $\mathcal{L}_4 = \lambda_4\partial u_3/\partial x_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_5 = \lambda_5(\partial p/\partial x_1 + \rho c\,\partial u_1/\partial x_1)$, for the speeds $\lambda_1 = u_1 - c$, $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = \lambda_4 = u_1$ and $\lambda_5 = u_1 + c$ (eqs. 15 to 23). For one-dimensional acoustic waves, $-\mathcal{L}_1$ is the rate of change at a point of the amplitude $p' - \rho cu'$ of the upstream wave, which is carried unchanged along its characteristic, so that the $\mathcal{L}_i$ are amplitude variations of the waves that cross the boundary. The outgoing ones are computed from the interior with one-sided differences, of third order in their code, and the derivatives along the boundary as in the interior. **[GK]** Their $\mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_5$ correspond, up to factors, to the acoustic amplitudes $\mathcal{L}_4$ and $\mathcal{L}_3$ of Thompson in 2.3.1, whose numbering 2.3.2 and 2.4.1 keep.

Where the exterior is known, for instance from an asymptotic solution, the incoming $\mathcal{L}_i$ can be set from it, as [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987) suggests in the restatement of Poinsot and Lele (§2.2). This prescribes derivatives only, and may let mean values such as the pressure drift. Usually nothing is known, and the incoming amplitudes are estimated from the local one-dimensional inviscid (LODI) relations, which follow from eqs. 9 to 13 when transverse and viscous terms are dropped (§2.3). In primitive variables they read $\partial\rho/\partial t + [\mathcal{L}_2 + \frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1)]/c^2 = 0$, $\partial p/\partial t + \frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1) = 0$, $\partial u_1/\partial t + (\mathcal{L}_5 - \mathcal{L}_1)/(2\rho c) = 0$, $\partial u_2/\partial t + \mathcal{L}_3 = 0$ and $\partial u_3/\partial t + \mathcal{L}_4 = 0$ (eqs. 24 to 28), and similar relations hold for the temperature, the mass flux, the entropy and the stagnation enthalpy (eqs. 29 to 32). Most physical conditions have a counterpart among them: constant entropy needs $\mathcal{L}_2 = 0$, and a constant pressure $\mathcal{L}_5 = -\mathcal{L}_1$. The LODI relations are not physical conditions but compatibility relations between the physical conditions and the amplitudes of the waves. They only estimate the incoming amplitudes, while the boundary is advanced with the full equations, transverse and viscous terms included.

The strategy has three steps (§2.4, Table II). First, the conservation equation that each inviscid physical condition makes redundant is dropped, such as the energy equation where the pressure is imposed. Second, the LODI relation of each condition gives the incoming amplitudes in terms of the outgoing ones. Third, the remaining conservation equations, with these amplitudes, advance the variables that no condition fixes. At a subsonic inflow with imposed velocities and temperature, four waves enter and $\mathcal{L}_1$ leaves (§3.1). The momentum and energy equations are dropped, the LODI relations give $\mathcal{L}_5 = \mathcal{L}_1 - 2\rho c\,du_1/dt$, $\mathcal{L}_3 = -du_2/dt$, $\mathcal{L}_4 = -du_3/dt$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$ from the temperature, and the continuity equation then gives the density. At walls, the reflected wave follows from the outgoing one: $\mathcal{L}_1 = \mathcal{L}_5$ where the normal velocity vanishes (§3.4 to §3.6). Used alone, the LODI relations give simple approximate conditions. Non-reflection at an outlet, $\mathcal{L}_1 = 0$, then becomes $\partial p/\partial t - \rho c\,\partial u_1/\partial t = 0$, the relation that [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), [Bayliss and Turkel](#bayliss-turkel-1980) (1980) and [Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) (1980) used, as Poinsot and Lele cite them (§2.4, footnote), and the first approximation of [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977).

For the Navier–Stokes equations, viscous conditions supplement the inviscid ones, but enter only the third step, as modifications of the conservation equations, so that they vanish smoothly with the viscosity ([Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) 1992, §2.5, Tables III and IV). The choices follow the theory of [Strikwerda](#strikwerda-1977) (1977) and [Oliger and Sundström](#oliger-sundstrom-1978) (1978) with two exceptions. An inflow with imposed velocities and temperature takes four conditions, not five, since the remaining density follows from the continuity equation without viscous terms, and the supersonic outflow differs as well. At a subsonic outflow, the tangential viscous stresses and the normal heat flux are given zero normal derivatives. At edges and corners, the derivatives along a second boundary are replaced likewise by characteristic amplitudes, estimated from a second LODI system (§2.6). Such treatments are necessary with a centred interior scheme, and need compatible choices: at the corner between a wall without slip and an outlet of imposed pressure, the pressure is left to the corner treatment, so that it reaches the outlet pressure only in the steady state. A general rule for the combinations at edges and corners was still lacking.

### 2.4 Relaxation towards the far field at subsonic outflow and inflow

#### 2.4.1 The drift of the mean pressure

For nonlinear computations, [Thompson](#thompson-1990) (1990), as restated by [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §4), sets $\mathcal{L}_4 = 0$ at a subsonic outflow. Nothing then keeps the time-averaged pressure from drifting, the less so when characteristic conditions are imposed at the inflow as well ([Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) 1980, [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) 1992, as cited by Colonius). Physically, the upstream characteristic would carry whatever acoustic reflection is needed to hold the pressure downstream, at least in steady flow. The Dirichlet condition $p = p_\infty$ fixes the pressure, and is still standard in many codes, but it reflects badly, completely for plane waves. Rudy and Strikwerda (1980), as Colonius restates them, therefore modify the one-dimensional characteristic condition to $p_t - \rho au_t + \alpha(p - p_\infty) = 0$, which imposes the pressure in a steady state (eq. 14). They optimised the parameter $\alpha$ to reach the steady state fastest in a linearised one-dimensional problem. Poinsot and Lele (1992) adopt the condition in their characteristic formulation, and [Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) (1993) with the conditions of Giles in sheared flows. No systematic study tells how to set $\alpha$ in general, and the right value probably depends on several factors, among them the discrete stability of 2.6.

#### 2.4.2 Subsonic inflow

At an inflow, a target profile of velocity, and perhaps of temperature or another thermodynamic quantity, is often wanted ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). The traditional approach imposes Dirichlet conditions on the right number of variables, four at a subsonic inflow of inviscid flow in three dimensions, and well-posedness has been proved for several such sets, inviscid and viscous. [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992) combine Dirichlet conditions on the velocity and the temperature with a characteristic analysis for the remaining component of $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ that closes the equation of continuity. All Dirichlet conditions, however, reflect the upstream acoustic wave badly, since they force fluctuations of acoustic variables to zero. Linearised, imposing the one-dimensional characteristics equals the one-dimensional non-reflecting condition and lets plane waves pass upstream, but fixing the velocity and the density perturbations reflects even plane waves completely. An inflow reflects far less where more is known of the incoming flow. If it is laminar with small disturbances, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_o + \boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}'$ can be linearised. Steady, unidirectional, transversely sheared flows at constant pressure are exact inviscid solutions, so that $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_o = 0$ and all incoming fluctuations $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}'$ are prescribed. For viscous flow a solution of the boundary-layer equations serves, whose $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_o$ follows the slow growth of the layer along the flow. The conditions of 2.2 can then set $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}'$, with the caveats stated there. An astrophysical wind that enters subsonically through an over-specified inflow, and a characteristic inflow tested in its place, follow in 2.8.2.

#### 2.4.3 The partially reflecting outflow of Poinsot and Lele

A perfectly non-reflecting boundary need not give a well-posed problem ([Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) 1992, §3.2). In a free shear layer with imposed inflow and perfectly non-reflecting conditions on the other sides, nothing tells the flow its mean pressure: physically, that information comes from waves reflected far away, where a static pressure $p_\infty$ holds, and these never return through perfect conditions. Tests also suggest that perfectly non-reflecting conditions for the Euler equations may be well posed in one dimension but ill posed in most two-dimensional situations. At a subsonic outlet, four waves leave and $\mathcal{L}_1$ enters. Imposing $p = p_\infty$ on the boundary is well posed but reflects acoustic waves, so the NSCBC take as their one physical condition a pressure imposed at infinity, which fixes no variable on the boundary and keeps all conservation equations. The incoming wave then brings the mean pressure back towards $p_\infty$, through $\mathcal{L}_1 = K(p - p_\infty)$ with $K = \sigma(1 - \mathcal{M}^2)c/L$ (eq. 40). Here $\mathcal{M}$ is the largest Mach number in the flow, $L$ a size of the domain and $\sigma$ a constant. The form of $K$ is that of [Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) (1980), who, as Poinsot and Lele cite them, applied a similar correction in the energy equation only. With $\sigma = 0$ the condition is perfectly non-reflecting, the method of [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987), as Poinsot and Lele restate him. Where an exact $\mathcal{L}_1^\mathrm{exact}$ is known, it is added to $K(p - p_\infty)$, so that the second term matches derivatives across the boundary while the first holds the mean, although eq. 40 alone usually suffices. The treatment is exact for one-dimensional waves and well posed in several dimensions, but waves that do not meet the boundary at normal incidence are reflected a little, while numerical oscillations and drift are still prevented. Where reflection is wanted, as to study the coupling of longitudinal acoustic waves with the vortices shed by a shear layer, imposing the outlet pressure gives $\mathcal{L}_1 = -\mathcal{L}_5$ and reflects the waves completely (§3.3).

#### 2.4.4 The reflection of the relaxed outflow by frequency

[Selle, Nicoud and Poinsot](#selle-et-al-2004) (2004) analyse the partially reflecting outflow of 2.4.3, which they call the linear relaxation method (§I). No boundary treatment handles both the mean flow and the acoustic waves perfectly: conditions that control the mean flow reflect acoustic waves, and purely non-reflecting ones let mean values drift. The relaxation $\mathcal{L}_1 = K(P - P_\infty)$ acts as a spring that pulls the pressure at the outlet towards $P_\infty$. A small $K$ controls the mean pressure poorly, and a large one makes the boundary reflect, in phase as well as in magnitude. For a harmonic wave $\mathcal{L}_5 = 2\rho cU_0i\omega e^{-i\omega t}$ that reaches the outlet of a semi-infinite tube, the LODI relations of the velocity and the pressure give a pressure whose transient decays as $e^{-Kt/2}$, and then the reflection coefficient $R_\mathrm{out} = \mathcal{L}_1/\mathcal{L}_5 = -1/[1 - i(2\omega/K)]$ (§III, eqs. 11 to 15). Its magnitude is $[1 + (2\omega/K)^2]^{-1/2}$ and its phase $-\pi - \arctan(2\omega/K)$ (eqs. 16 and 17). The outlet thus acts as a low-pass filter on the reflected waves. Low frequencies are reflected almost completely, with the phase of an outlet at fixed pressure, and high ones leave. The cut-off frequency $f_c = K/(4\pi)$, at which $|R| = 1/\sqrt{2}$ and half of the acoustic energy returns into the domain, separates the two, and above it the delay of the boundary moves the phase from $-\pi$ towards $-3\pi/2$ (eq. 18, Table 1). Since the analysis rests on the one-dimensional inviscid relations, it holds for every characteristic treatment that relaxes the incoming wave in this way (§II).

The compressible code AVBP, run on the Euler equations at second order in space with a Runge–Kutta scheme of third order in time, confirms eq. 15 closely in magnitude and phase (§IV, Table 2, fig. 5). A tube of 0.5 m on 400 cells, with a mean velocity of 10 m/s and a speed of sound of 348 m/s, is forced at 500 Hz through a non-reflecting pulsed inlet. $K$ is varied at the outlet, and $R = \mathcal{L}_1/\mathcal{L}_5$ is measured there with one-sided differences. In a duct of length $L$ with an imposed inlet velocity, which reflects with $R_\mathrm{in} = 1$, the eigenfrequencies $\omega$ solve $e^{2i\omega L/c} + 1 - 2i\omega/K = 0$, whose complex roots give the frequency and the damping of each mode (§V, eqs. 19 to 21). For very large $K$ they tend to the undamped modes $f_n = (2n + 1)c/(4L)$ of a duct with fixed inlet velocity and outlet pressure, the quarter wave and its odd harmonics, since $\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1$ then vanishes (eq. 22, figs. 7 and 8). For smaller $K$ all modes are damped, but their frequencies fall through the phase shift of eq. 17, and at very small $K$ the quarter-wave mode disappears. A relaxed outlet thus shifts the resonant frequencies of a domain as well as damping its modes, which biases the acoustics of a computation, the concern of the paper for combustion instabilities (§I).

[Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) (1980), as Selle et al. restate them, scaled the coefficient as in 2.4.3, $K = \sigma(1 - M^2)c/L$, and their theory gave an optimum $\sigma = 0.27$ for the convergence of steady computations (§VI, eq. 23). The cut-off gives $K$ a new meaning: it should lie below every acoustic frequency expected in the domain. In the duct, the lowest is that of the quarter-wave mode, $f_0 = (1 - M^2)c/(4L)$, so that $K$ may be at most $\pi(1 - M^2)c/L$, that is $\sigma \le \pi$ (eqs. 24 to 26). The lower bound comes from the drift of the mean values, not from acoustics, and depends on the Reynolds number, on swirl and on the geometry: values below 0.1 often lengthen the convergence and sometimes keep the mass fluxes and the pressure from settling (fig. 9). Simulations of the duct, started from a Gaussian perturbation of the pressure, confirm the theory (§VII, figs. 10 to 13). With $\sigma = 10\pi$ the high frequencies leave within 0.1 s, but the first three modes stay, at the shifted frequencies of eq. 21. With $\sigma = \pi$ all modes have gone by 0.1 s, although their frequencies are shifted as well. The authors recommend $0.2 < \sigma < \pi$ for such a duct, and, in more complex domains, a cut-off below the lowest eigenfrequency of the acoustic equation solved for the domain (§VIII). They also cite an optimum of 0.58 from numerical simulations that judge the evacuation of acoustic waves as well (§VI), among them those of [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992), who themselves found their optimum near the analytical value and used 0.25 (2.4.6).

#### 2.4.5 Relaxed steady and single-frequency conditions

[Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, §3.5, §3.6) imposes the exact conditions of each Fourier mode along the boundary in a time-marching code by relaxing the incoming characteristics of the mode towards their exact values at a rate $\alpha$. The lag keeps the evolution well posed, as conditions that set the incoming one-dimensional characteristics are. For the steady conditions, the Fourier coefficients $\hat{c}_n$ of the characteristics of a mode with wavenumber $l \neq 0$ obey $\partial\hat{c}_1/\partial t = -\alpha\hat{c}_1$, $\partial\hat{c}_2/\partial t = \alpha\{-[(c\beta + v)/(c + u)]\hat{c}_4 - \hat{c}_2\}$ and $\partial\hat{c}_3/\partial t = \alpha\{[(c\beta + v)/(c + u)]^2\hat{c}_4 - \hat{c}_3\}$ at the inflow, and $\partial\hat{c}_4/\partial t = \alpha\{[2u/(c\beta - v)]\hat{c}_2 - [(c\beta + v)/(c\beta - v)]\hat{c}_3 - \hat{c}_4\}$ at the outflow, with $\beta = i\,\mathrm{sign}(l)(1 - M^2)^{1/2}$ for a Mach number $M < 1$ and $\beta = -\mathrm{sign}(v)(M^2 - 1)^{1/2}$ for $M > 1$ (eqs. 170 to 172). Numerical experience suggests $\alpha = 1/P$ in the non-dimensional variables, with the pitch $P$ of the periodic boundary (§3.6). For the mean mode, $l = 0$, the user prescribes the changes of the incoming one-dimensional characteristics needed to reach chosen mean conditions: the mean entropy, flow angle and stagnation enthalpy at the inflow, and the mean exit pressure at the outflow. A separate report gives the details. It also removes the second-order non-uniformities of entropy and stagnation enthalpy across the inflow that the linear theory leaves, by modifying one inflow condition and replacing another by the constraint of uniform stagnation enthalpy. The single-frequency conditions take the same form, with $\lambda = cl/\omega$ and $S = [1 - (c^2 - u^2)\lambda^2/(c - v\lambda)^2]^{1/2}$ in place of $\beta$ (eqs. 166 to 169). Giles had not yet implemented them, and the right $\alpha$ was unknown: too large a value may make the problem ill posed and the scheme unstable, and too small a value slows the convergence (§3.5).

#### 2.4.6 Tests of outlets

[Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992, §4 to §6, Table V) test outlets in a code without numerical dissipation, of sixth order in space and third order in time, with characteristic inlets and walls throughout. Four outlets are compared. The reference, B1, is the method of [Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) (1980), as Poinsot and Lele describe it: velocities and density are extrapolated at zeroth order, and the pressure obeys $\partial p/\partial t - \rho c\,\partial u_1/\partial t + K(p - p_\infty) = 0$ (eq. 42). Its $\sigma = 0.58$ worked best in the tests of Rudy and Strikwerda, although their analysis of a linearised one-dimensional system gave an optimum near 0.27. Variants of it are common, extrapolating density and velocity with a relaxed or fixed pressure, or extrapolating the tangential velocity and the entropy and taking the normal velocity and the pressure from Riemann invariants. B2 is the perfectly non-reflecting characteristic outlet, $\sigma = 0$, B3 the partially reflecting one, and B4 a reflecting outlet at constant pressure.

A steady laminar shear layer in a duct between slip walls, with velocities of 0.8 and 0.9 times the speed of sound on its two sides, on a coarse grid of $41 \times 41$ points, shows the steady states (§4, figs. 3 to 6). Under B1, the flow rates through inlet and outlet oscillate after a reduced time of about 30 and never settle. Rudy and Strikwerda had not met this with the scheme of MacCormack, whose artificial dissipation damps the errors of extrapolation, which a scheme without dissipation never damps. Under B2 the waves leave quickly, but the mean pressure in the domain falls linearly, and with it the flow rates, so that no steady state is reached: with perfectly non-reflecting conditions the problem appears ill posed. Under B3 with $\sigma = 0.25$, the waves leave, the mean pressure settles and the flow rates become equal after a reduced time of about 25. The result depends little on $\sigma$. A value of 0.1 drifts like $\sigma = 0$, 0.25 and 0.4 give nearly the same good results, and values above about 0.7 cause large oscillations. The optimum lies close to the analytical optimum of Rudy and Strikwerda, and $\sigma = 0.25$ served all their tests, although other configurations or methods might need another value. Under B4 the first longitudinal acoustic mode of the duct cannot leave, and only viscosity damps it, so that no steady state is reached either. Whether a steady state exists at all thus depends strongly on the outlet.

A vortex without net circulation, carried through the outlet by a uniform flow at 1.1 times the speed of sound, tests an unsteady flow (§5, figs. 8 to 11). Under B1, the vorticity becomes discontinuous at the outlet, and short numerical waves run upstream, reflect at the inlet and form a new vortex there, of about 0.15 times the initial peak vorticity, a purely numerical feedback between outlet and inlet even in supersonic flow. The peak vorticity even rises while the vortex crosses the boundary, and the vorticity does not vanish after it has left. Under B3, and identically under B2, the vortex leaves smoothly, the reflected numerical waves are weak, and the peak and total vorticity fall monotonically and vanish once the vortex has gone. The perfectly non-reflecting conditions are well posed here, as in one dimension and in flows without transverse gradients of the mean flow near the boundary. For one-dimensional acoustic waves at normal incidence, Gaussian pulses with a half-width of 15 points, on 121 points, leave with physical reflections below $10^{-6}$ and numerical ones near $10^{-4}$ of the incident amplitude, by their fuller report.

A Poiseuille flow at a Reynolds number of 15 and a Mach number of 0.1, in a duct ten half-widths long, with isothermal walls without slip and an imposed inlet flow rate, tests a viscous flow at low Reynolds number (§6, figs. 12 to 16). Under B1, strong gradients of pressure and temperature form near the outlet, the velocity profile is wrong, and the outlet tends to keep its initial profiles. Under B3, the pressure gradient is 0.998 times the exact one over most of the duct, without boundary layers, and the profiles of velocity converge quickly to the exact solution, those of temperature nearly so in a duct slightly too short for the thermally established regime. Under B4, the acoustic waves are damped quickly in so viscous a flow, and the results are accurate as well, apart from a small disturbance of the temperature near the outlet, which may come from the treatment of the corner or from the reflections of the constant pressure. The authors conclude that their method is more precise and stable than methods based on extrapolation and Riemann invariants, above all with the schemes of direct simulation, whose lack of numerical dissipation lets the errors made at the boundaries persist (§7).

### 2.5 Absorbing layers

#### 2.5.1 Damping in a layer

The nonlinear outflow of turbulent flow, at which linearised conditions err badly (2.3.2), motivates absorbing layers: regions next to the boundary in which the physics of the flow is modified ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5). Many of them can also serve at inflows and in the far field. They either improve the condition that closes the domain or make one unnecessary. Many of their ideas go back to [Israeli and Orszag](#israeli-orszag-1981) (1981), who devised them for linear wave equations when local non-reflecting conditions were still hard to specify. For linear systems those difficulties have since been resolved in other ways (2.2), but the ideas remain in wide use, in linear and nonlinear computations alike. An absorbing layer damps disturbances before they meet the boundary condition: by artificial dissipation through upwinding, by a raised physical viscosity or a hyperviscosity, or most simply by a linear friction, $\mathbf{q}_t + N(\mathbf{q}) = -\sigma(x)(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{q}_o)$ (eq. 15). Here $N$ is a possibly nonlinear operator, $\sigma(x)$ a friction coefficient that usually rises smoothly from zero in the physical domain to a positive value in the layer, and $\mathbf{q}_o$ a nominal state about which disturbances are defined, zero for linear equations. Disturbances decay exponentially while they stay in the layer, and whatever the boundary reflects is damped again on its way back. The inner edge of the layer, however, where $\sigma$ varies, itself reflects (Israeli and Orszag 1981). Without further measures, only a slow rise of the damping over a long distance gives satisfactory results, and the layers become thick and costly.

#### 2.5.2 The perfectly matched layer

A layer becomes non-reflecting if the eigenfunctions of the modes of the interior equations stay unchanged in it, except that their phase speeds become complex so that they decay ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.1). This strictly applies only to linearised systems. The perfectly matched layer (PML) achieves it and damps all disturbances within the layer, which a non-reflecting condition of low order, usually the one-dimensional characteristic one, closes at its outer edge. It was first formulated for electromagnetic radiation ([Berenger](#berenger-1994) 1994), and extended to the linearised Euler equations by [Hu](#hu-1996) (1996), following the modal analysis of 2.2. Early versions became unstable over long times. Later work resolved this, and strongly well-posed layers now exist for inviscid disturbances of a uniform mean flow ([Abarbanel, Gottlieb and Hesthaven](#abarbanel-et-al-1999) 1999, [Hu](#hu-2001) 2001, [Hagstrom](#hagstrom-2003) 2003).

Unlike the local conditions of 2.2.2, the PML has parameters to tune: the thickness of the layer, or the number of grid points that it costs, and the profile $\sigma(x)$ of the absorption. Unless the inner edge is treated as an internal boundary, for instance by characteristic matching (Hagstrom 2003), the profile must be smooth to the order of the scheme, or the edge reflects numerically. [Abarbanel et al.](#abarbanel-et-al-1999) (1999) use $\sigma(x) = C(|x - L|/\delta)^n$, with the width $L$ of the domain, the width $\delta$ of the layer, a constant strength $C$ and $n = 4$ for their method of fourth order. With $C = 1$, acoustic and vortical pulses in two dimensions, in a uniform flow at Mach 0.5 along $x$, leave more accurately through layers of only 6 points than through one-dimensional characteristic conditions, and dramatically better, at greater cost, through layers of 20 to 30 points.

Fig. 5 of the review compares the error over time of an acoustic pulse in a flow at Mach 0.5 under the Padé conditions of degrees $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 2)$, with the treatment of corners of 2.2.2, the PML of Abarbanel et al. (1999) and the supergrid layer of 2.5.5 ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002). The pulse is tested apart from vortical disturbances, since the local Padé conditions are exactly non-reflecting for linear vortical disturbances. The error is measured against a reference solution on a domain so large that no reflection returns within the time considered, which also isolates the error of the boundary from other errors of discretisation. Thick PML layers are needed to match the errors of the Padé condition of degrees $(0, 0)$. That of degrees $(2, 2)$ is best at short times, but no better than $(0, 0)$ at long times, when the error comes from waves tangent to the boundaries, which every local condition reflects completely. Padé conditions of higher order cannot be compared, for lack of a stable treatment of corners. [Hagstrom and Nazarov](#hagstrom-nazarov-2002) (2002) extend the PML to non-uniform flows, in particular to parallel shear flows. The interface between domain and layer can still be kept free of reflection, but the physical instabilities of the mean profile prevent any simple guarantee that all waves decay in the layer. Optimising the two free parameters of the layer, besides its thickness, nevertheless gave satisfactory results.

#### 2.5.3 Layers for nonlinear flow

Applied to nonlinear equations, a linearised PML produces quadratic errors, which make its interface reflect, just as linearised local conditions do ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.2). Whether its non-reflection repays the effort is therefore questionable. Simple modifications of eq. 15 give reasonable results in nonlinear flow, though with thicker layers than in the linear case ([Freund](#freund-1997) 1997). A layer performs better the better the condition that closes it ([Israeli and Orszag](#israeli-orszag-1981) 1981), and needs no layer at all if that condition is exact. Supersonic flow has exact conditions, at least for inviscid flow and without discrete effects, since all waves enter at a supersonic inflow and all leave at a supersonic outflow. Following [Ta'asan and Nark](#taasan-nark-1995) (1995), an artificial convection added to the equations makes the flow supersonic in layers near the inflow and the outflow. At the outflow, waves that the inner edge of the layer lets through cause no reflection at the outer boundary, and those that it reflects are partly damped by the friction of eq. 15 before they return. Applied smoothly, the damping and the convection keep reflections small. At the inflow the friction can be weaker, since waves running upstream slow to zero speed at the sonic point and stay long in the layer, while incoming vortical forcing passes quickly and is damped less. [Karni](#karni-1996) (1996) instead slows the convection in the layer by slowing-down operators, so that the damping has long to act. Like the PML, these act only on the outgoing waves, but the non-reflection is approximate, since the decomposition into modes is one-dimensional, applied at an angle to the boundary that is computed locally from the interior solution. All such layers depend strongly on their width, and have even more parameters and profiles than the PML, to be set by experiment.

#### 2.5.4 Grid stretching and filtering

A mapping of an infinite domain onto a finite one avoids a boundary condition altogether, and is accurate for smooth solutions that neither radiate nor advect ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.3). Waves of a fixed wavelength, however, become ever more poorly resolved where the grid stretches, until the discretisation no longer supports them and aliases them back into the domain. Since the most poorly resolved waves of dispersive differences travel backwards (2.6.1), an infinitely stretched grid returns every disturbance, and infinity reflects perfectly. If the poorly resolved disturbances are dissipated, by dealiasing, artificial viscosity, explicit damping or filtering, the dispersion of the stretched grid even helps, since a gradual stretching lets disturbances stay there as long as wanted. In one dimension, the equations read $\mathbf{q}_t + \xi_x\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})_\xi = 0$, with the physical coordinate $x$ and the finite computational coordinate $\xi$ (eq. 16). The metric $\xi_x$ falls from about 1 near the source to zero as $x \to \infty$, so that, read with $\xi$ as the physical coordinate, the stretching slows every disturbance to rest, like the operators of [Karni](#karni-1996) (1996). [Rai and Moin](#rai-moin-1991) (1991) combined stretching with filtering into an absorbing layer for incompressible flow, and [Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) (1993) applied it to compressible aeroacoustics. In both, the grid was stretched to a large but finite distance and closed by an artificial boundary condition. Colonius et al. switched on an explicit filter gradually in the layer, through variable coefficients, and reduced the reflections of nonlinear vortical structures at the outflow by several orders of magnitude below those of the linearised outflow condition of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990). The layer was, however, wide compared with a PML, and its parameters and profiles could again be set only by trial and error.

#### 2.5.5 Fringes, windowing and the supergrid model

The fringe method of [Spalart](#spalart-1988) (1988) for spatially developing boundary layers avoids conditions at inflow and outflow altogether ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.4). The boundaries are periodic, and an absorbing layer near them changes the flow before it is recycled as inflow. A linear friction like that of eq. 15 is added to the momentum equations, and the difficulty lies in the choice of $\mathbf{q}_o$. The streamwise velocity is driven towards a solution of the Navier–Stokes equations, such as a laminar boundary layer $Q(x, y)$ in two dimensions, made periodic by blending through a smoothed step $S$ that rises from 0 at the start of the fringe, $x = L - \Delta$, to 1 at the edge of the periodic box, $x = L$ (eq. 17). With a Fourier spectral method, the blending functions, usually combinations of Gaussians or hyperbolic tangents, must preserve periodicity and continuous derivatives to round-off. In incompressible flow, the transverse velocity is chosen so that the target is free of divergence. [Nordström, Nordin and Henningson](#nordstrom-et-al-1999) (1999) argue from linear model equations with constant coefficients, and confirm by experiments with the full problem, that the periodic fringe converges, in incompressible flow at least, to the problem with inflow and outflow: disturbances decay before they are recycled, and the fringe has little influence upstream.

**[GK]** As printed, eq. 17 reads $q_o = Q(x, y) - [Q(x - L, y) - Q(x, y)]\,S((x - L + \Delta)/\Delta)$, which gives $2Q(L, y) - Q(0, y)$ at the edge of the box. Periodicity requires the inflow profile $Q(0, y)$ there, which the sign $+$ in front of the bracket gives.

The spatial windowing of [Guo, Adams and Kleiser](#guo-et-al-1994) (1994), revisited by [Schlatter, Adams and Kleiser](#schlatter-et-al-2003) (2003), also combines periodic boundaries with an absorbing layer ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.4). In one dimension, the equations are multiplied by a window $w(x)$, which is zero at the edges of the domain, rises to unity across the fringe and is unity inside: $w\mathbf{q}_t + w\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})_x = 0$, or $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t + (w\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}))_x = w'\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})$ for the windowed variables $\tilde{\mathbf{q}} = w\mathbf{q}$ (eq. 18). If the window meets constraints of differentiability and periodicity like those on $S$, a Fourier expansion of $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}$ keeps spectral accuracy. Since $w$ vanishes at the boundary, $\mathbf{q}$ cannot in general be recovered from $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}$. Where the flow near the boundaries is a uniform base flow with perturbations, the regularised dewindowing $\mathbf{q} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{q}}$ turns eq. 18 into $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t + w\mathbf{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})_x = 0$ (eq. 19), the exact analogue of an infinite stretching of the grid. Disturbances must therefore be dissipated in the fringe, lest they be aliased back. Dealiasing is possible with periodic boundaries, but additional absorption in the fringe works much better ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002), such as the friction of eq. 15 that Guo et al. (1994) add. For a prescribed inflow profile, Schlatter et al. (2003) dewindow by $\mathbf{q} \approx (1 - w)\mathbf{q}_o + \tilde{\mathbf{q}}$, with $\mathbf{q}_o$ as in the fringe method.

Windowing in physical space is filtering in Fourier space, and the reverse, so that windowing filters out the scales larger than the domain ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002). By analogy with large-eddy simulation, which filters small scales and supplies a model for those below the grid, a supergrid model should then correct for the unphysical effects of truncating the domain. Colonius and Ran (2002) take $\mathbf{q} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{q}}$ and filter eq. 19 in physical space, which amounts to dewindowing, since large scales are shortened continually as they travel into the fringe, as on a stretched grid. The filtered equations then need models for the nonlinear products in $\mathbf{f}$, as in large-eddy simulation, and for the products of the window with the fluxes, for which a Gaussian filter and a model of tensor diffusivity were chosen, modified to be globally diffusive. In model problems the boundary is then highly transparent ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, figs. 4 and 5). For the acoustic pulse it performs a little better than a PML of the same number of points, and so of about the same cost. For the nonlinear vortex of 2.3.2, a layer of 10 points reflects 1000 times less than the strength of the vortex, whatever that strength, even when the vortex induces inflow at the outflow boundary. The review grants that nothing yet favours windowing and the supergrid model over other absorbing layers, whose parameters and profiles could be optimised to a similar performance. The supergrid model, however, has fewer parameters to tune. They reduce to two scalars, the number of points across the layer and the maximum slope of the window, for which, properly scaled, only a small range of values comes into question, with little influence on the results. The analogy with the modelling of turbulence may also frame better models, and periodic boundaries may ease estimates of the energy ([Nordström et al.](#nordstrom-et-al-1999) 1999).

#### 2.5.6 Wave-damping zones in simulations of discs

[de Val-Borro et al.](#de-val-borro-et-al-2006) (2006) compare grid and particle codes on a planet that opens a gap in a two-dimensional disc, between the radii $0.4a$ and $2.5a$, with the orbital radius $a$ of the planet, on $128 \times 384$ cells in radius and azimuth (§3). The disc is isolated, without inflow of mass, and the codes in polar coordinates close it with solid boundaries, next to which zones kill the waves to reduce their reflection (§3.2). In the radial ranges $[0.4a, 0.5a]$ and $[2.1a, 2.5a]$, the equation $dx/dt = -(x - x_0)R(r)/\tau$ is solved after each time step for the surface density and the components of the velocity, $x$ (eq. 10). Here $\tau$ is the orbital period at the boundary concerned, and $R(r)$ a parabolic function that is 1 at the boundary of the domain and 0 at the inner edge of the zone. The paper does not define the values $x_0$ towards which the damping drives. The codes in Cartesian coordinates damp in the outer ring alike, and let gas flow out freely through the edges of their square grid.

The damping does not conserve mass, although the loss is small (§3.2). After 200 orbits, the disc of the heavier planet has lost about 8 per cent of its mass in two of the shock-capturing codes and about 3 per cent in three of the upwind codes (§5.1). With the lighter planet, the damping near the inner boundary may also add angular momentum to the disc artificially (§5.3). Only the region between $0.5a$ and $2.1a$ is meaningful for the comparison. Despite the damping, the vortensity shows reflected waves in four codes, NIRVANA-GDA, RH2D, GLOBAL and GENESIS, all of them among the upwind codes of §4.1.1 (§5.1 and §5.2, figs. 3 and 11), and several codes reflect waves at the outer boundary in the viscous runs with the lighter planet (§5.4, fig. 27).

### 2.6 Characteristic conditions in finite-volume codes with ghost cells

#### 2.6.1 Boundary closures and their stability

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §3) treats discretisation in the setting of finite differences, the most common, and notes that analogous effects arise in other discretisations. Two related problems of closure arise at a boundary: wide stencils do not fit near it, and the conditions fix only some of the variables. The first is met by one-sided stencils at the boundary and biased ones next to it, which may be one order less accurate than the interior scheme without lowering its overall order ([Gustafsson](#gustafsson-1975) 1975). The second is met either by extrapolating the unspecified variables from the interior or by imposing some of the full equations, or combinations of them, at the boundary, whose derivatives normal to it then need one-sided stencils. Both can be accurate, but stability needs more care. Von Neumann analysis with periodic boundaries is neither necessary nor sufficient in general. Schemes of first and second order, which need special treatment only at the boundary point, have caused little difficulty, but stable closures for schemes of high order, and compact ones above all, have been much harder to find.

Most analyses use the advection equation $u_t + cu_x = 0$ on $[0, 1]$, with $c > 0$ and an inflow at $x = 0$ (eq. 9). Lax stability keeps the solution bounded as the mesh is refined at a fixed time, and the normal-mode theory of [Gustafsson, Kreiss and Sundström](#gustafsson-et-al-1972) (1972) analyses it. Asymptotic stability keeps the error bounded in time, and requires that the spatial operator of the semi-discrete scheme have no eigenvalues in the right half of the complex plane. Stable closures are usually found a posteriori, by computing the eigenvalues numerically, and often only by sacrificing accuracy at the boundary. Since closures are combined with other approximations of the continuous conditions anyway, the order of accuracy near the boundary matters less than stability and conservation. Operators that satisfy summation by parts, with boundary conditions imposed by simultaneous approximation terms, reduce stability to algebraic constraints on the boundary stencils ([Carpenter, Gottlieb and Abarbanel](#carpenter-et-al-1994) 1994). Such a term imposes the condition non-locally: it adds a dissipative term that drives the solution towards the boundary condition near the boundary, in the spirit of the buffer regions of 2.5.

The dispersion of difference schemes causes reflections of another kind ([Trefethen](#trefethen-1982) 1982, [Vichnevetsky](#vichnevetsky-1987) 1987). The central difference $u_x \approx (u_{j+1} - u_{j-1})/(2\Delta x)$ applied to eq. 9 has two normal modes $u_j(t) = \hat{u}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\kappa^j$ at each frequency below the cut-off $c/\Delta x$, with $\kappa^2 + 2i\Omega\kappa - 1 = 0$ and $\Omega = \omega\Delta x/c$ (eqs. 10 and 11, fig. 3). One travels at nearly the correct group velocity $c$ at small wavenumbers and converges to the solution. The other, spurious mode has a negative group velocity and only a few points per wavelength. Spurious waves can feed back from downstream even in supersonic flow, and in subsonic flow they add a path of feedback to the physical sound that approximate outflow conditions excite. Such feedback can make a shear layer that is nominally only convectively unstable force itself ([Buell and Huerre](#buell-huerre-1988) 1988, [Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) 1993). Larger stencils add further spurious modes, which decay into the domain. Upwind-biased schemes damp the short spurious waves quickly, so that they seldom matter, but simulations that resolve turbulence usually prefer schemes of low dissipation and high order.

In the discrete case, even the trivially non-reflecting condition of eq. 9 reflects, since any local closure couples the physical and spurious modes: physical into spurious at the outflow, and spurious into physical at the inflow. Discretely non-reflecting closures minimise these reflections, and their reflection coefficients also decide their stability, which requires them to be bounded for all complex $\omega$. For the linearised Euler equations in several dimensions, an isentropic discretisation has nine reflection coefficients at a boundary instead of four, since each physical wave has a spurious partner of opposite group velocity ([Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) 2000). Localising the exact continuous condition and the discretely exact closure are then two approximations whose errors can work against each other. By 2004, neither summation by parts nor discretely non-reflecting closures had been used in more general computations, with non-uniform mean flows, corners, nonlinearity or viscosity.

[Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992, §5, fig. 7) carry this distinction over to outlets. The physical waves that an outlet reflects have long wavelengths, while the numerical ones are shorter than four cells for a centred scheme of second order and travel upstream with a group velocity $u_g = k_gU$. The ratio $k_g$ is $-1$ for central differences of second order, $-3$ for the Padé scheme of fourth order and $-13/3$ for the scheme of sixth order of their code. Numerical waves that reach the inlet return as physical waves, which closes a numerical feedback between the two boundaries. An acoustic wave that meets a reflecting outlet in subsonic flow returns both a physical wave at $u - c$ and a numerical one, and in supersonic flow the numerical one alone, so that supersonic outlets are no general cure. A boundary treatment therefore has two reflection coefficients, of physical and of numerical waves, and both must be small.

Some studies extrapolate all flow variables, or the fluxes, at an outflow ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4), which corresponds to no boundary condition at all for the continuous problem. The closure by extrapolation still amounts to some condition, even if its continuous counterpart is hard to establish.

#### 2.6.2 A finite-volume scheme in characteristic form

[Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) (2024, §5) build their code CHAR by treating every face of a cell as a boundary (fig. 4). The primitive variables are averages over cells, stored at their centres. At each face, the state is the average of the two neighbouring cell states (eq. 37), and the eigensystem of the Jacobian normal to the face, computed from that state, is split into its positive and negative subspaces. The difference of the two cell states across the face, divided by their distance, is projected onto both subspaces: the positive part updates the cell on the right of the face, the negative part the cell on the left. Each cell is thus updated by the incoming characteristics of its six faces, plus the inhomogeneous terms (eq. 44), a finite-volume form of the characteristic equations that works anywhere in the interior. At a boundary face, the incoming subspace alone is unknown, and setting it is the boundary condition. Once the incoming characteristic derivatives at the face are set, the normal derivative of the state across it follows, since the incoming modes always involve at least seven primitive variables and $B_z$ follows from the solenoidal condition. This fixes the state in the ghost cell beyond the face, although the update of the boundary cell needs only the product $A_{zI}\partial_z\mathbf{U} = S_{zI}\mathbf{L}_{zI}$, not the ghost values themselves. The boundary layer is advanced by a forward-Euler step of first order. Two simplifications remain. Since the state beyond the face is unknown, the incoming subspace at the boundary face is approximated by that of the boundary cell, so that the ghost, face and cell states are not exactly consistent. A nonlinear optimisation that would make them consistent was not yet stable.

#### 2.6.3 Finite differences and the transverse terms

[Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) (2024, App. D) first tried finite differences. The transverse terms were split by upwinding with the subspaces of the eigensystem of each cell, $A_x\partial_x\mathbf{U} \approx A_{x+,i}(\mathbf{U}_i - \mathbf{U}_{i-1})/(x_i - x_{i-1}) + A_{x-,i}(\mathbf{U}_{i+1} - \mathbf{U}_i)/(x_{i+1} - x_i)$, and alike in $y$ (eqs. D16 to D19). The scheme was unstable. Each of $A^+$ and $A^-$ holds non-physical terms that cancel only in their sum. The cancellation fails when different derivatives are projected onto the subspaces of one eigensystem, or one derivative onto the subspaces of two eigensystems, as when a difference across a face updates one cell through the eigensystem of that cell and its neighbour through the eigensystem of the neighbour. A Taylor expansion shows the errors to be of second order. In the finite-volume scheme of 2.6.2, a single centred derivative is always projected onto the pair of subspaces of the single eigensystem at the face, which keeps the updates of the two cells in balance.

A ghost cell can likewise be set from the incoming characteristic derivatives by a one-sided difference, $\mathbf{U}_{-1} \approx \mathbf{U}_0 - \Delta z\,E^{-1}\mathbf{L}_z$, with the transformation $E^{-1}$ from characteristic to normal derivatives (App. E, eqs. E21 to E23). [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987, [1990](#thompson-1990)), as Tarr et al. restate him, described and implemented this method for hydrodynamics in two dimensions. Tarr et al. found that it worked well for a variety of one-dimensional problems of magnetohydrodynamics, but failed in several dimensions when coupled to the finite-difference transverse terms above, which led them to the finite-volume scheme.

#### 2.6.4 Coupling codes through ghost cells

CHAR serves as a boundary module for another code ([Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) 2024, §6). Coupled to the Lagrangian-remap code LaRe3D ([Arber et al.](#arber-et-al-2001) 2001), it overlaps the two layers of ghost cells and the first two interior layers of LaRe3D along the boundary, and advances in step with it, at the time step of LaRe3D. At the end of each step, the cells of CHAR inside the domain of LaRe3D are overwritten with values interpolated from LaRe3D, and the ghost cells of LaRe3D with values interpolated from CHAR. The hybrid is needed because LaRe3D stores its variables on a staggered grid and advances them by a predictor-corrector scheme, in which the characteristic decomposition is awkward to formulate. Since CHAR is independent of the meshing, gridding and time advance of the code it serves, it can couple to any code of magnetohydrodynamics, join codes of different physics, or drive a layer inside a domain (§6, §8).

#### 2.6.5 Characteristic conditions through the ghost cells of a Godunov scheme

[Motheau, Almgren and Bell](#motheau-et-al-2017) (2017) set characteristic conditions through ghost cells, so that a finite-volume code keeps the same stencil and method at the boundary as in the interior (§I). A Godunov method computes time-centred fluxes on the faces of the boundary from values extrapolated in space and time from cell centres, those of the ghost cells included (fig. 1). Ghost cells are commonly filled by extrapolation of zeroth or first order, which suffices for the one-dimensional Euler equations, a well-posed problem, but in complex multi-dimensional flows generates spurious waves that travel back into the domain. A direct implementation of characteristic conditions would need special discretisations at the boundary, and the only earlier characteristic method for ghost cells known to the authors filled a single ghost cell at first order in one dimension, without transverse terms or corners. Their method, GC-NSCBC, is shown with the unsplit Godunov scheme of second order of the compressible astrophysical code CASTRO, with piecewise parabolic reconstruction, characteristic tracing and full corner coupling, whose stencil of nine points needs four ghost cells (§III, §IV). In two dimensions, Motheau et al. use the four wave amplitudes of Poinsot and Lele for the primitive variables $\mathbf{Q} = (\rho, u, v, p)^\mathrm{T}$, with $\mathcal{L}_4$ for the downstream acoustic wave (eqs. 20 to 25). Since the ghost values are needed at time $n$, the time derivatives of the LODI relations play no part. Instead, the amplitudes, the outgoing ones computed from the interior with one-sided differences of second order and the incoming ones modelled, are transformed back into the normal derivatives of the primitive variables in the last interior cell, $\partial\mathbf{Q}/\partial x = S\Lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ (eqs. 26, 39 and 40). The first ghost value then follows from the centred difference across that cell, $\mathbf{Q}_{i-1} = \mathbf{Q}_{i+1} - 2\Delta x\,\partial\mathbf{Q}/\partial x$, and each further one from a wider difference formula for the same derivative (eqs. 27 to 34). The method suits any number of ghost cells and other schemes that use them, and extends directly to three dimensions (§IV, §VI).

The incoming amplitudes follow the NSCBC with transverse terms (§IV.A). At a subsonic outflow, $\mathcal{L}_\varphi = K(p - p_t) - (1 - \beta)T_\varphi$, with $\varphi = 1$ at the right boundary and 4 at the left, a target pressure $p_t$, the transverse term $T_\varphi$ weighted through $\beta$, and $K = \sigma c(1 - M^2)/l_x$ for a domain of length $l_x$, with $\sigma$ usually 0.25 (eqs. 35 to 38). The choices of $\sigma$ and $\beta$ depend strongly on the flow, the Mach number may be taken locally or averaged over the boundary, and no consensus on them exists. At a subsonic inflow, the three incoming amplitudes relax the normal velocity, the temperature and the tangential velocity towards targets $u_t$, $T_t$ and $v_t$, with coefficients $\eta$, each less its transverse term (eqs. 42 to 46). At walls the normal velocity vanishes, so that $\mathcal{L}_2 = \mathcal{L}_3 = 0$ and the amplitude of the reflected acoustic wave equals that of the incident one. For the viscous fluxes, the ghost cells then take the velocity of the wall and, at an isothermal wall, its temperature, or at an adiabatic one the temperature of the last interior cell (§IV.A.3). At corners the waves along both axes are coupled. Their derivatives fill the ghost cells along both axes of the corner cell, and the rest of each edge is filled as above (§IV.B, fig. 2, eqs. 47 to 55). Following [Lodato, Domingo and Vervisch](#lodato-et-al-2008) (2008), a linear system couples the unknown waves at a corner between two outflows. Where an inflow meets an outflow, imposing all waves would be unstable, and a compatibility condition sets the incoming wave of the outflow to zero, so that the pressure adapts to the local flow (eqs. 56 to 67).

Four tests compare the method with ghost cells filled by copying the last interior value, the zero-gradient outflow of 1.3 (§V). In an inviscid channel of 1 m on $256 \times 32$ points, periodic across the flow, the inflow velocity is suddenly doubled from 20 to 40 m/s (§V.A, fig. 3). With extrapolation, which imposes the new velocity in the ghost cells of the inlet, the flow settles quickly but in a wrong state: the velocity misses its target, and density and pressure take unphysical values, the symptoms of an ill-posed boundary. With GC-NSCBC, without transverse terms, $\eta = 2$ and $\sigma = 0.25$, the flow adjusts more smoothly and reaches the targets, with density and pressure unaffected. In the second test (§V.B, fig. 4), a vortex is carried out of a square domain at 100 m/s, as in case C of [Granet et al.](#granet-et-al-2010) (2010), on $128 \times 128$ points, with $\sigma = 0.25$, inflow coefficients $\eta = 1$ and $\beta$ the Mach number averaged over each boundary. It leaves with its shape kept and almost no spurious reflection, very close to the results of Granet et al. with a cell-vertex code that needs no ghost cells. With extrapolation the vortex appears to be sucked through the boundary and is distorted, and strong pressure fluctuations arise there and remain in the domain. A circular pressure pulse that leaves a square domain through four outflows keeps its circular shape under GC-NSCBC, with $\beta = 0.5$ and without instability at the corners, but takes a square shape under extrapolation, which gradually sends spurious waves inwards (§V.C, fig. 5). The flow over a backward-facing step at a Reynolds number of 800, with a parabolic inflow profile, walls without slip and an outflow, reproduces the two recirculation zones and the velocity profile of a reference solution, without instabilities near the corners (§V.D, figs. 6 and 7).

### 2.7 Magnetohydrodynamics

#### 2.7.1 Magnetohydrodynamics in characteristic form

[Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) (2024, §2) write the ideal equations of magnetohydrodynamics for the primitive variables $\mathbf{U} = (\rho, \epsilon, v_x, v_y, v_z, B_x, B_y, B_z)^\mathrm{T}$, with the specific internal energy $\epsilon$ of an ideal gas and in units in which the permeability drops out, as $\partial_t\mathbf{U} + A_x\partial_x\mathbf{U} + A_y\partial_y\mathbf{U} + A_z\partial_z\mathbf{U} + \mathbf{D} = 0$, with inhomogeneous terms $\mathbf{D}$ such as gravity (eqs. 1 to 3). Each matrix $A_n$ can be diagonalised, as $A_z = S_z\Lambda_zS_z^{-1}$, but the three cannot be diagonalised at once outside special cases. This does not matter for a boundary, at which only the direction normal to it is diagonalised (eq. 4). The eight eigenvalues are $\lambda_{1,2} = v_z$, $\lambda_{3,4} = v_z \mp c_a$, $\lambda_{5,6} = v_z \mp c_s$ and $\lambda_{7,8} = v_z \mp c_f$ (eqs. 5 to 7). Here $c_a = |b_z|$ is the Alfvén speed projected on $z$, with $b_n = B_n/\sqrt{\rho}$, and the fast and slow speeds are $c_{f,s}^2 = \frac{1}{2}(a^2 + b^2) \pm \frac{1}{2}[(a^2 + b^2)^2 - 4a^2b_z^2]^{1/2}$, with the speed of sound $a$, $a^2 = \gamma(\gamma - 1)\epsilon$, and $b^2 = \sum_n b_n^2$. The system has one eigenvalue $v_z$ more than that of [Roe and Balsara](#roe-balsara-1996) (1996), since it keeps $B_z$ as a variable and enforces the solenoidal constraint through an additional mode rather than by construction. In degenerate limits, such as a vanishing field, the eight solutions collapse onto the five of hydrodynamics.

Grouping the derivatives in each direction with the eigenvalues and the left eigenvectors defines the characteristic derivatives $\mathbf{L}_n = \Lambda_nS_n^{-1}\partial_n\mathbf{U}$, with which the equations read $\partial_t\mathbf{U} + S_x\mathbf{L}_x + S_y\mathbf{L}_y + S_z\mathbf{L}_z + \mathbf{D} = 0$ (eqs. 9 and 10). Each component $L_\sigma$ is a mode. Its left eigenvector weights the derivatives of the primitive variables into one coupled change that travels at one speed $\lambda_\sigma$, the value of $L_\sigma$ is the strength of the mode, and the right eigenvectors give the response of each primitive variable to it. Of all conceivable variations of $\mathbf{U}$, only those that the eight characteristic derivatives describe are admitted by the equations. For a boundary normal to $z$, the transverse and inhomogeneous terms join in $\mathbf{C} = A_x\partial_x\mathbf{U} + A_y\partial_y\mathbf{U} + \mathbf{D}$, which can be computed from the state in the plane of the boundary, so that $\partial_t\mathbf{U} + S_z\mathbf{L}_z + \mathbf{C} = 0$ (eqs. 11 and 12). The vector $\mathbf{L}_z$, by contrast, holds derivatives normal to the plane, and its components that travel inwards carry information from outside, which the interior cannot supply.

The modes are $L_1 = v_zB_z'$, which conserves the magnetic flux carried with the flow, the entropy mode $L_2$, and the Alfvén modes $L_3$ and $L_4$, the slow modes $L_5$ and $L_6$ and the fast modes $L_7$ and $L_8$, each pair travelling backwards and forwards relative to the flow (eq. 14). Their eigenvectors take the normalisation of [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) (1988) and Roe and Balsara (1996), which stays well behaved in the degenerate limits, for instance by setting $\beta_x = \beta_y = 1/\sqrt{2}$ where the transverse field vanishes (eq. 15). Expanded, the equations show how each primitive variable responds to the modes (eq. 18). The density, for instance, changes because fluid of another entropy arrives through $L_2$, or because slow or fast disturbances arrive through $L_5$ to $L_8$. A variable responds to every mode whatever its direction, but each characteristic derivative must be computed upwind, so that the change it represents travels in the right direction. The transformation between $\mathbf{L}_z$ and $\partial_z\mathbf{U}$ has full rank for any valid field in three dimensions, and changing one characteristic derivative changes several primitive variables at once. This is why characteristics suit boundary conditions: each mode is a coupled, physically admissible variation of the whole state.

#### 2.7.2 Incoming modes at a boundary

Three senses of direction must be kept apart ([Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) 2024, §2, fig. 1). A mode travels forwards or backwards relative to the bulk velocity, by the sign in its eigenvalue, in the positive or negative direction of the axis, by the sign of the eigenvalue itself, and inwards or outwards relative to a boundary. At the lower boundary of a domain the inward modes are the positive ones, at the upper boundary the negative ones. Table 1 lists the inward modes at a lower boundary $z_\mathrm{min}$ against the normal velocity, with $0 \le c_s \le c_a \le c_f$. Where the plasma leaves faster than the fast speed, $v_z \le -c_f$, all eight modes leave, and the outside cannot influence the domain at all. Slower outflow lets in the forward fast mode alone ($-c_f \le v_z \le -c_a$), then also the forward Alfvén mode ($-c_a \le v_z \le -c_s$), and the forward slow mode as well ($-c_s \le v_z \le 0$). Inflow adds the two advected modes $L_1$ and $L_2$ ($0 \le v_z \le c_s$), and then in turn the backward slow, Alfvén and fast modes, until all eight enter where $v_z \ge c_f$. The inward modes reach seven of the eight equations at an outflow and all eight at an inflow, since $B_z$ responds only to $L_1$. A mode whose speed matches the bulk velocity does not propagate and has zero amplitude, so that the bounds of each range belong to both neighbours. The number of incoming modes thus changes along a boundary and in time with the local velocity (§4). Since the characteristics do not map one to one onto the primitive variables, constraints set arbitrarily on primitive variables may leave the boundary consistent, under-determined or over-determined (§4). Where the supplied information is not exactly consistent with the equations, the boundary is over-determined, and the closest admissible state must be sought (§2).

At a boundary, the eigenvalues split into positive and negative parts, $\Lambda = \Lambda^+ + \Lambda^-$ with $\Lambda^\pm = (\Lambda \pm |\Lambda|)/2$, and the projections $P^\pm = \Lambda^\pm\Lambda^{-1}$, with the pseudo-inverse, select the modes of each direction (§3, eqs. 19 to 27). They split the coefficient matrices into $A = A^+ + A^-$ and the characteristic derivatives into $\mathbf{L}^\pm = P^\pm\mathbf{L}$, whose spatial derivatives are taken upwind: from below for the positive modes and from above for the negative ones. The subscripts I and O name these subspaces as inward and outward at a given boundary.

Setting a characteristic boundary condition means setting the characteristic derivative of each incoming mode (§3). [Thompson](#thompson-1990) (1990), as Tarr et al. restate him, catalogued standard conditions of hydrodynamics in these terms, such as walls with and without slip, uniform inflow, non-reflection and constant pressure, and [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992) extended the method to terms that are not hyperbolic, such as viscosity. Such conditions start from a physical constraint, compute the normal derivatives of the primitive variables that it implies, and solve for the incoming characteristic derivatives. Non-reflecting conditions, which [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), as Tarr et al. restate him, first derived for general nonlinear hyperbolic systems, magnetohydrodynamics included, set the incoming amplitudes to zero, for instance $L_8 = 0$ where only the fast mode enters. Tarr et al. stress that these conditions are truly non-reflecting only for simple waves. As Hedstrom already noted, a weak shock or the interaction of several simple waves changes the exterior in ways that should eventually cause reflections, whereas the conditions assume that the exterior is a passive reservoir, unchanged by what leaves through the boundary.

#### 2.7.3 Over-specification through the primitive variables

Setting one primitive variable at a boundary implies how the others evolve ([Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) 2024, §8). An update $B_x \to B_x + \delta B_x$, meant as an Alfvén wave travelling along the background field $B_z$, requires $v_x \to v_x - \delta B_x/\sqrt{\rho}$, and any other velocity represents no Alfvén wave, perhaps no admissible motion at all. For many overlapping waves or an emerging flux rope, consistent settings are hard or impossible to find directly, and some dynamics are not even allowed. An update that represents a slow wave travelling inwards, where the plasma leaves faster than the local Alfvén speed, is inadmissible, since no slow mode then enters (2.7.2). Codes apply such conditions anyway, LaRe3D among them, with unknown effects, and since magnetohydrodynamics propagates information, bad information that enters through the boundary eventually corrupts the whole system. Conditions set through the incoming characteristics avoid this. The modes are the admissible variations of the state (2.7.1), so that the primitive variables are set consistently and the boundary cannot be over-specified. Non-reflecting conditions, however, make strong assumptions about the exterior that are seldom stated. In tests of several of them, Tarr et al. found that they work reasonably for transient disturbances such as waves and shocks, but can fail badly when larger structures are carried out of the domain, as [Kee et al.](#kee-et-al-2025) (2025) show.

#### 2.7.4 Two non-reflecting conditions in several dimensions

[Kee et al.](#kee-et-al-2025) (2025) implement non-reflecting conditions on the formalism of 2.7.1 in the code LaRe3D, and ask whether a domain with such a boundary behaves like a larger domain without it (§1, §2). In one dimension, a non-reflecting condition holds each incoming characteristic derivative at its initial value, which is zero in a locally uniform plasma (§2.1). In several dimensions, transverse derivatives give the modes components along the boundary, and the different modes need not be aligned, so that non-reflection has no unique meaning. Two common choices are compared. Fixed conditions hold each incoming derivative at its initial function of space, reset it to that value whenever the mode enters again, and set it to zero for a mode that first left and later enters (§2.1.1). In all their runs the initial values are zero, so that all incoming derivatives vanish at all times. Nothing inside the domain may then alter them, and the boundary evolves through the outgoing derivatives, the transverse terms and the inhomogeneous terms alone, as in the conditions of [Grappin, Léorat and Buttighoffer](#grappin-et-al-2000) (2000) and [Grappin, Aulanier and Pinto](#grappin-et-al-2008) (2008). Cancellation conditions set each incoming derivative to cancel its share of the transverse and inhomogeneous terms, $L_{\sigma,I} = -\sum_\zeta S^{-1}_{\sigma\zeta}C_\zeta$, which reduces to zero for a uniform plasma in one dimension (§2.1.2). The outgoing derivatives cannot change them, but other information on the boundary can, through $\mathbf{C}$, and the time derivatives at the boundary fall towards zero as more modes enter. [Jiang et al.](#jiang-et-al-2011) (2011) use them, and the conditions of the code Bifrost ([Gudiksen et al.](#gudiksen-et-al-2011) 2011) take their limit of zero velocity, which cancels the transverse forces but leaves the transverse advection alone (§5).

The authors stress a mismatch between what such conditions do and how they are used (§2.1.2). A non-reflecting condition removes the influence of the outgoing on the incoming derivatives, whereas it is often used as though it mimicked a larger domain. A static, force-free field shows the difference. In characteristic terms it is a superposition of standing Alfvén, slow and fast waves that cancels the transverse terms, so that changing any one derivative upsets the balance. The Fixed conditions preserve such a field, since every derivative keeps its balanced initial value, while the Cancellation conditions preserve it only in trivial cases. Once the field is advected, even uniformly, both fail to keep the balance, since its derivatives vary along the boundary and must then vary in time. The Fixed conditions cannot vary, and the Cancellation conditions vary only with the transverse terms, whereas the balance involves $\mathbf{C}$ and both the incoming and the outgoing derivatives. Removing information from the domain is easy, but removing it correctly is very complex.

The boundary runs in the separate characteristic code of 2.6.4, in a thin layer that covers the ghost cells of LaRe3D (§2.2). LaRe3D keeps density and internal energy at cell centres, each component of the field on a face and the velocity at the vertices, while the characteristic code keeps all variables at cell centres. At each time step, the first two interior layers of LaRe3D are averaged onto it, and its values are averaged back into the ghost cells. The normal field in the ghost cells is instead solved from $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ with the stencil of LaRe3D, which keeps the divergence zero to round-off. At the boundary itself, where no average across the face exists, the eigensystem is taken from the nearest cell centre.

#### 2.7.5 Tests against a larger domain

Every test compares a run with a non-reflecting lower boundary against a ground truth: the same problem on a domain that extends beyond that boundary ([Kee et al.](#kee-et-al-2025) 2025, §3, §4). A weighted mean squared difference of all primitive variables, normalised by their covariance in the ground truth, measures the agreement point by point (eq. 3).

A hot sphere, with 10 per cent more internal energy than a uniform plasma with $\rho = \epsilon = 1$ and a field $\mathbf{B} = (1, 1, 1)$, expands along the field at a plasma beta near 1, so that the number of incoming modes varies along the boundary and in time (§3). On $128^3$ cells in a unit cube, periodic at the sides, the sphere starts 0.3 above the boundary, and the ground truth extends one unit further down. By $t = 1$, fast, slow and Alfvén disturbances have all met the boundary. Both conditions pass them out without reflection, and the cells agree with the ground truth to fractions of a percent (fig. 2), so that the runs look like cut-outs of the larger one. The patterns of vertical velocity left in the plane of the boundary differ slightly, since each run carries an implicitly different model of the exterior: in the periodic ground truth, waves that leave downwards wrap around and re-enter. In one dimension, at 1024 cells per unit length, a linear Alfvén wave passes all but about one part in a thousand, rarefactions of the shock tubes of [Sod](#sod-1978) (1978) and of [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) (1988) leave differences below 0.1 per cent, and their shocks, contacts and compound waves differences of about 1 per cent and a few per cent (App. A). The zero-gradient boundaries of LaRe3D, at the far ends, reflect so much that the runs are stopped before those reflections arrive. Even the reflection of the Alfvén wave is the condition doing what it is designed to do: in this set-up the wave has amplitude only in the two fast characteristics, and setting the incoming one to zero misrepresents the influence of the part that has left on the part that remains.

A spheromak of unit radius, in a domain of four radii on $128^3$ cells, is first relaxed and then carried out through the lower boundary at a uniform speed $v_\mathrm{ad}$ (§4). After relaxation, the fast speed lies between 0.97 and 3.27, the Alfvén speed between 0 and 2.24, and the slow speed between 0 and 0.95, and four speeds of advection, 3.5, 2.3, 1.0 and 0.1, give outflows faster than all modes, between the fast and Alfvén speeds, between the Alfvén and slow speeds, and slower than the slow mode. While the spheromak passes, zero, one, two and three modes enter. In the two faster cases, the runs differ negligibly from the ground truth, by an average of about one percent of a standard deviation. The largest jump in the difference lies between the second and third cases, at the step from outflow faster than the Alfvén speed to slower. In the two slower cases, the differences are of order unity or more, as large as the variations of the spheromak itself, and they grow as the advection slows, whichever condition is used. In the slowest case the two conditions differ wildly: the Cancellation condition pulls the spheromak out faster than the flow, while part of it bounces off the Fixed condition. Even in the second case, the two conditions end with different numbers of incoming modes.

#### 2.7.6 Why a non-reflecting boundary is not a boundary of minimal impact

The departures from the ground truth arise from the loss of information that a non-reflecting condition implies ([Kee et al.](#kee-et-al-2025) 2025, §5). The incoming derivatives carry all the influence of the exterior on the domain. The Fixed conditions remove them, the Cancellation conditions set them from transverse and inhomogeneous terms alone, and neither represents the exterior in general. What has left the domain may still matter to what remains. [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), as Kee et al. restate him, gave the example of a slow wave that leaves, followed by a fast wave that overtakes it outside, where their interaction can send a wave back into the domain. A non-reflecting condition discards such returns along with true reflections, and the two kinds of difference are hard to tell apart. Both grow with the number of incoming modes. Numerical reflections of the method itself are negligible, since driving the same boundary with the full state of the ground truth reproduced it closely in the work of [Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) (2024). The conditions of Bifrost come close to a condition of minimal impact, since they let advection from the interior persist while removing the forces at the boundary, but they ignore the outgoing derivatives in the balance of forces and the non-advective terms, such as compressive heating, in the updates of density, energy and field. Kee et al. seek a condition of minimal impact, which would let structures leave as though the boundary were absent. For the top boundary of simulations of coronal mass ejections, the choice matters: Cancellation conditions would make eruptions easier, and Fixed conditions harder (§6).

### 2.8 Open boundaries in astrophysical simulations of clouds and winds

#### 2.8.1 Discs

In the comparison of codes on a planet in a disc of [de Val-Borro et al.](#de-val-borro-et-al-2006) (2006), FARGO tested a transmitted-wave condition against the damping zones of 2.5.6 (§4.1.1.4). The pitch angle of the wake of the planet at the inner and outer boundaries follows from the WKB approximation, and the ghost ring takes the content of the last ring of the domain, shifted in azimuth by the amount that this angle dictates. The condition removes reflected waves very efficiently, and gives results like those of the damping zones, since both aim to avoid the reflection of waves (§6).

#### 2.8.2 A subsonic wind through an inflow boundary

[Mandal et al.](#mandal-et-al-2024) (2024) drive winds from an active galactic nucleus onto turbulent, self-gravitating clouds with the code PLUTO, using the HLLC Riemann solver, piecewise parabolic reconstruction and a Runge–Kutta integrator of third order (§2.1), on $1024 \times 512 \times 512$ cells over $200 \times 100 \times 100\,\mathrm{pc}$ (§2.2). The wind enters through the left boundary, whose ghost cells hold its density, velocity and pressure (§2.6). The other boundaries are diode boundaries, outflow conditions modified to forbid inflow, so that gas can only leave. Most of the winds are dominated by thermal energy and subsonic, at Mach numbers of 0.28 to 0.33 (Table 1). At a subsonic inflow, two of the three characteristic waves of one-dimensional flow enter and one leaves, so that only two primitive variables, ideally density and pressure or density and velocity, can be prescribed, and the third must follow from the interior. Prescribing all three reflects the outgoing wave numerically at the boundary, which a non-reflecting condition would avoid. Without one, the wind does not emerge as injected. After a transient, its parameters in the first interior cell settle by about 0.4 cloud-crushing times at values that differ from those in the ghost cells (Table 2, fig. 1): the density falls by about 20 per cent, the velocity rises by 48 to 71 per cent, and the pressure falls by 27 to 46 per cent. The Mach number thus roughly doubles, to 0.62 to 0.68, while the power of the wind rises by 9 to 28 per cent. The authors judge their qualitative results unaffected, since the settled winds still lie in the intended range of parameters.

In App. A they test a non-reflecting inflow in one dimension. From the primitive equations and the eigenvectors of their Jacobian, taken as locally constant, the characteristic variables are $w_1 = p/(\rho a) - u$, $w_2 = \rho - p/a^2$ and $w_3 = p/(\rho a) + u$, for the speeds $u - a$, $u$ and $u + a$ (eqs. A1 to A19). At the inflow, the outgoing variable $w_1$ is held at its value in the first interior cell, while density and velocity take their target values, which gives the ghost pressure $p_\mathrm{B} = \gamma\rho_\mathrm{t}(w_{1,\mathrm{I}} + u_\mathrm{t})^2$ (eqs. A20 to A26). In a run with the most powerful wind at half the resolution, density and velocity then stay at their targets, but the pressure, and with it the power, departs from its target by about 90 per cent, more than the power of the wind injected with all three variables, which varied by about 30 per cent (fig. A1). A non-reflecting inflow thus does not ensure the intended power either, since one primitive variable stays free. Runs that take the settled density and velocity as targets agree with the main results once the wind has settled.

**[GK]** With $a^2 = \gamma p/\rho$, $p/(\rho a) = a/\gamma$, so that eq. A23 holds $a/\gamma - u$ fixed between the first interior cell and the ghost cell. For equal densities this gives $p_\mathrm{B} \approx p_\mathrm{I} + 2\rho a(u_\mathrm{B} - u_\mathrm{I})$, twice the change $\rho a(u_\mathrm{B} - u_\mathrm{I})$ that the outgoing characteristic carries in linear acoustics, and for equal velocities it carries the ratio $p/\rho$ instead of the pressure across a jump in density. With the coefficients of $w_1$ frozen, as the derivation of eqs. A9 and A10 assumes, the condition reads $p_\mathrm{B} = p_\mathrm{I} + \rho a(u_\mathrm{B} - u_\mathrm{I})$.

## 3. Software architecture

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004) describes no code. [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, §1.3) implemented most of his conditions in his own program for steady and unsteady flows in turbomachinery, whose numerical details a separate report describes. The report itself gives the conditions in analytic form, from which implementations suited to other algorithms can be derived, and lists them in dimensional variables for that purpose (§3.8). The damping zones of [de Val-Borro et al.](#de-val-borro-et-al-2006) (2006, §3.2) are applied as a separate operator after each time step. [Tarr et al.](#tarr-et-al-2024) (2024, §6) implement their boundary as a code of its own, CHAR, which overlaps the boundary layers of the code it serves and exchanges interpolated values with it after each time step (2.6.4). [Mandal et al.](#mandal-et-al-2024) (2024, §2.6) set their inflow through the ghost cells of PLUTO, and use its diode boundaries elsewhere. [Motheau, Almgren and Bell](#motheau-et-al-2017) (2017, §III, §IV.A.3) implement their conditions in CASTRO, built on the framework BoxLib for adaptive meshes, as a filling of the ghost cells at the start of each step, after which the Godunov scheme runs unchanged up to the boundary. Once the convective fluxes are computed, the ghost values are no longer needed, and at walls the ghost cells take the velocity and temperature of the wall for the viscous fluxes (2.6.5).
