# Synthesis of performance in Python

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on how finite-volume codes for hyperbolic problems reach high performance in Python, kept in `reference-works/Performance in Python/Docs`. The collection is gathered around the paper of [Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) (2026, [arXiv:2503.20899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20899)) on AGATE, a code for magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) written in Python (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) (2026, §4.7, Table 2), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Works that the papers cite are cited as the papers cite them. Identifiers are checked against the records of [arXiv](#web-arxiv-api) and [Crossref](#web-crossref). Measured rates are restated with their problem, hardware, precision and number of cores, and are converted into cell updates per second where a source gives what the conversion needs.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

[Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) (2026) wrote the paper at the Heliophysics Division of the NASA Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, with funding from the Living With a Star Strategic Capabilities programme of NASA (acknowledgements). ### 1.2 Aims and scope

Simulations in physics demand ever more computing, and the codes that run on the largest machines are written in languages of low level, C++ or Fortran in computational physics and heliophysics alike ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §1). These languages give performance but bar the average scientist, while Python, among the most popular languages, makes development and debugging easier. Some frameworks for high-performance computing (HPC) offer interfaces in Python, as AMReX does through pyAMReX ([Myers et al.](#myers-et-al-2024) 2024), but the code beneath such an interface stays hard to modify or extend, and the slowness of Python has kept it out of HPC. Three libraries change this: NumPy vectorises operations on arrays ([Harris et al.](#harris-et-al-2020) 2020), Numba generates machine code that runs on several threads ([Lam, Pitrou and Seibert](#lam-et-al-2015) 2015), and CuPy reaches graphics processing units (GPUs) through the CUDA libraries of NVIDIA ([Okuta et al.](#okuta-et-al-2017) 2017). On them AGATE builds a code written entirely in Python. It solves the ideal, Hall and Chew–Goldberger–Low (CGL) equations of MHD by finite volumes, and is meant for production runs, the development of algorithms, prototyping and teaching alike.

The paper concludes that pure Python is impractical for most uses, that NumPy and Numba give useful speed-ups for small simulations on central processing units (CPUs), and that the modes on the GPU run more than 40 times faster than Numba on large problems (§5). It sees AGATE as a viable platform for MHD in research, applications and teaching, whose modular design prepares further physics, methods and geometries.

### 2.1 The interpreter and vectorisation in NumPy

#### 2.1.1 The array as a view on memory

Lists and dictionaries, the data structures of Python, do not suit fast numerical computation, and the NumPy array was developed for it from the mid-1990s ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011, [arXiv:1102.1523](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.1523)). An array is a uniform collection of elements in any number of dimensions, characterised by the type of its elements and by its shape, and underneath it describes one or more blocks of memory. Five attributes do so: the address of its first byte, the type of its elements, its shape, its strides, which give the number of bytes to skip in memory to reach the next element along each axis, and flags, which say whether the array may be modified and whether its memory is contiguous in the row-major order of C or the column-major order of Fortran. Strides let the same memory be seen in different ways without copying it. A slice that takes every second element along both axes is a view with doubled strides, which shares the memory of its parent, so that writing into the view changes the parent. A transpose or a reshape changes only the strides and the shape, and so costs nothing, and even the type of the elements may be reinterpreted in place. The paper names three techniques that make computation with arrays efficient: vectorising calculations, avoiding copies of data in memory, and minimising the number of operations (abstract).

#### 2.1.2 Vectorisation and broadcasting

Loops of Python that apply a simple computation to each element of a large set of data are slow. Grouping the element-wise operations into operations on whole arrays, called vectorisation, lets NumPy perform them in C ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011). A polynomial of second degree evaluated on $10^5$ numbers takes about 500 ms in a loop of Python and 1 ms vectorised. A forward or central divided difference is written with slices offset by one element, as `(y[1:] - y[:-1])/(x[1:] - x[:-1])`, and runs 100 times faster than a loop on 1000 elements. Projecting $10^5$ points through a camera matrix, by the matrix product of NumPy and a division through broadcasting, takes 9 ms, 70 times faster than a loop.

Arrays of different shapes combine by broadcasting. Their dimensions, aligned from the last, match where they are equal or where one of them is 1 or missing, and the result takes the larger size along each. The broadcast arrays are never built, since NumPy reads their elements through strides of zero. The distances $R_{ijk} = (i^2 + j^2 + k^2)^{1/2}$ on a grid of $200^3$ points, computed from three full arrays of coordinates, allocate four named and five temporary arrays of about 64 MB each, some 576 MB in all, and need 48 million operations. Three one-dimensional vectors of coordinates, broadcast against one another, need about 128 MB and 16 million operations, and take 182 ms instead of 410 ms.

#### 2.1.3 Temporaries and the memory bus

As arrays grow, vectorised expressions slow down through the large temporary arrays that they create ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011). The polynomial $x^2 - 3x + 4$ becomes four operations, each of which allocates an array for its result. Most systems of arrays offer no way around these temporaries, but NumPy lets operations run in place, storing their results in an existing array without allocating new memory. Computing $x^2$ into the output array and then subtracting $3x$ and adding 4 in place takes 600 μs instead of 1 ms, almost twice as fast. $3x$ itself is not computed in place, as that would change the input. Cython, which the same issue of the journal describes ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011), Theano ([Bergstra](#bergstra-2010) 2010) and numexpr ([Cooke et al.](#cooke-et-al-numexpr)) lessen the load on the memory bus further, and Cython serves where code cannot easily be vectorised. Vectorisation and broadcasting are no panacea: where repeated operations run over very large blocks of memory, an outer loop over chunks with vectorised inner operations can use the cache better. The paper concludes that, applied skilfully, NumPy spends most of the time of a computation in vectorised operations rather than in loops of Python, and that optimising compilers such as Cython exploit the cache better still.

**[GK]** Since release 1.13, NumPy avoids some temporaries itself on platforms that provide the C function `backtrace`: it turns `d = a + b + c` into `d = a + b; d += c`, which saves memory bandwidth on large arrays ([NumPy 1.13.0 release notes](#web-numpy-113)). The same release defines the results of operations whose input and output overlap in memory, such as shifted views of one array, as if the inputs were copied first, and makes the copies where a heuristic finds them needed. Universal functions also write into a given array through their argument `out`, which avoids even the temporary $3x$ of the example above ([NumPy documentation](#web-numpy-ufuncs)).

#### 2.1.4 Processors starved for data

The speed of processors has grown faster than that of memory, to the point that processors now wait for data most of the time ([Alted](#alted-2010) 2010, [doi:10.1109/MCSE.2010.51](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCSE.2010.51)). In the early 1980s memory was accessed at about 1 MHz, and memory and processors kept pace up to 16 MHz at the end of the decade. In the early 1990s memory levelled off while processors passed 100 MHz, at which a word is consumed every 10 ns, a rate that no memory could sustain, and the first caches on the chip appeared. By 2000 processors reached 1 GHz and waited up to 50 clock ticks for each read or write of memory, which second-level caches of up to 12 MB came to mitigate. When the frequency could no longer rise, processors gained cores, four in the common processors of 2010, and a third level of cache. The latency of memory is now about 150 times the clock step of a processor or more, and its throughput, although it improves faster than its latency, lags about 25 times behind. Memory does not keep up because faster memory needs more layers of wiring, more complex logic and higher frequencies, whose voltage drives up the consumption of energy and the heat, at a cost that few will pay. The caches nearer the processor are faster and smaller, since speed costs energy, and the hierarchy from the first-level cache to the disk now has at least six levels (Fig. 1).

The organisation of memory has therefore become the key factor in optimisation, and the data structures must be adapted to the hierarchy, which programmers must still learn to do at a fairly low level ([Alted](#alted-2010) 2010; [Drepper](#drepper-2007) 2007). Most remedies exploit two principles. Temporal locality reuses data several times over a short period, so that only the first access fetches it from slow memory. Spatial locality reads data sequentially, which the circuits fetch much faster than scattered data and which prefetching brings into the cache before the processor asks for it. The blocking technique exploits both: it moves a contiguous block that fits into the cache, operates on it and reuses it as much as possible, and writes it back to memory (Fig. 2). Simple in principle, it is less so in practice, since the block may be sized for the first, second or third level of cache, or for main memory, and only experiment can tell which is best. Libraries that already block are therefore to be preferred, such as LAPACK for matrices and numexpr ([Cooke et al.](#cooke-et-al-numexpr)), a virtual machine in Python and C that evaluates complex arithmetic expressions on arrays of any size, blocking them and compiling them just in time for a good balance between the cache and the prediction of branches. Compression can relieve the memory bus as well: the library Blosc, a fast lossless compressor that blocks and shuffles the bytes, moves highly compressible data from memory to the processor faster than uncompressed data, though not data that compress poorly (sidebar).

Several cores on one chip share a single memory bus, which starves them further, and keeping their caches coherent lengthens the latency of memory again ([Alted](#alted-2010) 2010). Non-uniform memory access (NUMA) gives each processor, typically each socket, banks of its own, which it reads quickly while it reads those of the others slowly. This raises the throughput where the data stay local to their processes, but makes moving data between processors more costly, so that software that improves the access to memory matters still more on several cores than on one. Graphics processors reach their memory with up to ten times the bandwidth over independent parallel paths, at the price of smaller memories, and programming models such as OpenCL let them work together with CPUs. The paper concludes that the gap will keep widening and more applications will be limited by the access to memory, so that the arrangement of the data, not the code itself, becomes central to the design of programs. **[GK]** The halving of the rate per core of Athena++ on a full node, which its authors ascribe to the bandwidth of memory rather than to arithmetic (`athena-synthesis.md`, 3.9), is this effect.

### 2.2 Compiled kernels

#### 2.2.1 Five modes of one code

AGATE offers five modes of acceleration, None, Numpy, Numba, Cupy and Kernel, which decide which library carries out the numerical work at the lowest level ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §2.3). The first three run on CPUs and the last two on GPUs, through CuPy. The code selects the matching functions when it starts, and all code above them, the algorithms and the organisation of the program (3.1), is the same in every mode. Each class of implementation defines several versions of each of its functions, each serving one or more modes (App. A). The appendix shows them on a loop over one-dimensional arrays, which computes an intermediate value from a sine, sets it to $-1$ where it is negative, and multiplies it by the mean of two input arrays.

1. None runs the loop in pure Python. It iterates over `numba.prange` instead of `range`, which behaves as `range` outside Numba and so keeps the function valid for the next mode.
2. Numba passes the same function to its just-in-time compiler with `nopython=True` and `parallel=True`, so that the iterations of `prange` are shared among the cores.
3. Numpy needs a function of its own without loops, in operations on whole arrays: the sine of an array, the negative values replaced through a boolean mask, and the result assigned into the output array in place.
4. Cupy compiles a loop body written in Python just in time for the GPU, as Numba does for the CPU, but needs a separate function, since the index of each thread on the GPU is computed from the indices of its block and thread, which have no meaning on a CPU.
5. Kernel writes the loop body in CUDA C as a string, which CuPy compiles and calls as a raw kernel.

Each kernel thus exists in four versions: a loop shared by None and Numba, a vectorised function, a loop for CuPy and a kernel in CUDA C. CuPy also replaces the functions of NumPy on the GPU directly, but preliminary tests found this inferior to the two GPU modes, so AGATE does not offer it. Numba compiles for GPUs as well, which AGATE supports, but CuPy gave consistently faster kernels for effectively the same code, so CuPy is preferred on GPUs.

#### 2.2.2 Cython

Python owes its place in science to its readable and concise code and to its interface to C, through which existing code in C, C++ and Fortran is reused and critical sections are written in C ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011, [doi:10.1109/MCSE.2010.118](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCSE.2010.118)). Computational loops written in Python are slow, largely because the language is so dynamic, and while NumPy removes many of them, some computations are expressed well only as loops. Cython, which grew out of Pyrex by Greg Ewing, extends Python with declarations of C types and compiles the result to C, which is compiled and linked against Python and called from the interpreter. With the declarations, attributes and calls are resolved at compile time, the generated code avoids most of the costs of the run time, and the C compiler applies all its optimisations. Whatever cannot be determined statically keeps the semantics of Python, so that only the parts that dominate the run time need annotations, since 80 per cent of the run time is spent in 20 per cent of the code. The compiler turns idioms of Python into specialised C: a loop over `range` into a loop of C where the range and the type of its variable allow it, `enumerate` into a counter, a chain of comparisons of an integer into a `switch`, and classes declared with `cdef` into structures of C with tables of virtual functions, which serve data that do not fit arrays, such as trees and graphs.

A numerical integral of the Gamma function of the GNU Scientific Library, a loop with typed variables, runs 30 times faster than the same loop in Python, and as fast as the equivalent NumPy expression but with much less memory ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011, Figs. 1 and 2). For expressions on whole arrays, such as $v = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{1/2}$, NumPy is usually fast enough, with two exceptions. On very small arrays evaluated many times, the overhead of Python in each call of NumPy dominates. On very large arrays, NumPy needs large temporaries and moves the intermediate results repeatedly over the memory bus, which in most scientific settings is the bottleneck rather than the processor. A loop in Cython over typed NumPy arrays computes the expression without temporaries and runs about ten times faster on large arrays (Fig. 5). Typed callbacks for solvers of equations and for optimisers cut the overhead of their calls, by a factor of 40 for an ordinary differential equation in 12 variables. Data that do not form rectangular arrays, such as the coefficients $a_{lm}$ of a spherical harmonic transform, stored in one dimension at position $l^2 + l + m$, are handled by plain loops. Their variance per $l$, up to $l_\mathrm{max} = 1500$, runs more than 1000 times faster in Cython than in Python, and 27 times faster than a loop of Python over calls of NumPy.

Cython calls C and C++ natively, and Fortran through Fwrap, a separate tool that wraps Fortran 77 to 2003 through the interoperability of Fortran 2003 with C ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011). Arrays passed to Fortran are laid out in column-major order, by `np.asfortranarray` or `order='F'`, so that no copy is needed, and arguments of intent out are modified in place. Compared with pure Python, the disadvantages of Cython are the time of compilation and a separate build phase, so that projects mix modules of Python and of Cython. An experimental pure mode declares the types through decorators that the interpreter ignores. Compared with C and Fortran, its main limit is parallelism in shared memory. Sections declared `nogil` contain only C and may run in parallel, which is tedious, and OpenMP is not yet supported, while parallelism by message passing between processes, as with MPI, is well supported. Compared with C++, it lacks templates, so that code must be repeated for each type of data or generated by a template engine. Cython pays where the time goes into low-level arithmetic in the code itself rather than in libraries, and where the computation does not express well in operations of NumPy, as in sequential algorithms.

**[GK]** The present documentation of Cython describes parallel loops through `cython.parallel.prange`, run by OpenMP, which flags of the compiler such as `-fopenmp` enable, and whose body has had to release the GIL. From Cython 3.3 the body may hold the GIL, which runs in parallel only on free-threaded builds of Python ([Cython documentation, parallelism](#web-cython-parallelism)). From Cython 3.1, a module declares with the directive `freethreading_compatible = True` that it may run without the GIL. A module without it re-enables the GIL when a free-threaded interpreter imports it, and the support is called experimental ([Cython documentation, free threading](#web-cython-freethreading)).

#### 2.2.4 Pythran

A scientific application in Python costs from twice the time of its equivalent in C, where the Python only calls routines of C, to a hundred times or more, where it accesses arrays element by element ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, [doi:10.1088/1749-4680/8/1/014001](https://doi.org/10.1088/1749-4680/8/1/014001), §1). The cost comes from interpretation, from the dynamic binding of names, and from the absence of any optimisation of the byte code. As scientific applications are said to spend 90 per cent of their time in 10 per cent of the code, a compiler need optimise only a part of an application. Two families of tools close the gap. Static translators turn Python into C or C++: Cython through the Python/C API, Nuitka likewise into C++, and ShedSkin into C++ without that API for implicitly typed programs, failing on dynamic ones. Just-in-time compilers turn the costly parts into native code while the program runs: numexpr for expressions of NumPy given as strings, Numba and Parakeet for code centred on NumPy, and PyPy for the whole language. Apart from PyPy, none applies the static optimisations that compilers of C have used for decades, and those that translate to C leave them to the C compiler, which can do little with the generated code: GCC hardly vectorises the output of Cython, although the semantics of the arrays are known at the level of Python.

Pythran compiles a module written in a subset of Python 2.7 into parametric C++ ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §1, §2). It supports comprehensions, generator expressions, lambda functions, nested and polymorphic functions and global variables, but not classes of the user or dynamic features such as introspection and variables of changing type. Unlike Cython, its input stays valid Python, which runs unchanged in the interpreter. Comments such as `# pythran export foo(int list, float)` give the signatures of the functions that leave the module, and the types of all other functions are inferred from their calls. A front end turns the module into the abstract syntax tree of Python and then into a simpler intermediate representation, a subset of that tree. A middle end analyses and optimises it, and a back end turns it into C++ templates, instantiates them for the exported signatures and compiles them. The generated code needs a compiler of C++11, as g++ 4.8.2 or clang++ 3.3, Boost.Python for the bindings, and the template library pythonic, which emulates the standard library of Python and NumPy on top of the Standard Template Library of C++, GMP for long integers, and the Numerical Template Toolbox (NT2) for vectors.

The analyses are type-agnostic (§3). An alias analysis finds which locations a name may denote, so that `sum` is known to be the built-in. Purity analyses find functions without global effects and without changes to their arguments, from which follow maps that may run in parallel and expressions that are constant. Chains of use and definition remove dead code and rename variables whose type changes, which C++ could not hold. The optimisations at the level of Python turn generator expressions and `map`, `zip` and `filter` into lazy iterators where this is valid, which removes temporary lists, fold constant expressions at compile time, and unroll loops over lists of known length, while inlining is left to the C++ compiler (§4). Expressions of NumPy receive three optimisations at the level of the library (§5). Expression templates fuse their universal functions into a single loop without intermediate arrays, which also raises the arithmetic per element. The fused loop is vectorised with the instructions for vectors of the processor through boost.simd of NT2. And it is parallelised through OpenMP, as such loops carry no dependence. Loops written by the user are parallelised explicitly by directives of OpenMP placed in comments, such as `#omp parallel for` (§6). Parallelising outer loops is often better than relying on the inner expressions of NumPy, as it avoids a barrier at the end of each expression and gives each thread more work. A computation of a Julia set parallelised so on its outer loop reaches the perfect speed-up of 4 on four cores.

**[GK]** The present documentation of Pythran, version 0.19.0, gives Python 3.7 and later as supported, the support of Python 2.7 having ended with version 0.9.5, and gives instructions for Linux and macOS, with Windows in progress ([Pythran documentation](#web-pythran-docs)).

#### 2.2.5 Designing a code with Numba

Libraries such as NumPy and SciPy escape the GIL, which restricts the interpreter of CPython to one thread, by running their work in compiled extensions, so that the GIL limits performance only where a program passes control back and forth between the interpreter and compiled code ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, [arXiv:2303.08394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08394), pp. 1–2). This happens where no compiled implementation of the algorithm exists, or where the interpreter must organise much data to feed one. Numba promises to remove the need for a compiled language there. It compiles a subset of Python that works on NumPy arrays through the compiler infrastructure LLVM, which generates machine code for CPUs and GPUs, vectorises it automatically where the hardware allows, and may run it on several threads outside the GIL, while the metadata of the arrays on their dimensions, type and layout let the code use the caches well (p. 2). A function marked by a decorator is compiled when it is first called, for the types of its arguments, and the result is cached for later calls with the same types (Fig. 1). The bytecode is analysed into an intermediate representation, the types are inferred from those of the inputs, and LLVM generates the machine code. Each call unboxes its Python objects into native types and boxes its results again, without copying memory, but at a cost per call.

The authors test the promise of Numba on PyExaFMM, a multithreaded implementation of the kernel-independent fast multipole method in three dimensions, an algorithm built on an octree that needs much organisation of data and careful allocation of memory ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, pp. 2, 4–6). Its pitfalls are several (p. 3). Where Numba finds no type of its own for a Python object, or meets an unsupported feature, it compiles in object mode, which is no faster than Python and happens silently unless the mode without Python is demanded. Three ways of writing the same steps, two products of random matrices whose rows are stored in a dictionary typed for Numba, run at different speeds, 29.1 ms against 25.7 ms for matrices of $1000 \times 1000$, apparently depending on whether the dictionary is created inside or outside the compiled functions (Table 1, Listing 2). The optimisations that cause such differences are opaque to the user, and a typed dictionary used from the interpreter is always slower than a dictionary of Python because of the boxing. Numba is therefore closer to a framework of programming than to a mere compiler, with a learning curve of its own: performance demands familiarity with its internals and may change the design of algorithms and data structures substantially. The measurements were made on an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3970X of 32 cores, with Python 3.8.5 and Numba 0.53.0.

The authors conclude that optimal multithreaded performance with Numba needs careful consideration of the algorithm, of the implementation of Numba and of a data-oriented design, and that the expertise required may exceed that of the audience at which Numba aims ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, pp. 8–9). Numba nevertheless transforms projects that value the expressiveness of Python, simple builds on all platforms and its ecosystem, and that have a few isolated bottlenecks. PyExaFMM stays concise, at 4901 lines, deploys on all platforms through Conda and the usual channels of Python, and needs no separate interface to a compiled library.

### 2.3 Measured comparisons of the routes on the same kernels

#### 2.3.1 The modes of AGATE on the Orszag–Tang vortex

AGATE times its modes on the Orszag–Tang vortex in ideal MHD with divergence cleaning, run to $t = 0.5$ with the default algorithm on grids of $32^2$ to $2048^2$ cells ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §4.7, Table 2). The vortex fills $[0, 1]^2$ with periodic boundaries, starting from $\rho = 25/(36\pi)$, $p = 5/(12\pi)$, $v_x = -\sin(2\pi y)$, $v_y = \sin(2\pi x)$, $B_x = -B_0 \sin(2\pi y)$ and $B_y = B_0 \sin(4\pi x)$, with $B_0 = 1/\sqrt{4\pi}$ (§4.2). The machine holds two AMD EPYC processors of 64 cores each, with two threads per core, and an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Numba runs on 16 threads, the number that performed best in preliminary tests on $256^2$ cells. Pure Python is impractical, and NumPy is about 100 times faster on $128^2$ cells, where pure Python takes 1802 s and NumPy 17.6 s. Numba compiles for about 13 s at the start of each run, and is 2 to 5 times faster than NumPy on large grids: on $1024^2$ cells, NumPy takes 7477 s and Numba 1320 s. The modes on the GPU run more than 40 times faster than Numba and more than 300 times faster than NumPy on the larger grids. The Kernel mode takes 12.0 s on $1024^2$ cells and 87.1 s on $2048^2$, where NumPy and Numba were not run, as they would have taken too long.

**[GK]** From $512^2$ to $1024^2$ cells, the times of NumPy grow by a factor of 8.4, close to the factor of 8 by which the work grows in two dimensions, 4 in cells and 2 in steps. Those of Numba grow by only 5.3 and those of the Kernel mode by 3.9, so that both parallel modes use their hardware better on larger grids. The time of Numba on $32^2$ cells, 13.3 s, shows that its times include the compilation. The paper gives neither the number of steps nor the precision, so that its times cannot be converted into cell updates per second. The examples of its appendix compute in double precision.

#### 2.3.2 Pythran against CPython, Cython, PyPy and numexpr

The expression $d = (b^2 + c^2)^{1/2}$ of NumPy, timed on a quad-core i7 with hyper-threading, shows the three optimisations of Pythran in turn ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §5). NumPy takes 1.23 ms. Expression templates, which replace four temporary arrays and four loops by one allocation and one loop, bring it to 621 μs, vectorisation to 418 μs, and OpenMP on the four cores to 105 μs, about eleven times faster than NumPy. Vectorisation falls short of the factor of four that four numbers of double precision per AVX register promise, because of the unaligned transfers between memory and the registers. numexpr takes 395 μs, almost four times as long as Pythran. The paper does not give the length of the arrays.

Four benchmarks compare CPython 2.7.3, Cython 0.20, PyPy 2.0.2 and Pythran on the same machine, without any annotation of parallelism, each the best of repeated runs of `timeit` and compiled by g++ 4.8.2, mostly with `-O3` ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §7, Tables 1 to 4). The Cython versions needed manual conversion, with checks of bounds and negative indices off, C types for all variables and the mathematical functions of libm.

- NQueens, from the project Unladen Swallow, uses generator expressions and sets of integers heavily: 181 ms in CPython, 42.3 ms in Cython, 51.3 ms in PyPy and 22.5 ms in Pythran, eight times faster than CPython. Its lazy evaluation avoids many allocations, but more than half of its time still goes into allocating and freeing a set in the inner loop.
- rosen_der, a complex expression of NumPy on one-dimensional slices: 22.3 ms in CPython, 3.39 ms in Cython, 3.81 ms in Pythran and 51.3 ms in PyPy, whose support of NumPy was not yet mature. Cython is slightly faster, but only after its code was rewritten by hand as an explicit loop, and the automatic parallelisation of Pythran gains a further factor of four without any change to the input.
- growcut, image processing with explicit indexing of every element: 398 ms in CPython, 991 μs in Cython, 526 μs in Pythran and 5.54 ms in PyPy. All compilers gain hugely, as single elements are extremely slow to access in NumPy, and Pythran gains most, probably through cleaner code.
- blackscholes, with a list instead of an array as input, measures the cost of converting Python objects into dense native data: 490 μs in CPython, 74.7 μs in Cython, 34.8 μs in Pythran and 37.3 μs in PyPy, which needs no such conversion.

**[GK]** PyPy takes 51.3 ms in both NQueens and rosen_der, a coincidence that the speed-ups of the two tables, 3.52 and 0.43, are consistent with, so that it cannot be checked further.

### 2.4 Threads, the global interpreter lock and free threading

#### 2.4.1 Threads in Numba

Numba runs loops on several threads through `prange` in functions compiled with `parallel=True`, a syntax reminiscent of OpenMP ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, p. 7, Listing 3). Its threads come from a threading layer, OpenMP or Intel's Threading Building Blocks (TBB), chosen through the environment variable `NUMBA_THREADING_LAYER`. PyExaFMM takes OpenMP, which suits loops whose iterations carry similar work. Operations of NumPy inside compiled functions keep the threads of the libraries that implement them, such as OpenBLAS or the Math Kernel Library of Intel, so that a parallel region of Numba that calls them nests parallelism within parallelism. The threads then outnumber the cores, which leaves threads idle, breaks the locality of the caches and may make threads hang while they wait for one another. PyExaFMM therefore sets `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1` before it starts, so that only the threads of Numba run.

Each operator of PyExaFMM is parallelised over the nodes of the tree that it updates ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, pp. 7–8). For one operator, copying the data of each target into vectors that lie together in memory before the parallel loop makes it 13 per cent faster, on 32,768 leaves of at most 150 particles each with an expansion of order 10, about $10^6$ particles on the surface of a sphere. For the operators with long lists of interactions, such copies would take about 17 GB and cost more time to allocate than they save, so these loop over the targets and look up their data in place. On the same tree with an expansion of order 6, the whole algorithm takes $5.95 \pm 0.02$ s, after $9.00 \pm 0.01$ s of precomputation. The time spent outside the compiled code, in boxing, in organising inputs and in passing control between Numba and Python, stays below 5 per cent of each operator except one, whose organisation of data must run in the interpreter under the GIL, so that the code runs on all cores nearly all the time (Fig. 3).

### 2.7 Whole codes for fluids against compiled codes

#### 2.7.1 AGATE against Athena++ and Cholla

The same benchmark runs in Athena++ ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020) on the CPUs and in Cholla ([Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2015) 2015; [Caddy and Schneider](#caddy-schneider-2024) 2024) on the GPU, each built as its repository instructs ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §4.7, Table 2). Athena++ runs on one node, once without OpenMP and once with 16 threads, for which the mesh blocks are sized so that each thread has one. Cholla requires three dimensions, so it runs on $N \times N \times 3$ cells, and the Kernel mode of AGATE is timed on the same grid. Athena++ is considerably better optimised for CPUs than AGATE, which the authors expect, since they focus on GPUs. On $1024^2$ cells, Athena++ takes 1396 s without threads, about as long as Numba on 16 threads, and 214 s with them, against 7477 s for NumPy. On the GPU, Cholla runs 10 to 15 per cent faster than AGATE on the largest grids and about as fast on smaller ones, a difference that the authors accept for the advantages of Python. AGATE needs no compilation beyond the 13 s of Numba and well under a second on the GPU, where Athena++ and Cholla took 81 s and 119 s to compile. The authors find AGATE suited above all to rapid deployment, to students and to prototyping on smaller problems, and to HPC through its GPU modes.

**[GK]** The times of Athena++ without threads grow by a factor of 7.9 from $512^2$ to $1024^2$ cells, as the work does, and exceed those with 16 threads by a factor of 6.5, which suggests that it ran on one core. On $1024^2$ cells, NumPy is then 5.4 times slower than Athena++ on one core, and Numba on 16 threads 6.2 times slower than Athena++ on 16 threads.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture records how the codes divide the work between Python and their kernels, lay out their data, choose their physics and are installed. 3.1 digests AGATE, 3.2 the NumPy array, 3.3 Cython, 3.5 Pythran and 3.6 PyExaFMM.

### 3.1 AGATE: interfaces and implementations

AGATE builds its solver from Python classes, each step of the solution an interface paired with implementations ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §2.1, Fig. 1). The interfaces manage the operations of high level and shield the main program from the numerical detail, which the implementations carry out. A user may select or write any implementation that meets its interface. The code solves $\partial U/\partial t = \Delta(U, t)$, where $\Delta$ is the instantaneous rate of change of the state vector $U$, through the following classes.

- `Grid` holds the locations, sizes and connections of the cells. Only a uniform Cartesian grid exists so far.
- `Vector` stores $U$ on the grid, and `State` extends it for MHD with references to the single variables and storage for quantities outside the state, such as the ratio of specific heats.
- `Evolver` advances the state in time, by Runge–Kutta schemes from RK11, of one stage and first order, and RK22 to the strong-stability-preserving RK33, RK43 and RK53. It computes $\Delta$ through the interface `Scheme`, whose implementations are ideal, Hall and CGL MHD.
- `Scenario` sets the initial conditions and boundaries of a problem and creates the matching `State` on a `Grid`.
- `Hacker` runs tasks of the user when conditions are met during a run, from analysis on the fly to changes of the run. The test of reconnection (§4.6) uses one to compute the rate of reconnection without output files.
- `Roller` drives the loop in time from $t_0$ to $t_1$. Its functions of automatic initialisation build all classes of the algorithm from defaults and options, while every component can also be set by hand.

The default scheme is a Godunov-type finite-volume method in the form of eq. 1, with a global time step $\Delta t = C\,\Delta x_\mathrm{min}/v_\mathrm{max}$, whose Courant number defaults to 0.8, 0.45 and 0.325 in one, two and three dimensions (§2.2). Its steps are interfaces of their own: `Reconstructor`, `Speeder` for the wave speeds, `Fluxor` for the physical fluxes, `Riemann`, and `Sourceror` for the source terms. By default the conserved variables are reconstructed linearly with the monotonised central limiter, $\Delta U_i = \mathrm{minmod}[\beta(U_{i+1} - U_i), \beta(U_i - U_{i-1}), (U_{i+1} - U_{i-1})/2]$ with $\beta = 1.3$, and the HLL solver uses the signal speeds $s_\mathrm{L} = \min(v_\mathrm{L} - c_\mathrm{L}, v_\mathrm{R} - c_\mathrm{R})$ and $s_\mathrm{R} = \max(v_\mathrm{L} + c_\mathrm{L}, v_\mathrm{R} + c_\mathrm{R})$ (eqs. 2 to 6). Errors in the divergence of the magnetic field are cleaned by the generalised Lagrange multiplier of [Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2002) (2002), with $c_h$ at 95 per cent of the largest local wave speed and $c_p^2/c_h = 0.18$ (§3.1). The modes of acceleration enter below the interfaces (2.2.1), so that physics and algorithm, chosen by composing objects at run time, are chosen independently of the hardware. The tests reproduce the shock tube of Brio and Wu, the Orszag–Tang vortex, whistler waves with second-order convergence, magnetosonic waves and the firehose instability in CGL MHD, and the reconnection challenge of the Geospace Environmental Modeling (GEM) project (§4.1 to §4.6). The paper names NumPy, Numba, CuPy and Matplotlib as the software that it uses (acknowledgements), but does not describe how AGATE is installed.

### 3.2 NumPy: memory shared with other code

The NumPy array shares memory with other code without copying it ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011). A memory-mapped array addresses an array stored on disk through the interface of any other array, so that a small part of a very large file can be read or changed, and the changes are written back by flushing. Memory allocated by foreign code, such as a library in C++ or Fortran, becomes an array through the array interface, an attribute `__array_interface__` that holds a dictionary with the address of the data, the shape and the type of the elements. An example exposes a buffer allocated through ctypes and modifies it through NumPy. Structured data types combine fields of different types in one element, such as a time stamp of 64 bits and a position of two floating-point numbers, whose fields are then addressed by name, and they read the records of a binary file in one line. **[GK]** A structured array keeps the fields of each element together in memory, the layout in which topic 32 has the variables of a cell lie together, whereas an array per variable, or the variable on the first axis, keeps each field contiguous.

### 3.3 Cython: building and distributing compiled modules

Cython generates C, which a C compiler builds into an extension module ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011). Projects mostly mix modules of Python and of Cython, since the modules of Cython need not be recompiled when the Python changes. To avoid requiring Cython as a dependency, many projects ship the generated C files, which compile unchanged against Python 2.3 to 3.2 when the package is set up by distutils. Fwrap can likewise generate a deployable package for machines without Fwrap or Cython. Wrappers written directly in the Python/C API, with its steep learning curve, have drawn a sharp line between users in Python and developers in C, which Cython lowers. The extent of such coupling shows in SciPy, whose more than 200,000 lines of C++, 60,000 of C and 75,000 of Fortran faced about 70,000 lines of Python.

### 3.5 Pythran: modules that stay Python

Pythran turns a module, not a whole program, into a native module, so that the costly parts of a program are moved into modules of their own and compiled ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §2). The generated C++ can also be used from applications in C++. Pythran extends distutils with a class `PythranExtension`, used like any extension, and since its input is Python, a `setup.py` can fall back to the module in pure Python where Pythran is not available (§8). Pythran ran on Linux and Mac OS X and was distributed through its repository and the Python Package Index. **[GK]** SciPy builds some of its modules with Pythran, which brings along the library xsimd for vector instructions ([build file of SciPy 1.16.3](#web-scipy-meson)).

### 3.6 PyExaFMM: a data-oriented design around Numba

Data-oriented design favours data structures with simple layouts in memory, such as arrays, which make locality of the caches and parallelisation easier to achieve than objects that bundle attributes of several types ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, p. 6). Numba centres on arrays, and PyExaFMM follows it. Its octree is linear rather than built of pointers: each node is a unique identifier in a one-dimensional vector, and all other data, the coefficients of the expansions, the particles and the potentials, lie in further one-dimensional vectors, reached through indices that tie each identifier to its data. The authors note that Numba thus shaped their design and made the software more complex, although its data structures became simpler. A single Python object, `Fmm`, forms the interface (Fig. 2). It allocates the arrays of expansions and potentials, and its methods call the compiled functions of the operators and of their data, while trees and precomputed quantities are kept in a database in HDF5. The operators stay separate functions, so that each can be tested on its own, at the price of at least one passage between Numba and Python per call of an operator. A single compiled routine that returned to Python only once would be faster, but would give up modularity and unit tests. This structure parallels the classic design of a Python interface over a compiled language, with the difference that all of it is written in Python, although performant code for Numba may be Pythonic only in its syntax.
