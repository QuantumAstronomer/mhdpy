This synthesis digests the text by Michael Zingale in its version of 26 January 2025, kept as `References/Computational Hydrodynamics Book/CompHydroTutorial.pdf`. Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the text or check its claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. ## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin, version and licence

The text grew out of notes for students who begin research projects in computational astrophysics at Stony Brook University (preface). It covers the methods in common use for the hydrodynamics of astrophysical flows, at the level of upper-level undergraduates. Exercises within the text illustrate the central ideas. Source code is available for every method and every figure, since running the methods is held to be the best way to understand them. The text calls itself a work in progress: chapters are added over time, and revisions follow at irregular intervals, usually for a new student or a course.

The title page dates the version summarised here to 26 January 2025. The copyright notice covers the years 2013 to 2017. The text is part of the Open Astrophysics Bookshelf. Its LaTeX source is kept on GitHub in Open-Astrophysics-Bookshelf/numerical_exercises, and a PDF built from it is published at open-astrophysics-bookshelf.github.io/numerical_exercises/CompHydroTutorial.pdf. **[GK]** The published PDF is rebuilt on every push to the main branch, so that it always shows the latest version. As of 27 October 2026 no commit has followed the version summarised here, so the published PDF and the local copy agree. The README of the repository gives the title as *Computational Hydrodynamics for Astrophysics*.

The text is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). Zingale, of Stony Brook University, is its primary author. Ian Hawke (Southampton) contributed the material on WENO schemes, which the text treats in §5.5, §6.4 and §8.12. The preface traces the treatment of projection methods to an ongoing collaboration with Ann Almgren, John Bell, Andy Nonaka and Weiqun Zhang.

### 1.2 Scope and structure

The text runs to 266 printed pages in six parts, with 15 chapters and three appendices, followed by a bibliography of 88 entries. Its front matter of 22 pages holds the table of contents, lists of the figures and of the 53 exercises, the preface, a table of symbols and a list of contributors. The six parts cover the following.

- **Part I, Basics** (ch. 1 to 3). What a simulation is, and the numerical basics: sources of error, differentiation and integration, root finding, norms, the integration of ODEs and FFTs (ch. 1). The classification of PDEs as hyperbolic, elliptic or parabolic (ch. 2). Finite-volume grids, with differences and their order of accuracy, conservation, boundary conditions through ghost cells, and details of implementation (ch. 3).
- **Part II, Advection and Hydrodynamics** (ch. 4 to 8), nearly half of the text. Linear advection by first-order finite differences, with stability, implicit methods, Eulerian and Lagrangian frames, and convergence (ch. 4). Second- and higher-order finite-volume advection, with limiting, the reconstruct-evolve-average procedure, the method of lines, split and unsplit multi-dimensional schemes, and high-order finite differences with WENO reconstruction (ch. 5). Burgers' equation as a nonlinear model with shocks and rarefactions, also solved with WENO and flux splitting (ch. 6). The theory of the Euler equations: their eigenstructure, the exact solution of the Riemann problem, and alternative forms of the energy equation (ch. 7). The numerical methods for them (ch. 8). Interface states are reconstructed with piecewise constant, linear or parabolic profiles, with flattening and limiting on characteristic variables, and passed to Riemann solvers for the conservative update, with artificial viscosity. Further sections treat boundary conditions, multi-dimensional schemes including unsplit ones in three dimensions, source terms, simple geometries, test problems, method-of-lines integration, temperature and general equations of state, and WENO.
- **Part III, Elliptic and Parabolic Problems** (ch. 9 and 10). Elliptic equations, solved by Fourier methods, relaxation and multigrid (ch. 9). Diffusion, by explicit and implicit differencing and by multigrid in several dimensions, with non-constant conductivity and within hydrodynamics (ch. 10).
- **Part IV, Multiphysics Applications** (ch. 11 to 13). The coupling of physical processes, shown on diffusion-reaction and advection-diffusion problems, with a convergence test that needs no analytic solution (ch. 11). Reactive flow by operator splitting, with species, reaction networks and the burning modes of convective burning, deflagration and detonation (ch. 12). The planning of a simulation, from dimensionality and resolution to boundary conditions, timestep, convergence, computational cost and I/O (ch. 13).
- **Part V, Low Speed Hydrodynamics** (ch. 14 and 15). Incompressible flow by a cell-centred approximate projection method (ch. 14). Low Mach number methods, with their divergence constraints, multigrid for variable-density flows, atmospheric flows and combustion (ch. 15).
- **Part VI, Code Descriptions** (appendices A to C). The three companion codes, described below.

Magnetic fields are absent throughout: MHD occurs only in the title of a cited paper. Radiation transport is not treated, beyond mentions in the descriptions of the companion codes (preface, appendix A), and radiative cooling does not occur. Thermal conduction enters as diffusion in the energy equation (§10.6). Gravity enters as a source term (§8.7), through hydrostatic boundaries (§8.5) and in the atmospheric flows of §15.3. The gravitational potential appears only as an example of the Poisson equation (§2.3, §9.1, §9.2). Adaptive mesh refinement, mapped grids, domain decomposition on parallel computers and moving meshes built from a Voronoi tessellation are named only as directions for further study (§3.5). Smoothed particle hydrodynamics is named only as an alternative to grids (§3.1).

### 1.4 Notation

A table of symbols precedes the text and gives the meaning and units of each symbol (table 1). Vectors and matrices are set in bold. The conserved variables form the vector $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$, the primitive variables $\mathbf{q}$ and the characteristic variables $\mathbf{w}$, and $\mathbf{F}$ is the flux vector. The Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{A}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda$, collected in the diagonal matrix $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$, and left and right eigenvectors $\mathbf{l}$ and $\mathbf{r}$, collected in the matrices $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{R}$. $\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{q}_L, \mathbf{q}_R)$ denotes the Riemann problem between a left and a right state. The velocity is $\mathbf{U}$, with components $u$ and $v$ in two dimensions, set upright as against the calligraphic $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$ of the conserved variables.

Thermodynamic quantities are specific, that is per unit mass: $e$ is the internal energy, $E$ the total energy, $h$ the enthalpy and $s$ the entropy, and $\tau = 1/\rho$ is the specific volume. Two adiabatic indices are kept apart. The first adiabatic exponent is $\Gamma_1 = (\partial \log p / \partial \log \rho)_s$, and the ratio of specific heats is $\gamma = c_p / c_v$, with $c_p = (\partial h / \partial T)_{p, X_k}$ and $c_v = (\partial e / \partial T)_{\rho, X_k}$. The quantity $\gamma_e = p/(\rho e) + 1$ links pressure and internal energy. The sound speed is $c = \sqrt{\Gamma_1 p / \rho}$, and the Lagrangian sound speed is $C = \sqrt{\Gamma_1 p \rho}$. $\mathcal{C}$ is the CFL number, and $M = |\mathbf{U}| / c$ the Mach number. Species enter through their mass fractions $X_k$, which sum to one, and their creation rates $\dot{\omega}_k$. The remaining symbols are the gravitational acceleration $\mathbf{g}$, the heat sources $H$ and the nuclear energy source $H_\mathrm{nuc}$, the thermal conductivity $k_\mathrm{th}$, the source term $S$ of the divergence constraint, and $\mathcal{I}$, the integral under a piecewise parabolic reconstruction. All units are those of the cgs system, except two that the table gives in metres: m s$^{-1}$ for the sound speed and g m$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$ for the Lagrangian sound speed. **[GK]** The dimensions of $\sqrt{\Gamma_1 p / \rho}$ and $\sqrt{\Gamma_1 p \rho}$ show that centimetres are meant in both.

## 2. Numerical methods

Ten subjects group the numerical methods, each drawn from one or two consecutive chapters of the text. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

### 2.1 Numerical foundations

Chapter 1 reviews the numerical basics that later chapters assume, and refers to standard texts for depth: Garcia (1999), Newman (2012) and Pang (2006). Chapter 2 introduces the three classes of partial differential equations.

#### 2.1.1 Simulation and its approximations

Simulation stands in for the experiments that astronomy cannot perform: a model system is built and probed under a range of conditions (§1.1). Every simulation rests on approximations, starting with the choice of equations. The central one here is the fluid approximation. It describes the bulk properties of fluid elements, which are much larger than the mean free path of the particles yet much smaller than the system (fig. 1.1). Further approximations concern the physics included and the discretisation, which turns a continuous system of PDEs into a finite representation in memory.

The output of a code is therefore tested against problems with known solutions and against physical intuition, and never trusted blindly. The systems are strongly nonlinear, so small changes in the code or its environment, such as the compiler or its optimisation, can change the numbers considerably. Trends thus matter more than precise values, and parameter and convergence studies show the limits of a simulation. No code is best for everything, since every algorithm has its approximations and limits. Comparisons between codes build confidence, as for disc-planet interaction (arXiv:astro-ph/0605237), turbulent Rayleigh-Taylor mixing (doi:10.1063/1.1688328) and the Santa Barbara cluster (arXiv:astro-ph/9906160). Understanding a code requires understanding its methods, which are best learned by implementing them.

#### 2.1.2 Round-off and truncation error

Two kinds of error affect every algorithm (§1.2.1). Round-off error comes from representing numbers with a finite number of bits, for which the text refers to Goldberg (1991, doi:10.1145/103162.103163). Machine epsilon is the last value of $\epsilon$, halved repeatedly from one, for which $1 + \epsilon$ still differs from one in floating point. It differs between single and double precision. Rearranging an expression can reduce round-off, as in $x^3 - y^3 = (x - y)(x^2 + xy + y^2)$ (eq. 1.1), which the text presents as avoiding the subtraction of two very large numbers. Round-off can be reduced in this way, but never removed.

Truncation error belongs to the algorithm. An operator or function is approximated by a series in a small quantity, and the terms dropped form the error. For $|x| \ll 1$, the approximation $\sin x \approx x - x^3/6$ (eq. 1.3) leaves an error whose leading term scales as $x^5$; it is $\mathcal{O}(x^5)$, or fifth-order accurate. Measuring how the error falls as the small quantity shrinks, here by a factor of $2^5$ each time $x$ is halved, is a convergence test.

**[GK]** Halving from one yields $2^{-52} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ in double and $2^{-23} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$ in single precision. These are the spacings of floating-point numbers just above one, for the precisions $p = 53$ and $p = 24$ of IEEE 754 (Goldberg 1991, table 1). Goldberg calls half this value machine epsilon, as the bound on the relative error of rounding (Goldberg 1991, §1.2), so the term differs by a factor of two between conventions. The loss in a subtraction stems from operands that are close, not large: their leading digits cancel and expose the rounding errors they carry. A rearrangement such as eq. 1.1 replaces this catastrophic cancellation by a benign one between exact operands (Goldberg 1991, §1.4).

#### 2.1.3 Differentiation

Derivatives are needed either from values $a_i$ at equally spaced points $x_i$ with spacing $\Delta x$, the case that matters for the text, or from a function known analytically (§1.2.2). The one-sided differences $(a_i - a_{i-1})/\Delta x$ and $(a_{i+1} - a_i)/\Delta x$ (eqs. 1.4 and 1.5) follow from Taylor expansion about $x_i$ (eq. 1.6). They are first-order accurate: their truncation error is $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x)$, with a leading term of magnitude $\tfrac{1}{2} \Delta x\, |\partial^2 a/\partial x^2|$ at $x_i$ (eqs. 1.7 and 1.8). The centred difference $(a_{i+1} - a_{i-1})/(2 \Delta x)$ is second-order accurate, with an error $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$. Higher order generally brings a smaller error at the cost of a wider range of points (fig. 1.2), and second and higher derivatives follow from the same expansions.

For a function known analytically, the spacing $\delta x$ of a difference such as $[f(x_0 + \delta x) - f(x_0)]/\delta x$ (eq. 1.9) can be chosen. It balances truncation error, which falls with $\delta x$, against round-off error, which grows as $\delta x$ approaches machine epsilon (fig. 1.3). The balance is discussed by Yakowitz and Szidarovszky (1989), and $\delta x \approx \sqrt{\epsilon}$ is a good rule of thumb. Estimates for $\delta x$ and $\delta x/2$ together give an error estimate and, combined, a better result, which is the basis of Richardson extrapolation.

**[GK]** Eq. 1.7 does not follow from eq. 1.6 as printed. Solving eq. 1.6 for the derivative gives the right-sided difference, $\partial a/\partial x|_i = (a_{i+1} - a_i)/\Delta x - \tfrac{1}{2} \Delta x\, \partial^2 a/\partial x^2|_i + \ldots$, whereas eq. 1.7 pairs that error term with the left-sided difference. Expanding $a_{i-1}$ instead gives the left-sided difference, with an error term of opposite sign. The order of accuracy is unaffected.

#### 2.1.4 Integration

A quadrature rule approximates an integral by a weighted sum of function values (§1.2.2). On $[a, b]$ with $N$ equal intervals of width $\Delta x = (b - a)/N$, and so $N + 1$ points, the rectangle rule sums $\Delta x\, f(x_i)$ over the intervals (eq. 1.10). The trapezoid rule interpolates linearly in each interval and sums $\Delta x\, [f(x_i) + f(x_{i+1})]/2$ (eq. 1.11). Summing a simple rule over the intervals is called compound integration. Simpson's rule takes the intervals in pairs, so that $N$ is even, and fits a parabola through the three points of each pair. For points $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ it gives $(\gamma - \alpha)(f_\alpha + 4 f_\beta + f_\gamma)/6$ (eq. 1.12). Compounded, it weights the samples as $(\Delta x/3)(f_0 + 4 f_1 + 2 f_2 + 4 f_3 + \ldots + 2 f_{N-2} + 4 f_{N-1} + f_N)$ (eq. 1.13). The compound trapezoid rule converges at second order, and Simpson's rule at fourth. In practice, quadratic interpolation is as high as such rules go, and analogous rules exist for unequal spacing. Where the evaluation points can be chosen, Gaussian quadrature places them at the zeros of polynomials and is far more accurate. **[GK]** An $n$-point Gauss rule is exact for polynomials of degree up to $2n - 1$ (DLMF §3.5(v)).

#### 2.1.5 Root finding

The Newton-Raphson method improves a guess $x_0$ for a root of $f$ by the correction $\delta x = -f(x_0)/f'(x_0)$, from a first-order Taylor expansion (eqs. 1.14 and 1.15). It iterates until the correction falls below a tolerance (§1.2.3, fig. 1.5). It needs a good initial guess, since the dropped terms are small only near the root, and a derivative that does not vanish there, and it can fall into a cycle instead of converging. The secant method replaces the derivative with a difference. Bisection halves an interval that brackets a root, found by the change of sign of $f$, and Brent's method combines several methods into a robust one. **[GK]** Near a simple root, Newton's method converges quadratically and the secant method with order $(1 + \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 1.62$, while bisection converges slowly (DLMF §3.8(ii) and §3.8(iii)).

#### 2.1.6 Norms

A norm condenses the pointwise errors $\epsilon_i = |f_i - f(x_i)|$ of an approximation at $N$ points into one number (§1.2.4). The text uses the maximum norm $\|q\|_\infty = \max_i |q_i|$ and the normalised norms $\|q\|_p = [(1/N) \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} |q_i|^p]^{1/p}$, among them the $L_1$ and $L_2$ norms (eqs. 1.16 to 1.19). The factor $1/N$ makes norms on grids of different resolution comparable, as convergence studies require. The maximum norm is local, giving a single element all the weight, while the others are global.

#### 2.1.7 Ordinary differential equations

A system of first-order ODEs $\dot{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y})$ (eq. 1.20) covers higher-order ODEs too, through added variables (§1.2.5). Explicit methods evaluate $\mathbf{f}$ with known values only, as Euler's method $\mathbf{y}^{n+1} = \mathbf{y}^n + \Delta t\, \mathbf{f}(t^n, \mathbf{y}^n)$ does at first order (eq. 1.22). The most popular is the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, which averages four slopes. These are $\mathbf{k}_1 = \mathbf{f}(t^n, \mathbf{y}^n)$, then $\mathbf{k}_2$ and $\mathbf{k}_3$ at the midpoint after half steps with $\mathbf{k}_1$ and $\mathbf{k}_2$, and $\mathbf{k}_4$ at the end after a full step with $\mathbf{k}_3$. They combine as $\mathbf{y}^{n+1} = \mathbf{y}^n + (\Delta t/6)(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4)$, with weights like those of Simpson's rule (eqs. 1.23 to 1.27, fig. 1.6). The step size trades accuracy against cost, and an adaptive step size, driven by an estimate of the truncation error, balances the two.

Implicit methods evaluate $\mathbf{f}$ with the new values. Backward Euler, $\mathbf{y}^{n+1} = \mathbf{y}^n + \Delta t\, \mathbf{f}(t^{n+1}, \mathbf{y}^{n+1})$ (eq. 1.30), is solved by Newton iteration. A guess, naturally $\mathbf{y}^{n+1}_0 = \mathbf{y}^n$, is corrected by $\Delta\mathbf{y}$ from the linear system $(\mathbf{I} - \Delta t\, \mathbf{J})\, \Delta\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^n - \mathbf{y}^{n+1}_k + \Delta t\, \mathbf{f}(t^{n+1}, \mathbf{y}^{n+1}_k)$, with the Jacobian $\mathbf{J} = \partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{y}$ at the current iterate $\mathbf{y}^{n+1}_k$ (eqs. 1.31 to 1.38). The iteration stops once $\|\Delta\mathbf{y}\| < \epsilon \|\mathbf{y}^n\|$, for a small tolerance $\epsilon$. The Jacobian plays the role of the derivative in Newton's method, and a cheaper approximation to it may suffice. Explicit methods are simpler and cheaper per step, but implicit methods suit stiff problems, whose solutions change on widely different timescales (doi:10.1016/0021-9991(87)90001-5), such as nuclear reaction networks (1999ApJS..124..241T). A large spread in the eigenvalues of the Jacobian usually marks a system as stiff. Higher-order implicit methods exist as well.

#### 2.1.8 Discrete Fourier transforms

The discrete Fourier transform takes $N$ equally spaced samples $f_n$ to $F_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f_n e^{-2\pi i n k/N}$, for $k = 0, \ldots, N-1$ (eq. 1.39). The inverse is $f_n = (1/N) \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} F_k e^{2\pi i n k/N}$ (eq. 1.42). The transform serves both the analysis of data and the solution of certain linear problems (§1.2.6, §9.2). Its real part collects the cosine terms, belonging to symmetric functions, and its imaginary part the sine terms, belonging to antisymmetric ones (eqs. 1.40 and 1.41); amplitude and phase are an alternative. The text explains the factor $1/N$ by Parseval's theorem, read as equality of the total power in real and in frequency space, and checks it on the transform of a constant, which is a delta function. For real $f_n$ half of the $F_k$ are redundant, since $F_{-k} = F_k^*$, so the transform holds no more information than the samples. Direct summation costs $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) reorders the sums to reuse partial sums, at a cost of $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$. It assumes a periodic function on evenly spaced points, also across the boundary. On $[0, 1]$ with five points, the samples therefore lie at $0, 0.2, \ldots, 0.8$, not at $0, 0.25, \ldots, 1$. A function that is not periodic shows a signal at the wavenumber of the domain size.

**[GK]** For real $f_n$, eq. 1.39 gives $\mathrm{Im}(F_k) = -\sum_n f_n \sin(2\pi n k/N)$, since $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i \sin\theta$; eq. 1.41 lacks the minus sign. With the normalisation of eqs. 1.39 and 1.42, Parseval's theorem reads $\sum_n |f_n|^2 = (1/N) \sum_k |F_k|^2$, so the total powers differ by the factor $N$ (Smith 2007, section on the Rayleigh energy theorem). The factor $1/N$ in eq. 1.42 follows from the orthogonality of the discrete exponentials, $\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i k (n - m)/N} = N \delta_{nm}$. The FFT goes back to Cooley and Tukey (1965, doi:10.1090/S0025-5718-1965-0178586-1).

#### 2.1.9 Classes of partial differential equations

PDEs are grouped as hyperbolic, parabolic or elliptic (§2.1). The text sets the formal definitions aside as not very intuitive, and characterises each class by its prototype instead. The systems of multiphysics problems span the classes, but the methods for each class are developed separately before they are combined. **[GK]** Formally, a second-order equation $A \phi_{xx} + 2B \phi_{xy} + C \phi_{yy} + \ldots = 0$ in two variables is hyperbolic if $B^2 - AC > 0$, parabolic if $B^2 - AC = 0$ and elliptic if $B^2 - AC < 0$ (Weisstein, MathWorld, Partial Differential Equation). The wave, heat and Poisson equations below are prototypes of the three cases.

The prototype of a hyperbolic equation is the wave equation $\phi_{tt} = c^2 \phi_{xx}$ (eq. 2.1, §2.2). Its general solution $\phi(x, t) = \alpha f_0(x - ct) + \beta g_0(x + ct)$ (eq. 2.2) consists of two profiles, set by the initial conditions, that travel at the speed $c$ to the right and to the left. The simplest first-order hyperbolic equation is linear advection, $a_t + u a_x = 0$ (eq. 2.3), which moves any initial profile unchanged at the velocity $u$. It serves as the model equation for the numerical methods for hyperbolic equations. A system $\mathbf{a}_t + \mathbf{A} \mathbf{a}_x = 0$, with $\mathbf{a} = (a_0, \ldots, a_{N-1})^\intercal$ (eq. 2.4), is called hyperbolic if the eigenvalues of the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ are real, for which the text refers to LeVeque (2002, doi:10.1017/CBO9780511791253). Characteristics are curves in the space-time plane along which the solution is constant, and their speed is the speed at which information travels. For a linear equation or system, they determine the solution completely. **[GK]** For a system, the standard definition also requires $\mathbf{A}$ to be diagonalisable, so that its eigenvectors can split any data into waves (LeVeque 2002, §2.9). Along each characteristic $dx/dt = \lambda_p$, it is then the characteristic variable $w_p = \mathbf{l}_p \cdot \mathbf{a}$ that stays constant, rather than the solution as a whole (LeVeque 2002, §3.1 to §3.3).

The prototype of an elliptic equation is the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 \phi = f$ (eq. 2.5, §2.3). It contains no time: it is a pure boundary value problem, whose solution follows from the source $f$ and the boundary conditions alone. No information propagates, and $\phi$ responds instantly throughout the domain. In astrophysical flows it arises mostly as the equation for the gravitational potential.

The prototype of a parabolic equation is the heat equation $\phi_t = k\, \phi_{xx}$ (eq. 2.6, §2.4), which shares aspects of both other classes. It describes diffusion: a sharp feature spreads into a smoother profile, on a timescale set by the coefficient $k$. Parabolic equations appear for thermal diffusion, for the diffusion of species, and with viscosity. **[GK]** Eq. 2.6 as printed has $f$ in place of $\phi$ on its right-hand side. Dimensional analysis of eq. 2.6 gives the timescale of diffusion over a length $L$ as $L^2/k$.

### 2.2 Finite-volume grids

Chapter 3 introduces discretisation and the finite-volume grid on which the rest of the text builds.

#### 2.2.1 Discretisation

A continuous function $f(x, t)$ is represented on a computer by a finite number of values, at discrete points and times (§3.1). Gridless methods expand the function in continuous basis functions, as spectral methods do with sines and cosines. Alternatively, they represent the mass by particles and recover continuous fields by integrating over these with a kernel. The latter is smoothed particle hydrodynamics (doi:10.1016/0010-4655(88)90026-4), a method popular in astrophysics. Grid-based methods differ in the style of the grid and in the discretisation. Structured grids are logically Cartesian: each cell is addressed by one integer index per dimension, and the grid maps exactly onto a multi-dimensional array. Unstructured grids, for instance of triangles or tetrahedra, fit irregular domains easily, but need more complex data structures with less efficient memory access. On structured grids, finite-difference methods start from the differential form of the PDEs, finite-volume methods from the integral form. Conservation then follows naturally from the finite-volume discretisation, which explains its popularity in astrophysics. The text treats finite-volume methods on structured grids, which suffice for the boxes, discs and spheres of astrophysical problems (§3.2).

#### 2.2.2 Grids and their data

Three structured grids hold the same data in different ways (§3.2, fig. 3.1). A finite-difference grid stores point values $f_i = f(x_i)$ at $x_i = i \Delta x$, for $i = 0, \ldots, N$, with a point on each boundary (eq. 3.1). A cell-centred finite-difference grid divides the domain into $N$ zones and stores point values at their centres, $x_i = (i + 1/2) \Delta x$ for $i = 0, \ldots, N - 1$, so that the first value lies $\Delta x/2$ inside the boundary. Some finite-difference schemes stagger their variables, with the velocity on the zone edges and the density at the centres, for example. A finite-volume grid has the layout of the cell-centred one, but stores the average of $f$ over each zone, $\langle f \rangle_i = (1/\Delta x) \int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}} f(x)\, dx$ (eq. 3.2, §3.3). The zone edges carry half-integer indices, $x_{i \pm 1/2} = x_i \pm \Delta x/2$, and $\Delta x \langle f \rangle_i$ is the total amount of $f$ in the zone. On a regular grid, $\Delta x$ is constant. To second order, the zone average equals the value at the zone centre, $\langle f \rangle_i \approx f(x_i)$ (eq. 3.3), as a Taylor expansion inside the integral shows (eq. 3.4). **[GK]** The expansion gives $\langle f \rangle_i = f(x_i) + (\Delta x^2/24) f''(x_i) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^4)$.

Interpolation on a finite-volume grid must be conservative: the reconstructed $f(x)$, averaged over a zone, returns the average of that zone. The quadratic $f(x) = A (x - x_i)^2 + B (x - x_i) + C$ through three neighbouring averages has $A = (\langle f \rangle_{i-1} - 2 \langle f \rangle_i + \langle f \rangle_{i+1})/(2 \Delta x^2)$, $B = (\langle f \rangle_{i+1} - \langle f \rangle_{i-1})/(2 \Delta x)$ and $C = (-\langle f \rangle_{i-1} + 26 \langle f \rangle_i - \langle f \rangle_{i+1})/24$ (eqs. 3.5 to 3.9).

#### 2.2.3 Differences and stencils

Derivatives of finite-volume data can use the centred difference of 2.1.3, treating the averages as point values at the zone centres, and remain second-order accurate (§3.3.1). Higher accuracy comes from differentiating a conservative interpolant fitted to more zones. Every difference approximation is a linear combination of nearby values, its stencil, whose width counts the zones reached on either side. The second derivative $(f_{i+1} - 2 f_i + f_{i-1})/\Delta x^2$ (eq. 3.10), for example, spans three zones. **[GK]** A Taylor expansion shows this second difference to be second-order accurate.

#### 2.2.4 Conservation

Finite-volume grids suit conservation laws $\partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}/\partial t + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}) = 0$ (eq. 3.11), with conserved quantities $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$, such as mass, momentum and energy, and their fluxes $\mathbf{F}$ (§3.3.2). Integrating over a zone in one dimension and dividing by $\Delta x$ gives $\partial \langle \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}} \rangle_i/\partial t = -[\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})|_{x_{i+1/2}} - \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})|_{x_{i-1/2}}]/\Delta x$ (eqs. 3.12 and 3.13), whatever the discretisation in time. The average of a zone thus changes only by the difference of the fluxes through its two faces. Neighbouring zones share each face flux with opposite signs (eq. 3.14), so the sum over all zones telescopes, and the conserved quantities are conserved to round-off. Conservation is not accuracy, however: fluxes drawn from a random number generator would conserve just as well.

#### 2.2.5 Boundary conditions and ghost cells

A difference near the edge of the domain needs values outside it, such as $f_{\mathrm{lo}-1}$ for the one-sided difference $(f_i - f_{i-1})/\Delta x$ (eq. 3.15) in the first zone, $\mathrm{lo}$ (§3.3.3). The grid is therefore extended beyond the domain by ghost cells, which exist only to impose the boundary conditions, so that one update applies to every zone of the domain (fig. 3.2). Wider stencils need more ghost cells. Periodic boundaries copy from the opposite end, $f_{\mathrm{hi}+1} = f_\mathrm{lo}$ and $f_{\mathrm{lo}-1} = f_\mathrm{hi}$ (eqs. 3.16 and 3.17). A simple outflow, or zero-gradient, boundary copies the adjacent value, $f_{\mathrm{hi}+1} = f_\mathrm{hi}$ and $f_{\mathrm{lo}-1} = f_\mathrm{lo}$ (eqs. 3.18 and 3.19).

#### 2.2.6 Beyond the uniform grid

The text names four extensions as directions for further study (§3.5). Domain decomposition divides the domain into sub-domains for the processors of a parallel computer. Each sub-domain has its own perimeter of ghost cells, filled from neighbouring sub-domains or from the physical boundary conditions (fig. 3.3). Adaptive mesh refinement focuses resolution through a hierarchy of grids, after Berger and Colella (1989, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(89)90035-1). Each level is an even integer multiple finer than the next coarser one, with cells that line up, which makes conservation natural, and corrections at coarse-fine interfaces keep the levels consistent. Mapped grids transform a logically Cartesian mesh into a curvilinear one, and keep the integer indexing and the performance of structured grids. Lagrangian grids move particles with the flow and build a finite-volume grid from their Voronoi tessellation, as in the Arepo code (arXiv:0901.4107). **[GK]** Berger and Colella refine in time by the same ratio as in space, so that one explicit scheme is stable on every level, and admit only even ratios to simplify their error estimation (1989, §2). A coarse cell covered by a fine grid takes the conservative average of its fine cells. A coarse cell beside a fine grid takes, through the shared face, the sum of the fine fluxes over the fine time steps (1989, §3).

### 2.3 Advection

Chapters 4 and 5 develop the numerical methods for linear advection, the model problem for hyperbolic equations.

#### 2.3.1 The linear advection equation

The linear advection equation $a_t + u a_x = 0$ (eq. 4.1) carries a scalar $a(x, t)$ with the velocity $u$, to the right for $u > 0$ (§4.1). Any profile $a(x - ut)$ solves it, so the initial data move at the speed $u$ with their shape preserved, and the solution is constant along the parallel characteristics $x - ut = \mathrm{const}$ (fig. 4.1). Many hyperbolic systems, among them the equations of hydrodynamics, can be written as systems of nonlinear advection equations, so linear advection gives insight into their methods. Stability is a central concern: some discretisations blow up to unphysically large values within a few steps, and others are stable only for limited time steps.

#### 2.3.2 Upwind differencing and the CFL condition

A finite-difference grid stores $a^n_i = a(x_i, t^n)$ at points $x_i$ and times $t^n = n \Delta t$ (§4.2, fig. 4.2). The first-order upwind method $(a^{n+1}_i - a^n_i)/\Delta t = -u (a^n_i - a^n_{i-1})/\Delta x$ (eq. 4.2) is explicit, as the new value depends only on the old time level. Its one-sided difference takes the zone on the side from which information arrives, the upwind side, and this choice makes the method stable. The difference reaches one zone to the left, so one ghost point at each boundary lets the same update apply to every point. Stability also limits the time step to the time that information needs to cross a zone, $\Delta t \le \Delta x/u$ (eq. 4.3), the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition. With the CFL number $\mathcal{C} = u \Delta t/\Delta x$ (eq. 4.4), stability requires $\mathcal{C} \le 1$, and the time step is chosen as $\Delta t = \mathcal{C} \Delta x/u$ (eq. 4.5), with a typical value $\mathcal{C} = 0.7$.

At $\mathcal{C} = 1$ the upwind method shifts the data by exactly one zone per step and so advects without error. In a nonlinear system the speed varies from zone to zone, so $\mathcal{C} = 1$ cannot hold everywhere, and the most restrictive time step over all zones sets the step for the whole grid. The upwind method is first-order accurate and shows substantial numerical dissipation, the more so at small $\mathcal{C}$ (fig. 4.3). Standard tests are a Gaussian profile and the top hat, with $a = 1$ for $1/3 \le x < 2/3$ and $a = 0$ elsewhere on $[0, 1]$ (eq. 4.6), both with periodic boundaries. The forward-time centred-space (FTCS) method, with the centred difference $(a_{i+1} - a_{i-1})/(2 \Delta x)$, is unconditionally unstable: its amplitude grows without bound for any $\mathcal{C}$ (fig. 4.4).

#### 2.3.3 Stability

Stability is analysed by following a single Fourier mode, $a^n_i = A^n e^{I i \theta}$, with $I = \sqrt{-1}$ and a phase $\theta$; a method is stable if $|A^{n+1}/A^n| \le 1$ (§4.3). For FTCS, $a^{n+1}_i = a^n_i - (\mathcal{C}/2)(a^n_{i+1} - a^n_{i-1})$ (eq. 4.7) gives $A^{n+1} = A^n (1 - I \mathcal{C} \sin\theta)$ and so $|A^{n+1}/A^n|^2 = 1 + \mathcal{C}^2 \sin^2\theta$ (eqs. 4.8 to 4.11). This exceeds one for every $\mathcal{C} > 0$, unless $\sin\theta = 0$. For the upwind method, $|A^{n+1}/A^n|^2 = 1 - 2\mathcal{C}(1 - \mathcal{C})(1 - \cos\theta)$ (eq. 4.12), which is stable for $0 \le \mathcal{C} \le 1$. The analysis holds only for linear equations, whose Fourier modes decouple, but its ideas guide nonlinear problems as well. **[GK]** The method is known as von Neumann stability analysis. Charney, Fjørtoft and von Neumann (1950, doi:10.1111/j.2153-3490.1950.tb00336.x) applied it to numerical weather prediction.

Truncation analysis offers another view: the higher-order terms of the Taylor expansions show which equation a scheme actually solves. For the upwind method, this is $a_t + u a_x = (u \Delta x/2)(1 - \mathcal{C})\, a_{xx}$ (eqs. 4.13 to 4.15), an advection equation with a diffusion term. For $\mathcal{C} > 1$ the diffusion coefficient turns negative, so the scheme sharpens smooth features instead of spreading them, which is unphysical. For FTCS, the diffusion term is negative for every $\mathcal{C}$. **[GK]** This is modified-equation analysis (Warming and Hyett 1974, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(74)90011-4). For FTCS, the diffusion coefficient is $-u^2 \Delta t/2 = -(u \Delta x/2)\mathcal{C}$.

The domain of dependence gives a third view (§4.3.1, fig. 4.5). The numerical domain of dependence of $a^{n+1}_i$ holds the points that can influence it, for the upwind method $a^n_{i-1}$ and $a^n_i$. The physical one follows the characteristic back over one time step, to $x_i - u \Delta t = x_i - \mathcal{C} \Delta x$. A stable method must have a numerical domain of dependence that contains the physical one, since its update would otherwise miss information that determines the solution. The condition is necessary, but not sufficient: FTCS satisfies it and is unstable. **[GK]** This containment is the condition of Courant, Friedrichs and Lewy (1928, doi:10.1007/BF01448839), necessary for a difference scheme to converge. Eq. 4.3 is its form for the upwind method.

#### 2.3.4 Implicit differencing

Evaluating the spatial difference at the new time gives the implicit upwind method $(a^{n+1}_i - a^n_i)/\Delta t = -u (a^{n+1}_i - a^{n+1}_{i-1})/\Delta x$ (eq. 4.16). Its equations $-\mathcal{C} a^{n+1}_{i-1} + (1 + \mathcal{C}) a^{n+1}_i = a^n_i$ (eq. 4.17) form a coupled linear system (§4.4). With periodic boundaries, its matrix is bidiagonal with one corner entry (eq. 4.18), and other boundary conditions change its form. The matrix solve makes implicit methods more expensive than explicit ones, but this method is stable for any $\mathcal{C}$. Stability is not accuracy, however, and accurate solutions still need a small $\mathcal{C}$ (fig. 4.6). **[GK]** The amplification factor is $1/[1 + \mathcal{C}(1 - e^{-I\theta})]$, whose magnitude does not exceed one for any $\mathcal{C} \ge 0$.

#### 2.3.5 Eulerian and Lagrangian frames

The total time derivative of $a(x, t)$ along a path $x(t)$ is $da/dt = \partial a/\partial t + (\partial a/\partial x)(dx/dt)$ (eq. 4.19, §4.5). A stationary observer, with $dx/dt = 0$, sees $\partial a/\partial t$: this is the Eulerian frame. An observer moving with the flow, with $dx/dt = u$, follows a feature and sees the Lagrangian, material or advective derivative $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + u\, \partial/\partial x$ (eq. 4.20). Linear advection then reads $Da/Dt = 0$. The methods here are Eulerian, on a fixed grid through which the fluid moves, but the Lagrangian frame helps to understand how a fluid element changes.

#### 2.3.6 Errors and convergence

With periodic boundaries, a profile advected for one period should return to its initial state, so any difference is numerical error (§4.6). Its absolute size is the norm $\epsilon_\mathrm{abs} = \|a^\mathrm{final} - a^\mathrm{init}\|_2$, and its relative size $\epsilon_\mathrm{rel} = \epsilon_\mathrm{abs}/\|a^\mathrm{init}\|_2$ (eqs. 4.21 and 4.22), with the normalised norms of 2.1.6. The normalisation by $N$ keeps the absolute error comparable between resolutions; for the relative error it cancels. **[GK]** If the error scales as $\Delta x^p$, the errors at $N$ and $2N$ zones give the measured order of convergence, $p = \log_2(\epsilon_N/\epsilon_{2N})$.

#### 2.3.7 Finite-volume advection

In conservation form, linear advection reads $a_t + [f(a)]_x = 0$, with the flux $f(a) = u a$ (eq. 5.1, §5.1). Integrating over a zone gives the evolution of the zone average, written $a_i$ from here on, by the fluxes through its faces: $\partial a_i/\partial t = -([f(a)]_{i+1/2} - [f(a)]_{i-1/2})/\Delta x$ (eqs. 5.2 and 5.3). Two routes lead to second order in time. The first differences $\partial a_i/\partial t$ directly and evaluates the fluxes at the midpoint in time, a predictor-corrector or characteristic-tracing method (Colella 1990, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(90)90233-Q). The second treats eq. 5.3 as a system of ODEs and integrates it with a standard ODE method, which is the method of lines. The grid carries two ghost cells at each end (fig. 5.1).

#### 2.3.8 The predictor-corrector scheme

Evaluating the fluxes at $t^{n+1/2}$ gives the second-order update $(a^{n+1}_i - a^n_i)/\Delta t = -([f(a)]^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2} - [f(a)]^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2})/\Delta x$, with $[f(a)]^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2} = f(a^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2})$ (eqs. 5.4 and 5.5, §5.2). The interface state at the half time is predicted by a Taylor expansion of the zone data in space and time, and the conservative update is the correction. Each interface gets two such states, one predicted from the zone on its left and one from the zone on its right (fig. 5.2). These are $a^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = a^n_i + (\Delta x/2)(1 - u \Delta t/\Delta x)\, \partial a/\partial x|_i$ and $a^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,R} = a^n_{i+1} - (\Delta x/2)(1 + u \Delta t/\Delta x)\, \partial a/\partial x|_{i+1}$, each to $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$, where the time derivative is replaced by $-u\, \partial a/\partial x$ from the equation itself (eqs. 5.6 and 5.7). The slope may be the centred difference $(a_{i+1} - a_{i-1})/(2 \Delta x)$ (eq. 5.8), which amounts to a piecewise linear reconstruction in each zone. The two states on either side of the interface form a Riemann problem, whose solution $\mathcal{R}(a_L, a_R)$ gives a single interface state (eq. 5.9). For advection, it is the upwind state, $a_L$ for $u > 0$ and $a_R$ for $u < 0$ (eq. 5.10). The left state at the left boundary of the domain needs the slope in the first ghost cell, which in turn needs the second, so the method needs two ghost cells at each end, and wider limiters may need more (fig. 5.3).

#### 2.3.9 Limiting

Unlimited slopes produce oscillations near discontinuities, such as the edges of a top hat (fig. 5.4). Godunov's theorem states that any linear method for advection that is monotonic, introducing no new minima or maxima, is at most first-order accurate, for which the text refers to Laney (1998). A monotonic method of second order must therefore be nonlinear. Limiting makes it so, by reducing the slopes where they would create new extrema. The minmod limiter takes the slope $\mathrm{minmod}[(a_i - a_{i-1})/\Delta x, (a_{i+1} - a_i)/\Delta x]$ (eq. 5.11), which returns the argument of smaller magnitude when both share a sign, and zero otherwise (eq. 5.12). Without limiting, an overshoot and an undershoot form at a discontinuity within a single step and move away from it with every further step. With limiting, none appear and the profile stays narrower (figs. 5.5 and 5.6). **[GK]** As printed, eq. 5.12 has strict inequalities in both cases, so two equal arguments of the same sign return zero. Linear data, whose one-sided differences are equal, would then be flattened to first order. One case must read $|a| \le |b|$, as in $\mathrm{minmod}(a, b) = \mathrm{sign}(a) \min(|a|, |b|)$ for $ab > 0$.

Most limiters test the sign of the product of the left- and right-sided differences, which is negative at an extremum, where limiting is needed. The MC limiter (monotonised central difference) uses the extremum test $\xi = (a_{i+1} - a_i)(a_i - a_{i-1})$ (eq. 5.13). Its slope is $\min[|a_{i+1} - a_{i-1}|/(2 \Delta x), 2|a_{i+1} - a_i|/\Delta x, 2|a_i - a_{i-1}|/\Delta x]\, \mathrm{sign}(a_{i+1} - a_{i-1})$ for $\xi > 0$, and zero otherwise (eq. 5.14). It is second-order accurate for smooth flows. The text adds that LeVeque writes all three quantities in a minmod, a form that appears to limit a bit less. Limiting acts near extrema and steep gradients (fig. 5.7). The limiters are built to make the method total variation diminishing (TVD), for which the text refers to LeVeque (2002) and Toro (1997). A fourth-order variant of the MC limiter is more accurate in smooth flows; the text points to eqs. 2.5 and 2.6 of Colella (1985, doi:10.1137/0906009) and to eq. 191 of Colella (1990). With the slopes fully limited to zero, the scheme reduces to the first-order upwind method of eq. 4.2. The slope is commonly written as the change across a zone, $\Delta a_i = \partial a/\partial x|_i\, \Delta x$ (eq. 5.15). **[GK]** The minmod of the centred difference and of twice the two one-sided differences equals eq. 5.14 for all data. Where $\xi > 0$, the three share a sign and the smallest magnitude is taken; where $\xi \le 0$, both forms give zero. A TVD scheme does not let the total variation $\sum_i |a_{i+1} - a_i|$ grow in time (Harten 1983, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(83)90136-5; LeVeque 2002, §6.7). In terms of the ratio $\theta = (a_i - a_{i-1})/(a_{i+1} - a_i)$ of consecutive differences, the limited slope is $\phi(\theta)(a_{i+1} - a_i)/\Delta x$. Minmod has $\phi(\theta) = \max[0, \min(1, \theta)]$ and MC has $\phi(\theta) = \max[0, \min(2\theta, (1 + \theta)/2, 2)]$. The superbee limiter of fig. 5.10, which the text does not define, has $\phi(\theta) = \max[0, \min(2\theta, 1), \min(\theta, 2)]$ (LeVeque 2002, ch. 6; Roe 1986, doi:10.1146/annurev.fl.18.010186.002005).

#### 2.3.10 Reconstruct, evolve, average

The same scheme can be read as a sequence of reconstruction, evolution and averaging (§5.2.2, fig. 5.8). For $u > 0$, the conservative update $a^{n+1}_i = a^n_i + \mathcal{C}(a^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2} - a^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2})$ (eqs. 5.16 and 5.17) takes the left states and becomes $a^{n+1}_i = a^n_i + \mathcal{C}\{[a^n_{i-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \mathcal{C})\Delta a_{i-1}] - [a^n_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \mathcal{C})\Delta a_i]\}$ (eq. 5.18). Alternatively, each zone is reconstructed as the line $a_i(x) = a_i + (\Delta a_i/\Delta x)(x - x_i)$ (eq. 5.19), the profile is shifted by $\mathcal{C} \Delta x$, and the result is averaged over the zone. The fraction $\mathcal{C}$ of zone $i$ then comes from zone $i - 1$, with the average $I_< = a_{i-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta a_{i-1}(1 - \mathcal{C})$, and the fraction $1 - \mathcal{C}$ stays, with the average $I_> = a_i - \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta a_i\, \mathcal{C}$ (eqs. 5.20 to 5.25). Their weighted average, $\mathcal{C} I_< + (1 - \mathcal{C}) I_>$, reproduces eq. 5.18 (eqs. 5.26 to 5.28). Reconstruction, evolution and averaging are thus equivalent to the prediction of interface states, the solution of the Riemann problem and the conservative update.

#### 2.3.11 Convergence and the choice of limiter

The first-order method converges at first order and the second-order method at second order, though less where limiting acts on discontinuities (§5.2.2). For a smooth Gaussian advected for five periods at $\mathcal{C} = 0.8$, unlimited centred slopes converge at second order. MC slopes come close, with errors a few times larger, and minmod slopes give errors about ten times larger at a lower rate (fig. 5.9). The choice of limiter thus has a great effect on accuracy. Fig. 5.10 compares piecewise constant, centred, minmod, MC and superbee slopes on the Gaussian and on the top hat.

#### 2.3.12 The method of lines

The method of lines treats eq. 5.3 as an ODE in time, $\partial a_i/\partial t = -(u a_{i+1/2} - u a_{i-1/2})/\Delta x$ (eq. 5.29), with interface values interpolated in space only (§5.3). A piecewise linear reconstruction gives $a_{i+1/2,L} = a_i + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta a_i$ and $a_{i+1/2,R} = a_{i+1} - \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta a_{i+1}$ (eqs. 5.30 and 5.31, fig. 5.11), with the same limiting as before. The Riemann problem selects the interface value (eq. 5.32). A Runge-Kutta method of at least second order, to match the spatial accuracy, then integrates the ODEs.

#### 2.3.13 Finite volumes in several dimensions

In two dimensions, linear advection reads $a_t + u a_x + v a_y = 0$ (eq. 5.33), or $a_t + (u a)_x + (v a)_y = 0$ in conservative form for constant $u$ and $v$ (eq. 5.34, §5.4). The zone average $a_{i,j}$ (eq. 5.35) changes by the fluxes through the four faces of the zone (eqs. 5.36 and 5.37). Integrated over a step, the update uses fluxes averaged over each face and over the step (eqs. 5.38 to 5.40). To second order, these averages are the fluxes at the face centres and at the midpoint in time (eq. 5.41), which gives $a^{n+1}_{i,j} = a^n_{i,j} - \Delta t \{[(u a)^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j} - (u a)^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2,j}]/\Delta x + [(v a)^{n+1/2}_{i,j+1/2} - (v a)^{n+1/2}_{i,j-1/2}]/\Delta y\}$ (eq. 5.42). The interface states follow from one of two approaches. Dimensionally split methods treat each direction on its own and so reuse a one-dimensional method. Unsplit methods are more accurate and less susceptible to grid effects. A perimeter of ghost cells again sets the boundary conditions (fig. 5.12).

#### 2.3.14 Dimensional splitting

A split method updates the directions in turn (§5.4.1). An x sweep with the fluxes of $a_t + u a_x = 0$ gives an intermediate state $a^\star$ (eqs. 5.43 and 5.45), from which a y sweep with the fluxes of $a_t + v a_y = 0$ completes the step (eqs. 5.44 and 5.46). The interface states are those of one dimension, with the time derivative taken from the one-dimensional equation of the sweep, so no transverse terms enter (eq. 5.47). The y sweep starts from $a^\star$ and so sees the result of the x sweep. Second order in time follows from Strang splitting (Strang 1968, doi:10.1137/0705041): the order of the sweeps alternates every step, x before y and then y before x, with the same $\Delta t$ for the pair. **[GK]** Alternating the order lets the leading splitting errors of consecutive steps cancel. Strang's symmetric form achieves this within a single step, with half a step in x on either side of a full step in y.

#### 2.3.15 Corner transport upwind

The unsplit method, also called corner transport upwind (CTU), uses the flow in the transverse direction to predict the interface states, and updates all directions at once (§5.4.2, Colella 1990). The left state at $x_{i+1/2}$ becomes $a^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} = \hat{a}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} - (\Delta t/2)\, v\, \partial a/\partial y|_{i,j}$, the normal prediction $\hat{a}$ of the split method with a transverse flux difference (eqs. 5.48 and 5.49). The procedure has four stages. First, the normal predictions $\hat{a}$ are made on both sides of all x and y interfaces. Second, a Riemann problem at each interface gives transverse states $a^T$ (eqs. 5.50 and 5.51). Third, each normal prediction is corrected with the difference of the transverse states of its own zone, $a^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} = \hat{a}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} - (\Delta t/2)\, v\, (a^T_{i,j+1/2} - a^T_{i,j-1/2})/\Delta y$, and likewise with zone $i + 1$ for the right state (eqs. 5.53 and 5.54, fig. 5.13). Fourth, a second Riemann problem gives the final interface state (eq. 5.55), for the conservative update of eq. 5.38. Saltzman (1994, doi:10.1006/jcph.1994.1184) extends the method to three dimensions. Advected diagonally for one period on a $32 \times 32$ grid, a Gaussian and a top hat are smeared as in one dimension, and the top hat shows a slight undershoot on its trailing side (figs. 5.14 and 5.15). The advection solver of pyro implements this unsplit second-order method (§5.7).

#### 2.3.16 The time step in several dimensions

For split methods, the time step is usually $\Delta t = \mathcal{C} \min(\Delta x/|u|, \Delta y/|v|)$, with $\mathcal{C} \le 1$ (eq. 5.56, §5.4.3). Fully multi-dimensional methods use the more restrictive $\Delta t = \mathcal{C} (\sum_d |\mathbf{U} \cdot \mathbf{e}_d|/\Delta x_d)^{-1}$ with $\mathcal{C} \lesssim 1$ (eq. 5.57), as in Shu and Osher (1989, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(89)90222-2). For CTU, Colella (1990) argues that the transverse terms remove part of the instability of simpler schemes, so that eq. 5.56 suffices. A CFL number alone therefore does not define a time step, and results must state which of the two forms they use.

#### 2.3.17 The method of lines in several dimensions

The method of lines averages the fluxes over the faces only, without the time integral, and to second order takes them at the face centres (eqs. 5.58 to 5.60, §5.4.4). This gives the ODE $da_{i,j}/dt = -[(u a)_{i+1/2,j} - (u a)_{i-1/2,j}]/\Delta x - [(v a)_{i,j+1/2} - (v a)_{i,j-1/2}]/\Delta y$ (eq. 5.61). Interface states come from slopes in each direction separately, as in one dimension (eqs. 5.62 to 5.65), and every Runge-Kutta stage repeats the reconstruction and the Riemann problems. Both directions update together, coupled by the integrator. The method is simpler than CTU, but its range of stability is smaller. For two-dimensional first-order upwinding with $u = v$ and $\Delta x = \Delta y$, a Fourier mode $A^n e^{I i \theta} e^{I j \varphi}$ (eqs. 5.66 and 5.67) has the amplification $1 - \mathcal{C}(1 - e^{-I\theta}) - \mathcal{C}(1 - e^{-I\varphi})$ (eq. 5.68). Evaluated numerically, it exceeds one in magnitude for $\mathcal{C} > 1/2$. The limit is $1/d$ in $d$ dimensions, mirroring eq. 5.57 against eq. 5.56, and the analysis applies to each stage of a higher-order integrator. For this restriction, the text refers to Shu and Osher (1989, §4), McCorquodale and Colella (2011, doi:10.2140/camcos.2011.6.1) and Titarev and Toro (2004, doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2004.05.015). The fourth-order method of McCorquodale and Colella allows $\Delta t \sum_d |\mathbf{U} \cdot \mathbf{e}_d|/\Delta x_d \lesssim 1.4$. Total variation diminishing Runge-Kutta methods are popular here, for which the text cites a 1996 report of Gottlieb and Shu. A top hat advected with fourth-order Runge-Kutta is unstable at $\mathcal{C} = 0.8$ and stable at $\mathcal{C} = 0.4$ (fig. 5.16). **[GK]** The bound also follows analytically. For $\mathcal{C} \le 1/2$, the triangle inequality gives $|A^{n+1}/A^n| \le |1 - 2\mathcal{C}| + 2\mathcal{C} = 1$, while the mode $\theta = \varphi = \pi$ gives $|1 - 4\mathcal{C}| > 1$ for $\mathcal{C} > 1/2$.

#### 2.3.18 High-order finite differences

Higher order pays off in smooth flow, at a cost in computation and complexity, and many high-order methods do no better than second order at discontinuities (§5.5). High-order finite volumes become costly in several dimensions, since the flux through a face is an integral that needs quadrature over the face. With Gauss quadrature, third order takes at least two flux evaluations per face in two dimensions, and typically four in three (§5.5.1). The conservative finite-difference method keeps the update of §3.3.2, but reads $(F_{i+1/2} - F_{i-1/2})/\Delta x$ as a direct approximation of the flux derivative at $x_i$, with $F_{i+1/2}$ no longer tied to the flux itself (eq. 5.70, §5.5.2). Global conservation still holds, as each numerical flux serves two neighbouring points. With the method of lines, a high-order finite-difference method then needs a high-order time integrator and a stable, high-order approximation of the flux derivative. **[GK]** The numerical flux approximates $h(x_{i+1/2})$ for a function $h$ whose average over one zone width is the flux, $f(a(x)) = (1/\Delta x) \int_{x - \Delta x/2}^{x + \Delta x/2} h(\xi)\, d\xi$. The point values of the flux are then zone averages of $h$, so the finite-volume reconstruction formulas carry over unchanged, but above second order only on uniform grids (Shu 1997, §2.1.2).

#### 2.3.19 WENO reconstruction

For advection with a constant speed $u$, the finite-difference method needs a high-order value $a_{i+1/2}$ (§5.5.3). From the five values $a_{i-2}, \ldots, a_{i+2}$, it is $(2 a_{i-2} - 13 a_{i-1} + 47 a_i + 27 a_{i+1} - 3 a_{i+2})/60 + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^5)$ (eq. 5.71). Three-point stencils give three third-order alternatives: $(2 a_{i-2} - 7 a_{i-1} + 11 a_i)/6$, $(-a_{i-1} + 5 a_i + 2 a_{i+1})/6$ and $(2 a_i + 5 a_{i+1} - a_{i+2})/6$ (eqs. 5.72a to 5.72c). The wide stencil is more accurate, but reaches across a nearby discontinuity and so causes Gibbs oscillations, whereas a narrow stencil can be chosen to avoid it (figs. 5.17 and 5.18). Choosing the smoothest stencil is essentially non-oscillatory (ENO) reconstruction. ENO is less accurate than its stencils would allow, and its logical branches cost performance. Weighted ENO (WENO) combines all stencils instead. With the weights $1/10$, $3/5$ and $3/10$ on the three stencils, in the order written above, the combination reproduces eq. 5.71 (eq. 5.73). Near a discontinuity, the weights shift towards the stencils that avoid it.

In the notation of Gerolymos, Sénéchal and Vallet (2009, doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2009.07.039), a WENO scheme of order $2r - 1$ computes $r$ stencils $a_{r,k,i+1/2} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1} A_{r,k,\ell}\, a_{i+k-\ell}$, for $k = 0, \ldots, r - 1$ and constant coefficients $A$ (eq. 5.74). It combines them as $\sum_k \omega_{r,k,i+1/2}\, a_{r,k,i+1/2}$, with weights $\omega$ that vary from point to point and sum to one (eqs. 5.75 and 5.76). In smooth regions, the weights should approach the optimal weights $C_{r,k}$, for which the combination is of order $\Delta x^{2r-1}$ (eq. 5.77). Following Jiang and Shu, a smoothness indicator $\beta_{r,k,i+1/2} = \sigma_{r,k,\ell,m}\, a_{i+k-\ell}\, a_{i+k-m}$, a quadratic form with tabulated constants $\sigma$, measures the derivatives on each stencil and is large where they are large (eq. 5.78). The weights are $\omega = \alpha/\sum_k \alpha$ (eq. 5.79a), with $\alpha_{r,k,i+1/2} = C_{r,k}/(\epsilon + \beta^2_{r,k,i+1/2})$ and a small $\epsilon$ against division by zero (eq. 5.79b). Equal indicators return the optimal weights, and a stencil with a large indicator gets little weight (fig. 5.18). The reconstruction is taken from the left for $u > 0$ and from the right for $u < 0$, which is simplest to implement by reversing the data. The constants $C$, $A$ and $\sigma$ are tabulated by Gerolymos et al. and by Shu (1997). **[GK]** Eq. 5.76 sums the weights from $k = 1$, whereas the convex combination of eq. 5.75 needs the sum from $k = 0$. Jiang and Shu (1996, doi:10.1006/jcph.1996.0130) define the indicator of stencil $k$ as $\sum_{l=1}^{r-1} \Delta x^{2l-1} \int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}} (d^l p_k/dx^l)^2\, dx$, for the interpolating polynomial $p_k$ of the stencil: a sum over the squared, scaled derivatives up to order $r - 1$ (Shu 1997, §2.2.2, eq. 2.61). They set $\alpha = C/(\epsilon + \beta)^2$, with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ in their tests (Shu 1997, §2.2.2, eq. 2.59), where eq. 5.79b has $\epsilon + \beta^2$.

#### 2.3.20 Convergence of WENO

Advecting a Gaussian five times around a periodic domain, as for fig. 5.9, WENO schemes are more accurate from about 100 zones on, and converge faster (fig. 5.19). With the classic fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, $r = 3$ converges at fifth order, but $r = 5$ only at fourth order instead of ninth, as the error of the time integrator dominates. With an eighth-order Dormand-Prince integrator with adaptive steps, the schemes with $r = 3$ to $r = 6$ converge at the order $2r - 2$, which the text cannot explain (fig. 5.20).

#### 2.3.21 Further directions

The text lists further directions (§5.6). Many slope limiters exist, some of higher order and less restrictive in smooth flow (LeVeque 2002; Toro 1997). Limiting each direction separately, as in the unsplit reconstruction above, can still produce overshoots and undershoots. Bell, Dawson and Shubin (1988, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(88)90065-4) and May et al. (2011, doi:10.2140/camcos.2011.6.27) limit in several dimensions at once. For a velocity field $u(x, y)$, $v(x, y)$ given externally, a quantity $\phi$ obeys $\phi_t + (\phi u)_x + (\phi v)_y = 0$ (eq. 5.80). Its interface states gain the term $-(\Delta t/2)[\phi u_x]_{i,j}$ from the velocity gradient, besides the transverse term, and upwinding resolves both the normal and the transverse Riemann problems (eq. 5.81). This construction returns for low Mach number flows (§15.3.2). The text announces an extension to density-weighted quantities in compressible flow, but its cross-reference is unresolved.

### 2.4 Burgers' equation

Chapter 6 carries the methods of 2.3 over to Burgers' equation, a nonlinear equation whose solutions form shocks and rarefactions.

#### 2.4.1 Characteristics, shocks and rarefactions

The inviscid Burgers' equation $u_t + u u_x = 0$ (eq. 6.1) is the simplest nonlinear hyperbolic equation: $u$ is both the quantity advected and the speed at which it moves (§6.1). Setting the total change $du = u_t\, dt + u_x\, dx$ to zero and using the equation shows that $u$ is constant along the characteristics $dx/dt = u(x, t)$ (eqs. 6.2 and 6.3). Unlike those of linear advection, the characteristics are not parallel, since their speed is the solution itself. With the initial values $u_0 = u(x, 0)$ as speeds, the characteristics can be drawn over a short time. For a sinusoidal profile, they soon intersect (fig. 6.1). Where they meet, no unique initial state can be traced back, and this merging of characteristics in the x-t plane is a shock. Where slower fluid lies to the left of faster fluid, the characteristics diverge instead and leave a region to be filled with intermediate states, a rarefaction (fig. 6.2). Neither wave occurs in linear problems. **[GK]** The straight characteristics are exact for as long as the solution stays smooth, not only over a short time. As $u$ is constant along a characteristic, so is its speed, and each characteristic is the line $x = x_0 + u_0(x_0)\, t$ (LeVeque 2002, §11.2). Neighbouring characteristics meet where $1 + u_0'(x_0)\, t = 0$, so if $u_0$ decreases anywhere, a shock first forms at the breaking time $t_b = -1/\min_x u_0'(x)$.

#### 2.4.2 Shock speed and the Riemann problem

In conservative form, the equation reads $u_t + [\tfrac{1}{2} u^2]_x = 0$ (eq. 6.4), with the flux $f(u) = u^2/2$. The speed $S$ of a shock follows from the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition, which the text derives following LeVeque (2002). A shock crosses the rectangle $[x_l, x_r] \times [t^n, t^{n+1}]$ of the x-t plane diagonally, with $S = \Delta x/\Delta t$, so that the interval holds the right state $u_r$ at $t^n$ and the left state $u_l$ at $t^{n+1}$, while the fluxes through its left and right edges are $f(u_l)$ and $f(u_r)$ throughout (fig. 6.3). Integrating the conservation law over the rectangle gives $u_l - u_r = -(\Delta t/\Delta x)[f(u_r) - f(u_l)]$ (eqs. 6.8 to 6.11), hence $S = [f(u_r) - f(u_l)]/(u_r - u_l)$ (eq. 6.12), and $S = (u_l + u_r)/2$ for Burgers' equation (eq. 6.13). The Riemann problem then has two cases. For $u_l > u_r$, a compression, there is a shock, and the interface state $u_s$ is $u_l$ for $S > 0$, $u_r$ for $S < 0$ and zero for $S = 0$ (eq. 6.14). Otherwise there is no shock, and plain upwinding applies, as the state can be traced back in time without ambiguity: $u_s$ is $u_l$ for $u_l > 0$, $u_r$ for $u_r < 0$, and zero otherwise (eq. 6.15). **[GK]** LeVeque derives the jump condition in §11.8 of his book. A jump with $u_l < u_r$ also satisfies eq. 6.12, as an expansion shock, but the entropy condition excludes it: for Burgers' equation, it admits a shock only for $u_l > S > u_r$, so that characteristics run into the shock from both sides (LeVeque 2002, §11.13). The last case of eq. 6.15, $u_l \le 0 \le u_r$, is a transonic rarefaction, whose fan spans the interface and puts the sonic point $u = 0$ on it (LeVeque 2002, §12.3). The case $S = 0$ of eq. 6.14 gives a zero flux. A shock with $S = 0$ has $u_r = -u_l$, however, and the same flux $u_l^2/2$ on both sides, which either state gives, as does the Godunov flux, the maximum of $f$ over $[u_r, u_l]$ for $u_l > u_r$ (Shu 1997, §2.3.1, eq. 2.70). With $u_s = 0$, the zones on either side of a stationary shock change at every step instead of staying constant.

#### 2.4.3 The second-order method

The second-order method is that of linear advection (2.3.8). The interface states are predicted to second order by a Taylor expansion in space and time, with the time derivative replaced by $-u\, u_x$ from the equation. This gives $u^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = u^n_i + (\Delta x/2)(1 - u_i \Delta t/\Delta x)\, \partial u/\partial x|_i$ (eqs. 6.5 to 6.7), and likewise the right state. The only change from linear advection is that $u_i \Delta t/\Delta x$ varies from zone to zone, where it was the constant $\mathcal{C}$, and the slopes are limited as before. The Riemann problem of 2.4.2 gives the interface state, from which the flux is $F^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(u^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2})^2$ (eq. 6.16), and the conservative update is $u^{n+1}_i = u^n_i + (\Delta t/\Delta x)(F^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2} - F^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2})$ (eq. 6.17). The time step obeys the most restrictive CFL condition over all zones, $\Delta t = \mathcal{C} \min_i \Delta x/|u_i|$ (eq. 6.18). **[GK]** Eq. 6.18 writes $\Delta t = \min_i \Delta x/u_i$, without the absolute value that negative velocities need and without the CFL number of eq. 4.5.

#### 2.4.4 Tests and the conservative form

Two tests on $[0, 1]$ use 256 zones, $\mathcal{C} = 0.8$ and the MC limiter. Initial data with $u = 1$ on the left half and $u = 2$ on the right half spread out like a fan, a rarefaction wave (fig. 6.4). Sinusoidal data, $u = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\sin[2\pi(x - 1/3)/(1/3)]$ for $1/3 \le x \le 2/3$ and $u = 1$ elsewhere (eq. 6.19), steepen into a shock that moves to the right, as the characteristics of fig. 6.1 suggest (fig. 6.5). Without explicit viscosity, the shock is almost infinitely thin, smeared slightly only by the numerical diffusion of the truncation error. Solving the conservative form is essential for shocks: the finite-volume method rests on the integral form of the equation, and the Riemann solver handles the discontinuity. Differencing the nonconservative form $u_t + u u_x = 0$ (eq. 6.20) instead, for example with an upwind difference for $u_x$, gives the wrong shock speed. **[GK]** The exact solution of the first test is the centred rarefaction $u = (x - 1/2)/t$ for $t \le x - 1/2 \le 2t$, with $u = 1$ to its left and $u = 2$ to its right (LeVeque 2002, §11.10). For eq. 6.19, $u_0'$ is smallest, $-3\pi$, at $x = 1/2$, so the shock forms at $t_b = 1/(3\pi) \approx 0.11$, near $x = 0.61$. A conservative scheme that converges does so to a weak solution, whose shocks obey the jump condition of 2.4.2 (Lax and Wendroff 1960, doi:10.1002/cpa.3160130205; LeVeque 2002, §12.9 and §12.10). This limit need not satisfy the entropy condition (LeVeque 2002, §12.11). Nonconservative schemes can converge to solutions whose shocks move at the wrong speed, as Hou and LeFloch (1994, doi:10.1090/S0025-5718-1994-1201068-0) analyse.

#### 2.4.5 Characteristic tracing

Characteristic tracing, which returns for the Euler equations, includes information in an interface state only if the characteristic that carries it moves towards the interface (§6.2). Burgers' equation has a single characteristic speed, $u$, so the left state at $x_{i+1/2}$ takes the change in time only for $u_i > 0$: $u^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = u^n_i + (\Delta x/2)[1 - (\Delta t/\Delta x) \max(0, u_i)]\, \partial u/\partial x|_i$ (eq. 6.24). For $u_i \le 0$, the state is simply the value of the piecewise linear reconstruction at the interface. **[GK]** The right state mirrors this, as $u^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,R} = u^n_{i+1} - (\Delta x/2)[1 + (\Delta t/\Delta x) \min(0, u_{i+1})]\, \partial u/\partial x|_{i+1}$.

#### 2.4.6 WENO with flux splitting

For a nonlinear scalar conservation law $u_t + f(u)_x = 0$ (eq. 6.26), the WENO method of 2.3.19 fails on two counts (§6.4). It assumes that information travels in one direction only, and it reconstructs the state and evaluates the flux from it (eq. 5.5). Instead, the flux is reconstructed directly: from the point values $f_i = f(u_i)$, WENO gives the interface flux $f_{i+1/2}$. Which way this flux propagates is then unknown, and flux splitting resolves this. The flux is written as $f(u) = f^{(+)}(u) + f^{(-)}(u)$ (eq. 6.27), with $f^{(+)}$ carrying information to the right and $f^{(-)}$ to the left. The simplest choice is the Lax-Friedrichs splitting $f^{(\pm)}(u) = \tfrac{1}{2}[f(u) \pm \alpha u]$ (eq. 6.28) with $\alpha \ge \max |\partial f/\partial u|$, which gives $\partial f^{(+)}/\partial u \ge 0$ and $\partial f^{(-)}/\partial u \le 0$ (eq. 6.29). The algorithm takes $\alpha$ as the largest characteristic speed on the whole grid and forms the split fluxes. It reconstructs $f^{(+)}$ at each interface from the left and $f^{(-)}$ from the right, as for positive and negative speeds in 2.3.19, and sums the two to $f_{i+1/2}$. The method of lines then integrates the semi-discrete update of eq. 3.14. **[GK]** For a nonlinear flux, the flux of a reconstructed state differs from the interface value of the function $h$ of 2.3.18 by a term of order $\Delta x^2$, so evaluating the flux from the state would limit the method to second order. The splitting is that of Shu (1997, §2.3.2, eqs. 2.75 to 2.77). He requires the split fluxes to have as many derivatives as the order of the scheme, which rules out several popular splittings at high order. Upwinding by the sign of the Roe speed $[f(u_{i+1}) - f(u_i)]/(u_{i+1} - u_i)$ is simpler, but for Burgers' equation it can converge to an expansion shock that violates the entropy condition, so the global splitting is more robust (Shu 1997, §2.3.2).

#### 2.4.7 Comparison and convergence

Shortly after a shock forms from the sinusoidal data of eq. 6.19, the piecewise linear method with the MC limiter and flux-split WENO with $r = 3$ and $r = 5$ differ little (fig. 6.6). The focusing of the characteristics leaves a similar number of points within the shock for all of them. The piecewise linear method captures the edges of the shock slightly better than WENO with $r = 3$, which the text attributes to its exact Riemann solver, against the more diffusive Lax-Friedrichs splitting. With $r = 5$, WENO has a marginal advantage. WENO pays off where the profile is smooth, as in a rarefaction or before a shock forms. To show that high-order convergence holds for a nonlinear problem, a Gaussian profile is evolved for a short time, before any shock forms, and compared with the exact solution, found by tracing characteristics (fig. 6.7). With the classic fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, WENO converges at fifth order for $r = 3$ and at seventh order for $r = 4$, at least over part of the resolutions. At low resolution, higher-order terms of the error still matter, and at high resolution the error of the time integrator dominates for $r = 4$. **[GK]** Shu (1997, §2.3.1) ranks the monotone fluxes from the Godunov flux, the least dissipative, to the Lax-Friedrichs flux, the most, and finds that the difference between them shrinks with high-order reconstruction. The exact solution solves $u = u_0(x - u t)$ at each point, an implicit equation for the root finding of 2.1.5. With $\Delta t$ proportional to $\Delta x$, the error of a fourth-order integrator falls as $\Delta x^4$, so it eventually exceeds a spatial error of seventh order.

#### 2.4.8 Further directions

The equation treated so far is the inviscid Burgers' equation (§6.3). The full equation adds viscosity, a diffusion of velocity, as $u_t + u u_x = \epsilon u_{xx}$ (eq. 6.25). Its solution needs the methods for diffusion and for equations that combine types of PDE, which follow in §11.3. Besides its value for teaching, Burgers' equation is a simple model of traffic flow, in which shocks arise from sudden braking, a use that many texts discuss, LeVeque (2002) among them. **[GK]** The equation is named after Burgers (1948, doi:10.1016/S0065-2156(08)70100-5), who studied it as a model of turbulence. LeVeque treats traffic flow in §11.1 of his book.

### 2.5 The Euler equations

Chapter 7 sets out the theory of the Euler equations, and chapter 8 the numerical methods for them.

#### 2.5.1 Conservation laws and the equation of state

The Euler equations express the conservation of mass, momentum and energy in the fluid approximation (§7.1). Without source terms, they read $\partial \rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{U}) = 0$, $\partial (\rho \mathbf{U})/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{U} \mathbf{U}) + \nabla p = 0$ and $\partial (\rho E)/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho E \mathbf{U} + p \mathbf{U}) = 0$ (eqs. 7.1 to 7.3), with the total energy per unit mass $E = e + |\mathbf{U}|^2/2$ (eq. 7.4). An equation of state closes the system. A common choice is the gamma-law $p = \rho e (\gamma - 1)$, with $\gamma = 5/3$ for an ideal monatomic gas (eq. 7.5), but any relation $p = p(\rho, e)$ serves (eq. 7.6). Many astrophysical equations of state have no analytic form and are integrated numerically or interpolated from tables. The Navier-Stokes equations add dissipative terms, but in astrophysical flows these act on scales far below those of the system, as the very large Reynolds numbers show. For derivations from moments of the Boltzmann equation, the text refers to Choudhuri (1998, doi:10.1017/CBO9781139171069) and F. H. Shu (1992), and for derivations from conservation to Choudhuri (1998) and LeVeque (2002). In one dimension and Cartesian coordinates, the equations take the conservative form $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_t + [\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})]_x = 0$, with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}} = (\rho, \rho u, \rho E)^\intercal$ and $\mathbf{F} = (\rho u, \rho u u + p, \rho u E + u p)^\intercal$ (eqs. 7.7 to 7.11). Temperature does not enter them, although source terms often need it from the equation of state.

#### 2.5.2 Quasi-linear and primitive forms

With $m = \rho u$, $\mathcal{E} = \rho E$ and a gamma-law gas, $p = (\mathcal{E} - m^2/2\rho)(\gamma - 1)$ (eq. 7.12), and the flux can be written in the conserved variables alone (eq. 7.13). Its Jacobian $\mathbf{A} = \partial \mathbf{F}/\partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$ (eq. 7.14), with the sound speed $c = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$, gives the quasi-linear form $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_t + \mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}) \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_x = 0$ (eq. 7.15). A general equation of state needs the derivatives of $p(\rho, e)$ with respect to the conserved variables instead. The matrix is complicated, and for its eigenvectors the text refers to Stone et al. (2008, arXiv:0804.0402) and Toro (1997). The primitive variables $\mathbf{q} = (\rho, u, p)^\intercal$ give a simpler system, $\rho_t + u \rho_x + \rho u_x = 0$, $u_t + u u_x + p_x/\rho = 0$ and $p_t + u p_x + \gamma p u_x = 0$ (eqs. 7.16 to 7.18), or $\mathbf{q}_t + \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{q}) \mathbf{q}_x = 0$, where $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{q})$ has the rows $(u, \rho, 0)$, $(0, u, 1/\rho)$ and $(0, \gamma p, u)$ (eqs. 7.19 and 7.20). The velocity equation resembles Burgers' equation, and its nonlinearity admits shocks and rarefactions. The eigenvalues follow from $|\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}| = 0$ as $\lambda^{(-)} = u - c$, $\lambda^{(\circ)} = u$ and $\lambda^{(+)} = u + c$, the same for both matrices, since both describe the same physics. For a general equation of state, $p = p(\rho, s)$ with $Ds/Dt = 0$ turns the pressure equation into $p_t + u p_x + \Gamma_1 p u_x = 0$, with $\Gamma_1 = \partial \log p/\partial \log \rho|_s$ and $c^2 = \Gamma_1 p/\rho$ (eqs. 7.21 and 7.22). For the adiabatic indices, the text refers to Hansen, Kawaler and Trimble (2004, doi:10.1007/978-1-4419-9110-2), and $\Gamma_1 = \gamma$ for an ideal gas. The eigenvalues are the speeds at which information propagates, with the fluid and at the speed of sound relative to it. As they are real, the system is hyperbolic, and as $\mathbf{A}$ depends on $\mathbf{q}$, it is quasi-linear. **[GK]** The two matrices are similar. With $\mathbf{M} = \partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}/\partial \mathbf{q}$, substituting $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{q})$ into eq. 7.15 gives $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}) \mathbf{M}$, which has the same eigenvalues. Hyperbolicity also needs a complete set of eigenvectors (2.1.9), which eq. 7.24 provides for $c > 0$.

#### 2.5.3 Eigenvectors and the characteristic form

The right and left eigenvectors obey $\mathbf{A} \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)} = \lambda^{(\nu)} \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$ and $\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \mathbf{A} = \lambda^{(\nu)} \mathbf{l}^{(\nu)}$ for $\nu \in \{-, \circ, +\}$ (eq. 7.23). For the primitive system, they are $\mathbf{r}^{(\mp)} = (1, \mp c/\rho, c^2)^\intercal$ and $\mathbf{r}^{(\circ)} = (1, 0, 0)^\intercal$ (eq. 7.24), and $\mathbf{l}^{(\mp)} = (0, \mp \rho/2c, 1/2c^2)$ and $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ)} = (1, 0, -1/c^2)$ (eqs. 7.25 to 7.27). Each is fixed up to a constant factor, here chosen so that $\mathbf{l}^{(i)} \cdot \mathbf{r}^{(j)} = \delta_{ij}$. With the right eigenvectors as the columns of $\mathbf{R}$ and the left ones as the rows of $\mathbf{L}$, $\mathbf{L} \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{L}$, and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{A} \mathbf{R}$ is the diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues (eqs. 7.28 to 7.30). Multiplying the primitive system by $\mathbf{L}$ and inserting $\mathbf{R} \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{I}$ gives, with $d\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{L}\, d\mathbf{q}$, the characteristic form $\mathbf{w}_t + \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{w}_x = 0$ (eqs. 7.31 to 7.33): three decoupled advection-like equations $w^{(\nu)}_t + \lambda^{(\nu)} w^{(\nu)}_x = 0$ (eqs. 7.34 to 7.36). In general, $d\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{L}\, d\mathbf{q}$ cannot be integrated to give the characteristic variables $\mathbf{w}$. In a linear system, each $w^{(\nu)}$ would simply be advected at its speed, and $d\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{R}\, d\mathbf{w}$ would recover the primitive variables. Each eigenvalue defines a wave travelling along the characteristics $dx/dt = \lambda^{(\nu)}$, which are straight lines in a linear system. A jump $\Delta \mathbf{q}$ in the initial data splits into jumps $\Delta \mathbf{w} \approx \mathbf{L}\, \Delta \mathbf{q}$, approximate because $\mathbf{L}$ depends on $\mathbf{q}$, and each wave carries its own. The jump in $\mathbf{q}$ across a wave is thus proportional to the right eigenvector of the wave. As $\mathbf{r}^{(\circ)}$ has only a density component, only the density jumps across the middle wave, while pressure and velocity stay constant; for more, the text refers to Toro (1997, ch. 2 and 3) and LeVeque (2002). Sod's problem, a shock tube with $\rho_l = 1$, $u_l = 0$ and $p_l = 1$ on the left and $\rho_r = 1/8$, $u_r = 0$ and $p_r = 1/10$ on the right of a discontinuity at $x = 0.5$, shows the three waves (eq. 7.37, fig. 7.1; Sod 1978, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(78)90023-2). The left wave, $u - c$, is a rarefaction, the middle wave, $u$, the contact discontinuity, and the right wave, $u + c$, a shock. All three primitive variables jump across the outer waves, but only the density across the middle one, and the regions that no wave has reached keep their initial states. The text later adds $\gamma = 1.4$ for this problem (§8.9.1).

#### 2.5.4 The Riemann problem

The Riemann problem for the Euler equations has three waves and is nonlinear, so its solution is more complex than for advection or Burgers' equation, although the ideas carry over (§7.2). For the many methods of solving it, the text refers to Toro (1997). The waves separate four regions: L and R, which keep the input states as no wave has reached them, and the star regions $L_\star$ and $R_\star$ between the waves (fig. 7.2). The solution is usually sought in the primitive variables. By $\mathbf{r}^{(\circ)}$, the middle wave is a contact discontinuity, across which the fluid neither compresses nor expands and the velocity and pressure are continuous, so that the star regions share $u_\star$ and $p_\star$. Each outer wave is a shock where the flow compresses and a rarefaction otherwise. The unknowns are thus $u_\star$, $p_\star$, $\rho_{\star L}$ and $\rho_{\star R}$.

#### 2.5.5 Rarefactions

Across a rarefaction the entropy is constant, as the system in the variables $\mathbf{q}_s = (\rho, u, s)^\intercal$ shows (§7.2.1). With $Ds/Dt = 0$ and the pressure gradient $p_x = p_s s_x + (\Gamma_1 p/\rho) \rho_x$, where $p_s = \partial p/\partial s|_\rho$, its matrix has the same eigenvalues, and its outer right eigenvectors $(1, \mp c/\rho, 0)^\intercal$ have no entropy component (eqs. 7.38 to 7.42). Across a wave, the characteristic variables of the other two waves stay constant. Across the left wave, $\mathbf{l}^{(+)} \cdot d\mathbf{q} = 0$ and $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ)} \cdot d\mathbf{q} = 0$ give $du + dp/(\rho c) = 0$ and $d\rho - dp/c^2 = 0$ (eqs. 7.43 to 7.47). With the Lagrangian sound speed $C = \rho c$ and the specific volume $\tau = 1/\rho$, these read $du = -dp/C$ and $d\tau = -dp/C^2$ across the left wave, and $du = dp/C$ and $d\tau = -dp/C^2$ across the right wave (eqs. 7.48 and 7.49). These relations are general, and the second is the definition of $\Gamma_1$ at constant entropy, $dp/p - \Gamma_1\, d\rho/\rho = 0$. Integrating them from $p_l$ or $p_r$ to $p_\star$, with $C(\tau, p)$ from the equation of state, gives the solution across a rarefaction; the quantities that stay constant across it are its Riemann invariants. A gamma-law gas allows the integrals in closed form. Constant entropy gives $p = K \rho^\gamma$ with a constant $K$ (eq. 7.50), and integrating $du = -dp/(\rho c)$ shows that $u + 2c/(\gamma - 1)$ is constant across the left rarefaction (eqs. 7.51 to 7.53). With $p_L/\rho_L^\gamma = p_\star/\rho_\star^\gamma$ (eq. 7.54), this gives $u_\star = u_L + [2c_L/(\gamma - 1)][1 - (p_\star/p_L)^{(\gamma - 1)/2\gamma}]$, and likewise $u_\star = u_R - [2c_R/(\gamma - 1)][1 - (p_\star/p_R)^{(\gamma - 1)/2\gamma}]$ across the right rarefaction (eqs. 7.55 and 7.56). A general equation of state has no closed form, and integration gives $u_\star = u(p_\star)$ and $\rho_\star = 1/\tau(p_\star)$. These functions are written $u^\mathrm{rare}_L(p_\star)$ and $u^\mathrm{rare}_R(p_\star)$.

#### 2.5.6 Shocks

Entropy is not constant across a shock, which dissipates, so the states on either side are connected by the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions, one for each conservation law, which also give the shock speed (§7.2.2). They read $\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_\star) - \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_s) = S (\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_\star - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_s)$, with the shock speed $S$ and $s$ standing for L or R (eq. 7.57). In the frame of the shock, with the velocities $\hat{u} = u - S$, the fluxes are equal on both sides (eqs. 7.58 to 7.63). Following Colella and Glaz (1985, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(85)90146-9), the text derives the jumps $[q] = q_\star - q_s$ for a general equation of state. The mass flux through the shock is $W_L = \rho_L \hat{u}_L = \rho_\star \hat{u}_\star$ for the left shock, the 1-shock, and $W_R = -\rho_R \hat{u}_R = -\rho_\star \hat{u}_\star$ for the right shock, the 3-shock, so that both are positive (eqs. 7.68 and 7.69). The conservation of mass, momentum and energy then gives $[\tau] = -[p]/W_s^2$, $[u] = \mp [p]/W_s$ with the upper sign for the left shock, and $[e] = -\tfrac{1}{2}(p_\star + p_s)[\tau]$ (eqs. 7.64 to 7.81). For a trial $p_\star$, the procedure finds $\rho_\star$ by root finding on $[e] = -\bar{p}[\tau]$, with $\bar{p} = (p_\star + p_s)/2$ and $e_\star = e(p_\star, \rho_\star)$ from the equation of state. It then computes $W_s^2 = -[p]/[\tau]$ and $u_\star = u_s \mp [p]/W_s$ (eqs. 7.82 and 7.83), and the shock speed follows from the mass flux as $S = u_s \mp W_s/\rho_s$ (eq. 7.84). For a gamma-law gas, $e = p/[\rho(\gamma - 1)]$ (eq. 7.85) gives closed forms. The density behind the shock is $\rho_{\star s} = \rho_s [(\gamma + 1) p_\star/p_s + (\gamma - 1)]/[(\gamma + 1) + (\gamma - 1) p_\star/p_s]$ (eq. 7.86), and the mass flux $W_s^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} p_s \rho_s [(\gamma + 1) p_\star/p_s + (\gamma - 1)]$ (eqs. 7.87 and 7.88). Hence $u_\star = u_s \pm c_s [2/(\gamma(\gamma - 1))]^{1/2} (1 - p_\star/p_s)/[(\gamma + 1) p_\star/((\gamma - 1) p_s) + 1]^{1/2}$, with the upper sign for the left shock (eq. 7.89), and $S = u_s \mp c_s [(\gamma + 1) p_\star/(2\gamma p_s) + (\gamma - 1)/(2\gamma)]^{1/2}$ (eq. 7.90).

#### 2.5.7 The star state

The outer states connect to the star region along curves in the u-p plane, which hold all the states reachable through a shock or a rarefaction and which the text calls Hugoniot curves (§7.2.3). The curve of the left state is $u^\mathrm{shock}_{\star L}(p)$ for $p > p_L$ and $u^\mathrm{rare}_{\star L}(p)$ for $p \le p_L$, and likewise for the right state (eq. 7.91). The solution is the intersection of the two curves, the root $p_\star$ of $u_{\star L}(p_\star) - u_{\star R}(p_\star) = 0$ (eq. 7.92), which states that velocity and pressure do not jump across the contact. For a gamma-law gas, eqs. 7.55, 7.56 and 7.89 give both curves in closed form (eq. 7.93). In Sod's problem, the left state connects to the star state through a rarefaction and the right state through a shock (fig. 7.3). **[GK]** In LeVeque's terms, only the shock branch is a Hugoniot locus, and the rarefaction branch is an integral curve (LeVeque 2002, §13.7 and §13.8). For Sod's problem with $\gamma = 1.4$, eqs. 7.92 and 7.93 give $p_\star = 0.30313$, $u_\star = 0.92745$, $\rho_{\star L} = 0.42632$ and $\rho_{\star R} = 0.26557$, a shock speed of $1.75216$, and a rarefaction from $-1.18322$ at its head to $-0.07027$ at its tail, as tabulated by Marzouk (2020, arXiv:2108.01709, table 2). These values check an exact Riemann solver.

#### 2.5.8 Sampling the solution

The complete solution needs the region in which a given ray $\xi = (x - x_\mathrm{interface})/t$ of the x-t plane falls; for the interface itself, $\xi = 0$ (§7.2.4). The contact, with the speed $S_c = u_\star$, decides first. For $S_c < \xi$ the choice is between $R_\star$ and R, and for $S_c > \xi$ between L and $L_\star$ (fig. 7.4). For $S_c = 0$, the average of the $L_\star$ and $R_\star$ states is usually taken, and testing for this case explicitly, rather than with a non-strict inequality, keeps the solution symmetric (§8.9.1). The outer wave on the chosen side then decides. A shock moves at the speed of eq. 7.84: for $S_R > 0$ the interface lies in $R_\star$, and for $S_R < 0$ in R, and likewise on the left. A rarefaction spans the speeds from its head, $u_L - c_L$ or $u_R + c_R$, to its tail, $u_\star - c_{\star L}$ or $u_\star + c_{\star R}$. For a left rarefaction, $L_\star$ lies on the interface if both speeds are negative, and L if both are positive. Otherwise the fan spans the interface, and its inner structure is needed (fig. 7.5). A point in the fan lies on the ray $x/t = u - c$ (eq. 7.94), where the text breaks off. **[GK]** The text expresses the spreading of a fan as $|\lambda_\mathrm{head}| > |\lambda_\mathrm{tail}|$. This holds for a left fan that lies wholly to the left of the interface, or a right fan wholly to its right. In general, the head runs ahead of the tail, with $\lambda_\mathrm{head} < \lambda_\mathrm{tail}$ for a left fan and $\lambda_\mathrm{head} > \lambda_\mathrm{tail}$ for a right fan. Inside a left fan of a gamma-law gas, eq. 7.94 and the constancy of $u + 2c/(\gamma - 1)$ give $u = [2/(\gamma + 1)][c_L + (\gamma - 1) u_L/2 + \xi]$ and $c = [2/(\gamma + 1)] c_L + [(\gamma - 1)/(\gamma + 1)](u_L - \xi)$. Constant entropy then gives $\rho = \rho_L (c/c_L)^{2/(\gamma - 1)}$ and $p = p_L (c/c_L)^{2\gamma/(\gamma - 1)}$ (LeVeque 2002, §14.12). A right fan follows from $\xi = u + c$ and the constancy of $u - 2c/(\gamma - 1)$.

#### 2.5.9 Other thermodynamic equations

Other forms of the energy equation are sometimes useful (§7.3). Without heat sources, the first law $de + p\, d(1/\rho) = 0$ (eq. 7.95) applied to a fluid element gives $\rho\, De/Dt + p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{U} = 0$, once the continuity equation eliminates $D\rho/Dt$ (eqs. 7.96 to 7.98). Adding $e$ times the continuity equation gives $\partial(\rho e)/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{U} e) + p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{U} = 0$ (eq. 7.99). The internal energy is not conserved, as the term $p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{U}$ is not in conservative form. The specific enthalpy $h = e + p/\rho$ (eq. 7.100) obeys $\rho\, Dh/Dt = Dp/Dt$ (eqs. 7.101 to 7.103). In very subsonic flows, where $Dp/Dt = 0$, the enthalpy is therefore conserved, $\partial(\rho h)/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho h \mathbf{U}) = 0$ (eq. 7.104), for which the text refers to Bell et al. (2004, doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2003.10.035). For the temperature, $e(\rho, T)$ with $c_v = \partial e/\partial T|_\rho$ gives $c_v\, DT/Dt = (\rho\, \partial e/\partial \rho|_T - p/\rho) \nabla \cdot \mathbf{U}$ (eqs. 7.105 and 7.106). Likewise, $h(p, T)$ with $c_p = \partial h/\partial T|_p$ gives $c_p\, DT/Dt = (1/\rho - \partial h/\partial p|_T)\, Dp/Dt$ (eqs. 7.107 and 7.108). The two forms are equivalent, one in terms of density and the other of pressure, and they return for reactive flow at constant density or constant pressure. **[GK]** Eq. 7.106 omits the factor $\rho$ before $\partial e/\partial \rho|_T$, which the substitution $D\rho/Dt = -\rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{U}$ into eq. 7.105 introduces and the units require. For an ideal gas the term vanishes, since $e$ then depends on $T$ alone.

#### 2.5.10 The Godunov method

The numerical method mirrors that for advection (§8.1). The state is reconstructed to the interfaces, a Riemann problem at each interface gives the fluxes, and a conservative update advances the conserved variables. Conservative and primitive variables each serve in parts of the algorithm, and further physics enters as source terms, in the interface states and in the update. A high-order Godunov method is a finite-volume method whose interface fluxes come from solving the Riemann problem (§8.2). Its update $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{n+1}_i = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^n_i + (\Delta t/\Delta x)(\mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2} - \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2})$ (eq. 8.1) changes each conserved quantity only by its flux through the zone boundary. The flux is the flux function evaluated at a time-centred interface state, $\mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2} = \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2})$ (eqs. 8.2 and 8.3), which the Riemann solver finds from a left and a right state predicted at the interface (fig. 8.1). The prediction is easier in the primitive variables, with their simpler characteristic structure, and an algebraic transformation turns the primitive interface states into conserved ones (eq. 8.4). The zone averages are reconstructed as a polynomial in each zone, piecewise constant, linear or parabolic, whose average over the zone is the zone average (eqs. 8.5 to 8.7). Characteristic tracing under the polynomial then finds how much of each characteristic quantity reaches the interface in a step: the jumps in the primitive variables are projected onto the characteristic variables, and only those moving towards the interface are kept. Piecewise constant reconstruction, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1/2,L} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1/2,R} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1}$ (eqs. 8.8 and 8.9), is the original method of Godunov (1959). It ignores how the state varies across the zone and is first-order accurate in space, and, without any centring in time, also in time (§8.2.1).

#### 2.5.11 Piecewise linear reconstruction and characteristic tracing

For piecewise linear reconstruction, the zone data are first converted to the primitive variables (§8.2.2, fig. 8.2). A Taylor expansion through $\Delta x/2$ in space and $\Delta t/2$ in time, with $\partial \mathbf{q}/\partial t = -\mathbf{A}\, \partial \mathbf{q}/\partial x$, gives $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = \mathbf{q}^n_i + \tfrac{1}{2}[1 - (\Delta t/\Delta x) \mathbf{A}_i] \Delta \mathbf{q}_i$, second-order accurate in space and time (eqs. 8.10 to 8.12). The slope $\Delta \mathbf{q}_i$ is limited as for advection, so that no new extrema appear, with any of the limiters of 2.3.9. With $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{R} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{R} \mathbf{L}$, any vector decomposes as $\boldsymbol{\chi} = \sum_\nu (\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot \boldsymbol{\chi}) \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$, and $\mathbf{A} \Delta \mathbf{q} = \sum_\nu \lambda^{(\nu)} (\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}) \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$ (eqs. 8.13 to 8.20). Here $\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}$ is the jump in the characteristic variable of wave $\nu$, which carries a jump in $\mathbf{q}$ along $\mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$. Keeping only the waves that move towards the interface gives $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = \mathbf{q}^n_i + \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{\nu; \lambda^{(\nu)} \ge 0} [1 - (\Delta t/\Delta x) \lambda^{(\nu)}_i] (\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)}_i \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}_i) \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}_i$ (eq. 8.21), in which $\Delta t \lambda^{(\nu)}/\Delta x$ is the CFL number of the wave. The right state is $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,R} = \mathbf{q}^n_{i+1} - \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{\nu; \lambda^{(\nu)} \le 0} [1 + (\Delta t/\Delta x) \lambda^{(\nu)}_{i+1}] (\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)}_{i+1} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}_{i+1}) \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}_{i+1}$ (eq. 8.22), as discussed by Miller and Colella (2002, doi:10.1006/jcph.2002.7158, eq. 85). This restriction is characteristic tracing. The decomposition $P \boldsymbol{\chi} = \sum_\nu (\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot \boldsymbol{\chi}) \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$ is a characteristic projection (eq. 8.23), sometimes written with a subscript $>$ or $<$ for the tracing; summed over all waves, it returns its argument.

Colella and Glaz (1985, p. 278) argue that the eigenvector decomposition linearises the quasi-linear system, so the quantities projected should be kept small by subtracting a reference state. Saltzman (1994, eq. 8) adds that only jumps enter the interface state, so the reference state can be subtracted before the projection (eq. 8.24). Colella and Glaz (1985, p. 278) and Colella (eq. 2.11, in a paper the text does not name) take as reference state the part of the profile seen by the fastest wave moving towards the interface, $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{i,L} = \mathbf{q}^n_i + \tfrac{1}{2}[1 - (\Delta t/\Delta x) \max(\lambda^{(+)}_i, 0)] \Delta \mathbf{q}_i$ (eq. 8.25). Projecting the remainder gives $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{i,L} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t/\Delta x) \sum_{\nu; \lambda^{(\nu)} \ge 0} [\max(\lambda^{(+)}_i, 0) - \lambda^{(\nu)}_i] (\mathbf{l}^{(\nu)}_i \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}_i) \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}_i$ (eqs. 8.26 to 8.28), equivalent to expressions of Saltzman (1994, p. 161) and Colella (1990, p. 191). The right state uses $\min(\lambda^{(-)}_{i+1}, 0)$, of the leftmost wave, in the same way (eqs. 8.29 and 8.30). Since zone $i$ supplies both the right state at $x_{i-1/2}$ and the left state at $x_{i+1/2}$, codes compute its eigensystem once and build both states in one loop over the zones (fig. 8.3). Some sources write $\beta^{(\nu)} = \mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}$ and the states as combinations of the $\beta^{(\nu)}$, with variations depending on whether $\rho$ or $\tau$ is the primitive variable.

#### 2.5.12 Piecewise parabolic reconstruction

The piecewise parabolic method (PPM) of Colella and Woodward (1984, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(84)90143-8) reconstructs a parabola in each zone, which is more accurate than a line, although the prediction in time keeps the method second order (§8.2.3, fig. 8.4). The text follows Miller and Colella (2002, eqs. 88 to 90). After the projection, eq. 8.24 reads $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_+ - \sum_{\nu; \lambda^{(\nu)} \ge 0} \mathbf{l}^{(\nu)}_i \cdot \{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_+ - [\mathbf{q}^n_i + \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - (\Delta t/\Delta x) \lambda^{(\nu)}_i) \Delta \mathbf{q}_i]\} \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}_i$ (eq. 8.31). The term in square brackets is the average of the reconstruction over the part of the zone from which wave $\nu$ reaches the interface in a step, exactly so for a linear profile (eq. 8.32). With $\sigma^{(\nu)} = |\lambda^{(\nu)}| \Delta t/\Delta x$, a parabola enters through $\mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_+(\mathbf{q}_i)$, its average over the last $\sigma^{(\nu)} \Delta x$ of the zone (eq. 8.33, fig. 8.5; Almgren et al. 2010, arXiv:1005.0114, eq. 31), and $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L} = \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_+ - \sum_{\nu; \lambda^{(\nu)} \ge 0} \mathbf{l}^{(\nu)}_i \cdot [\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_+ - \mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_+(\mathbf{q}_i)] \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}_i$ (eq. 8.34). Miller and Colella take the reference state $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_+ = \mathcal{I}^{(+)}_+(\mathbf{q}_i)$ if $u + c > 0$ and $\mathbf{q}_i$ otherwise (eq. 8.35). Where the wave does not approach the interface, the original paper uses the limit of the parabola at the interface instead of $\mathbf{q}_i$, while the Castro paper always uses $\mathbf{q}_i$; for a linear system, the choice would not matter. The parabola is $\mathbf{q}(x) = \mathbf{q}_- + \xi (\Delta \mathbf{q} + \mathbf{q}_6 (1 - \xi))$, with $\xi = (x - x_{i-1/2})/\Delta x$, the edge values $\mathbf{q}_\pm$, $\Delta \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}_+ - \mathbf{q}_-$ and $\mathbf{q}_6 = 6[\mathbf{q}_i - (\mathbf{q}_- + \mathbf{q}_+)/2]$ (eqs. 8.36 to 8.38). Its average over the zone is $\mathbf{q}_i$, and over the left and right halves $\mathbf{q}_i \mp \Delta \mathbf{q}/4$ (eq. 8.40). The edge values are computed and limited as in the original paper, and the integral follows in closed form, $\mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_+ = \mathbf{q}_{+,i} - (\sigma^{(\nu)}_i/2)[\Delta \mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_{6,i}(1 - 2\sigma^{(\nu)}_i/3)]$ (eq. 8.39). Multiplying out the projection recovers the form of the original paper, $u_L = \tilde{u}_+ + C(\beta^{(+)} - \beta^{(-)})$ with $\beta^{(\pm)} = \mp [(\tilde{u}_+ - \mathcal{I}^{(\pm)}_+(u)) \pm (\tilde{p}_+ - \mathcal{I}^{(\pm)}_+(p))/C]/(2C)$, in which the contact carries no velocity jump (eqs. 8.41 to 8.46; Colella and Woodward 1984, eqs. 3.6 and 3.7). The original paper evaluates $C$ in the reference state. The right state at $x_{i-1/2}$ uses $\mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_-$, the average over the first $\sigma^{(\nu)} \Delta x$ of the zone, with $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_- = \mathcal{I}^{(-)}_-(\mathbf{q}_i)$ if $u - c < 0$ and $\mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_- = \mathbf{q}_{-,i} + (\sigma^{(\nu)}_i/2)[\Delta \mathbf{q}_i + \mathbf{q}_{6,i}(1 - 2\sigma^{(\nu)}_i/3)]$, as in eq. 31b of the Castro paper (eqs. 8.47 to 8.50). Newer limiters, which change only the edge values, avoid clipping the parabolas at smooth extrema (Colella and Sekora 2008, doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2008.03.034).

#### 2.5.13 Flattening, contact steepening and characteristic limiting

Shocks steepen themselves, as their characteristics converge, and the methods above can make them too steep (§8.2.4). Flattening adds dissipation at shocks, so that they spread over about two zones. A multi-dimensional procedure that examines the pressure and velocity returns a coefficient $\chi \in [0, 1]$ that multiplies the limited slopes, with $\chi = 1$ for no flattening and $\chi = 0$ for a first-order method, as in Saltzman (1994). The interface states are then blended as $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,\{L,R\}} \leftarrow (1 - \chi) \mathbf{q}_i + \chi \mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,\{L,R\}}$ (eq. 8.51). Flattening widens the stencil of piecewise linear and parabolic reconstruction to four ghost cells on each side, since it looks at the pressure two zones away and is needed in the first two ghost cells. Contacts do not steepen, as the velocity does not jump across the middle wave and its characteristics cannot converge. The original PPM paper steepens them artificially, which works well in one dimension but can cause problems in several. Opinion is split, and many argue that a need for contact steepening signals too little resolution. Some authors limit the characteristic variables instead of the primitive ones (§8.2.5; Stone et al. 2008, eqs. 37 and 38): the slopes $\Delta w^{(\nu)} = \mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{q}$ are limited, and $\Delta \mathbf{q} = \sum_\nu \overline{\Delta w}^{(\nu)} \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$ is recovered from the limited slopes (eqs. 8.52 and 8.53). This follows the formalism of advection more closely, but the recovered primitive variables can leave their physical range, for example with a negative density.

#### 2.5.14 Riemann solvers and the conservative update

The Riemann solver returns the interface state $\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2} = \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,L}, \mathbf{q}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,R})$ at every interface (eq. 8.54, fig. 8.6, §8.3), by the sampling of 2.5.8. The exact solution is expensive, so approximate solvers estimate the wave speeds and the star state. Some assume two shocks or two rarefactions, which fixes the form of the equation for $p_\star$. Two-shock solvers such as that of Colella and Glaz (1985) are common in astrophysics, and for Sod's problem their curves lie close to the exact ones, except at small $p$, where the solution is a rarefaction (fig. 8.7). Solvers for general equations of state often carry extra thermodynamic information to the interfaces, which overconstrains the system but avoids costly calls to the equation of state. For a transonic rarefaction, most approximate solvers interpolate linearly between the outer state and the star state instead of resolving the fan. The flux follows from the interface state as $\mathbf{F} = (\rho u, \rho u^2 + p, u p/(\gamma - 1) + \rho u^3/2 + u p)^\intercal$ (eq. 8.55). The HLL family instead approximates the fluxes directly, from estimates of the wave speeds and the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions, and its speed estimates must hold for a general equation of state. The conservative update is that of eq. 8.1 (eq. 8.56, §8.4). The time step is limited by the time the fastest wave takes to cross a zone, $\Delta t < \min \Delta x_d/(|\mathbf{U} \cdot \mathbf{e}_d| + c)$ over all zones and directions $d$ (eq. 8.57), the limit for CTU (2.3.16). Codes often also limit the growth of the time step, typically to a factor of 1.2 per step, and scale the first step down, say by a factor of 10, so that the step works up to the CFL limit over a few steps. Colella and Woodward (1984) report oscillations behind slow-moving shocks and add artificial viscosity, dissipation that acts at converging flows, since flattening alone does not suffice. A multi-dimensional analogue of their eq. 4.5 modifies the fluxes (§8.4.1). **[GK]** The HLL solver of Harten, Lax and van Leer (1983, doi:10.1137/1025002) assumes two waves enclosing a single intermediate state, and HLLC restores the contact wave between them (Toro, Spruce and Speares 1994, doi:10.1007/BF01414629).

#### 2.5.15 Boundary conditions

Boundary conditions fill the ghost cells, indexed here from the first valid zone lo (§8.5). Outflow gives all variables a zero gradient, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathrm{lo}-1,j} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathrm{lo},j}$ (eq. 8.58). It is imperfect, since some waves of the Riemann problem at the boundary can still enter the domain; only in supersonic flow do all waves point outwards. Reflecting boundaries, for solid walls and symmetry planes, mirror all variables across the boundary and reverse the normal velocity, so that the ghost cell $\mathrm{lo} - 1 - k$ takes the state of zone $\mathrm{lo} + k$ with $\rho u$ negated (eqs. 8.59 and 8.60). Inflow specifies the state on the boundary itself rather than at a zone centre, which modified reconstruction stencils near the boundary can account for. A hydrostatic boundary at the base of an atmosphere supplies the pressure that holds the atmosphere against gravity while letting acoustic waves pass (Zingale et al. 2002, doi:10.1086/342754).

#### 2.5.16 Unsplit methods in several dimensions

In two dimensions, the equations read $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_t + [\mathbf{F}^{(x)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})]_x + [\mathbf{F}^{(y)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})]_y = 0$, with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}} = (\rho, \rho u, \rho v, \rho E)^\intercal$, $\mathbf{F}^{(x)} = (\rho u, \rho u u + p, \rho v u, \rho u E + u p)^\intercal$ and $\mathbf{F}^{(y)} = (\rho v, \rho v u, \rho v v + p, \rho v E + v p)^\intercal$ (eqs. 8.61 and 8.62, §8.6). No transformation turns the system into characteristic variables, since the two Jacobians cannot be diagonalised at once. For the same reason, slopes are limited one direction at a time, and the multi-dimensional limiting of Bell, Dawson and Shubin (1988) for advection does not carry over. The unsplit method predicts the edge states by a Taylor expansion of $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$ in space and time, in which the time derivative brings in a transverse flux derivative. In the notation of Colella (1990), $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} - (\Delta t/2)\, \partial \mathbf{F}^{(y)}/\partial y$, with the normal state $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^n_{i,j} + \tfrac{1}{2}[1 - (\Delta t/\Delta x) \mathbf{A}^{(x)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}})] \Delta \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$ (eqs. 8.63 to 8.67). In the primitive variables $(\rho, u, v, p)$, each direction has a $4 \times 4$ matrix, with the eigenvalues $u - c$, $u$, $u$ and $u + c$ for $x$; the extra eigenvalue $u$ belongs to the transverse velocity, which is advected with the flow (eqs. 8.68 and 8.69). The normal states are one-dimensional and follow from the reconstruction of 2.5.11 and 2.5.12 in the primitive variables (eqs. 8.70 and 8.71). For the transverse term, Colella (1990) evaluates the transverse fluxes either with the zone-centred state (his eq. 2.13) or with the normal states (his eq. 2.15), each with a transverse Riemann problem, and the text prefers the second. The transverse states $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^T_{i,j \pm 1/2} = \mathcal{R}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{n+1/2}_{i,j \pm 1/2,L}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}}^{n+1/2}_{i,j \pm 1/2,R})$ give $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,L}) - (\Delta t/2)[\mathbf{F}^{(y)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^T_{i,j+1/2}) - \mathbf{F}^{(y)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^T_{i,j-1/2})]/\Delta y$, and the right state uses the transverse states of zone $i + 1$ (eqs. 8.72 to 8.75). A normal Riemann problem then gives the interface state and the flux $\mathbf{F}^{(x),n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j}$, so each interface needs two Riemann solves (eqs. 8.76 and 8.77). In the Riemann solver, the transverse velocity is taken from the left or right state by the sign of the contact speed. The conservative update adds the flux differences of both directions (eq. 8.78). In three dimensions, Saltzman (1994) adds further transverse Riemann problems to couple the corners (§8.6.1).

#### 2.5.17 Source terms

A source $\mathbf{H}$, as in $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_t + [\mathbf{F}^{(x)}]_x + [\mathbf{F}^{(y)}]_y = \mathbf{H}$, enters the prediction through the time derivative and adds $(\Delta t/2) \mathbf{H}_{i,j}$, with the zone-centred source, to the interface states, which is second-order accurate (eqs. 8.79 to 8.82, §8.7). This is the approach of Miller and Colella (2002), and it resembles a second-order Euler step for ODEs. Alternatively, the source enters the characteristic tracing, as in the original PPM paper. For gravity, the primitive equations gain $\mathbf{G} = (0, g, 0)^\intercal$ on the right-hand side, which acts on the velocity only (eq. 8.83). A parabola of $g$ in each zone gives the averages $\mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_\pm(g)$ carried by each wave, and the projection becomes $\sum_\nu \mathbf{l}^{(\nu)} \cdot [\tilde{\mathbf{q}} - \mathcal{I}^{(\nu)}_\pm(\mathbf{q}) - (\Delta t/2) \mathbf{G}] \mathbf{r}^{(\nu)}$ (eqs. 8.84 and 8.85). Only the velocity changes, by the terms $(\Delta t/2) \mathcal{I}^{(\mp)}_+(g)$ added to eq. 8.43 (eq. 8.86). The original paper has $\Delta t\, \mathbf{G}$ in the projection, which the text takes for a misprint: with both waves moving towards the interface, the source added to the left state is $(\Delta t/4)[\mathcal{I}^{(-)}_+(g) + \mathcal{I}^{(+)}_+(g)]$, which for a constant $g$ equals the $(\Delta t/2) g$ of the Taylor expansion. The original also puts $\mathbf{G}$ on the left-hand side, with the opposite sign. The conservative update needs the source centred in time, usually as the average $(\Delta t/2)[\mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^n_{i,j}) + \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{n+1}_{i,j})]$ (eq. 8.87). This looks implicit, but the equations can often be updated in sequence. For constant gravity in one dimension, $\mathbf{H} = (0, \rho g, \rho u g)^\intercal$, and the density, then the momentum with $(\Delta t/2)(\rho^n + \rho^{n+1}) g$, then the energy with $(\Delta t/2)[(\rho u)^n + (\rho u)^{n+1}] g$ are updated without implicit coupling (eqs. 8.88 to 8.90). Sources such as the Coriolis force need an implicit update, but one local to a zone, which can be solved analytically.

#### 2.5.18 Simple geometries

Spherical and cylindrical geometries capture three-dimensional volume effects in fewer dimensions (§8.8). In one-dimensional spherical geometry, the coordinate is the radius and the zones are shells whose volume grows outwards; the geometry enters both the interface states and the conservative update. The text writes the system as $\partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}/\partial t + r^{-2}\, \partial (r^2 \mathbf{F})/\partial r = 0$, or $\partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}/\partial t + \partial \mathbf{F}/\partial r = -2\mathbf{F}/r$ (eqs. 8.91 and 8.92). The primitive equations gain the sources $-2\rho u/r$ for the density and $-2\Gamma_1 p u/r$ for the pressure, while the velocity equation is unchanged (eqs. 8.93 to 8.95), and these sources enter the interface states as in 2.5.17. In the conservative update, the pressure term of the momentum equation is a gradient, not a divergence, and has other geometric factors. The update therefore weighs the fluxes with the face areas $A_{i-1/2} = r_{i-1/2}^2$ and the volumes $V_i = r_i^2 \Delta r$, and divides the pressure difference by $\Delta r$ (eqs. 8.96 to 8.100). Two-dimensional axisymmetric models use the $r$ and $z$ coordinates of a cylinder on a grid that is still Cartesian, imagined as rotated about the axis to form volumes (fig. 8.8). The system is $\partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}/\partial t + r^{-1}\, \partial (r \mathbf{F}^{(r)})/\partial r + \partial \mathbf{F}^{(z)}/\partial z = 0$, or the Cartesian form with the source $-\mathbf{F}^{(r)}/r$ (eqs. 8.101 and 8.102). The factors $A_{i-1/2,j} = r_{i-1/2} \Delta z$ and $V_{i,j} = r_i \Delta r \Delta z$ reproduce the cylindrical divergence (eqs. 8.103 to 8.106), and the pressure again needs separate treatment. Both cases fit the divergence $r^{-\alpha}\, \partial (r^\alpha \phi^{(r)})/\partial r$, with $\alpha = 1$ for axisymmetry and $\alpha = 2$ for spherical symmetry, and the geometric source $-\alpha \mathbf{F}/r$ (eq. 8.107). **[GK]** Eqs. 8.91, 8.92, 8.101 and 8.102 hold for mass and energy, but not for the momentum, in which the pressure enters as a gradient. The geometric source of the radial momentum is $-\alpha \rho u^2/r$, not $-\alpha(\rho u^2 + p)/r$, as the velocity equation 8.94 and the update of eq. 8.97 reflect. Eq. 8.104 gives the area of the z faces as $r_i \Delta z$, but with $V_{i,j} = r_i \Delta r \Delta z$, the z flux difference needs $A_{i,j-1/2} = r_i \Delta r$, the exact area of the annulus per radian. Eq. 8.100 approximates the exact volume of a shell per steradian, $(r_{i+1/2}^3 - r_{i-1/2}^3)/3 = r_i^2 \Delta r + \Delta r^3/12$, to second order.

#### 2.5.19 Test problems

Problems with analytic solutions make the best tests (§8.9). Shock tubes are Riemann problems, whose evolution is the solution of 2.5.4 to 2.5.8, and their initial states can produce any combination of shocks and rarefactions (§8.9.1). Flipping the initial states from left to right should mirror the solution to round-off. A common cause of broken symmetry is a test such as `if u > 0`, whose other branch takes $u = 0$ together with $u < 0$; testing $u < 0$ and catching $u = 0$ in a final branch avoids the bias. With their initial discontinuities, shock tubes drop limited methods to first order and suit convergence tests poorly. For a general equation of state, the Riemann problem can still be solved exactly, and Zingale and Katz (2015, arXiv:1501.01923) give shock tubes for a stellar equation of state. The tests shown use 128 zones, $\mathcal{C} = 0.8$ and the CGF Riemann solver, which §8.11 attributes to Colella, Glaz and Ferguson. Sod's problem with $\gamma = 1.4$ (eq. 8.108) gives a left-moving rarefaction and a right-moving contact and shock; the shock is weak, but the problem demonstrates the types of waves (figs. 8.9 and 8.10, for piecewise constant and parabolic reconstruction). The double rarefaction of Toro (1997), with $\rho = 1$ and $p = 0.4$ on both sides and $u = -2$ on the left and $u = 2$ on the right (eq. 8.109), starts from diverging flow, which the text describes as creating a vacuum in the middle (figs. 8.11 and 8.12). **[GK]** A true vacuum needs $u_R - u_L \ge 2(c_L + c_R)/(\gamma - 1)$, for which eqs. 7.55 and 7.56 reach $p_\star = 0$, and this is 7.48 for $\gamma = 1.4$. With $u_R - u_L = 4$, eq. 7.92 gives a near-vacuum with $p_\star = 0.0019$ and $\rho_\star = 0.022$.

The Sedov or Sedov-Taylor blast wave (Sedov 1959) deposits an energy $E_\mathrm{expl}$ at a point of a uniform medium, and a spherical shock moves outwards and evacuates the centre (§8.9.2). It has an analytic solution, for which the text points to a code of Kamm and Timmes (2007), and it tests the geometric factors of one-dimensional spherical and two-dimensional axisymmetric geometry. Energy put into a single zone would change its extent with the resolution and, in Cartesian coordinates, be squared off. Following Omang, Børve and Trulsen (2006, doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2005.08.023), the energy instead inflates a region of radius $r_\mathrm{init}$, of volume $V_\mathrm{init} = 4\pi r_\mathrm{init}^3/3$ for a spherical and $\pi r_\mathrm{init}^2$ for a cylindrical blast, at the pressure $p = (E_\mathrm{expl}/V_\mathrm{init})(\gamma - 1)$ (eq. 8.110). In one-dimensional spherical geometry with piecewise parabolic reconstruction, the shock position and the density and velocity profiles agree well with the analytic solution, while the pressure behind the shock is a little low, likely an artefact of the initialisation (fig. 8.13). In two Cartesian dimensions the blast is cylindrical. Density and pressure look symmetric, but the velocity magnitude and the internal energy show an imprint of the grid along the axes, and a bump in internal energy near the origin comes from the error in computing $e$ from $E$ and $\mathbf{U}$ (fig. 8.14, with piecewise linear reconstruction, the HLLC solver and $r_\mathrm{init} = 0.01$). The angle-averaged profile follows the analytic solution (fig. 8.15).

An advection test puts a profile in the density at constant pressure, so that it moves without dynamics (§8.9.3): $\rho = (\rho_1 - \rho_0) e^{-(x - x_c)^2/\sigma^2} + \rho_0$, $u = u_0$, $p = p_0$ and $\rho E = p/(\gamma - 1) + \rho u^2/2$ (eqs. 8.111 to 8.114). The values $\rho_0 = 10^{-3}$, $\rho_1 = 1$, $p_0 = 10^{-6}$, $u_0 = 1$ and $\sigma = 0.1$ are those of Fryxell et al. (2000, doi:10.1086/317361), and the ambient density keeps quantities divided by the density well defined. After ten periods, the shape is mostly preserved, but the peak is clipped, as for pure advection (fig. 8.16). Slow-moving or stationary shocks can set up oscillations behind them, as Colella and Woodward (1984) and LeVeque (2002) discuss (§8.9.4). Setting $S = 0$ in eq. 7.90 for a right-facing shock gives the pre-shock velocity $u_r = -c_r[(\gamma + 1) p_\star/(2\gamma p_r) + (\gamma - 1)/(2\gamma)]^{1/2}$ for a chosen pre-shock density and pressure and a chosen pressure jump $p_\star/p_r$, and eqs. 7.86 and 7.89 give the post-shock state (eqs. 8.115 and 8.116). For a jump of 100 with $\gamma = 1.4$, the states $\rho_l = 5.6698$, $u_l = -1.9336$, $p_l = 100$ and $\rho_r = 1$, $u_r = -10.9636$, $p_r = 1$ give a stationary shock (eq. 8.117), and adding 0.4 to both velocities makes it move at $S = 0.4$ (eq. 8.118, fig. 8.17). Two-dimensional Riemann problems start from four quadrants with different states and follow the interaction of their waves (§8.9.5; Schulz-Rinne, Collins and Glaz 1993, doi:10.1137/0914082). Some analytic estimates exist, and the problems test the symmetry of a code in complex flow. The text uses their configuration 3, as does LeVeque (1997, doi:10.1006/jcph.1996.5603), and shows it at $t = 0.8$ (fig. 8.18).

#### 2.5.20 The method of lines for the Euler equations

As for advection (2.3.12), the method of lines leaves the time integration to an ODE integrator instead of tracing characteristics (§8.10). The semi-discrete system is $d\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i,j}/dt = -[\mathbf{F}^{(x)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1/2,j}) - \mathbf{F}^{(x)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i-1/2,j})]/\Delta x - [\mathbf{F}^{(y)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i,j+1/2}) - \mathbf{F}^{(y)}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i,j-1/2})]/\Delta y$, with interface states at the current time (eq. 8.119). With second-order Runge-Kutta, a first slope $\mathbf{k}_1$ from $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^n$ and a second $\mathbf{k}_2$ from $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^n + \mathbf{k}_1/2$ give $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{n+1} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^n + \mathbf{k}_2$ (eqs. 8.120 to 8.122). The interface states come from a one-dimensional reconstruction and a Riemann problem at each interface. They have no transverse terms, which the second stage brings in implicitly, and no characteristic projection, since nothing is predicted in time. The order in time should match that in space, and the popular fourth-order Runge-Kutta method suggests a fourth-order spatial method. Beyond second order, however, a zone average no longer equals the value at the zone centre, $\langle \phi \rangle_i \ne \phi_i$, which matters, for instance, in converting between conserved and primitive variables; McCorquodale and Colella (2011) present a fully fourth-order method. With Runge-Kutta in several dimensions, the time step is more restrictive than with characteristic tracing: Titarev and Toro (2004) claim $0 < \mathcal{C} < 1/2$ in two dimensions and $0 < \mathcal{C} < 1/3$ in three, as in 2.3.17. Multiphysics usually splits the processes and treats them in turn, which standard methods do to second order in time, while higher order is harder.

#### 2.5.21 Temperature and internal energy

The pure Euler equations need no temperature, but source terms such as reactions, and complex equations of state, do (§8.11.1). It follows from the equation of state as $T = T(e, \rho)$, with $e = E - u^2/2$ (eqs. 8.123 and 8.124). Where kinetic energy dominates, at high Mach numbers, the subtraction can make $e$ negative through truncation errors in $u$ relative to $E$, and a floor for $e$ or another way of deriving it is needed. Bryan et al. (1995, doi:10.1016/0010-4655(94)00191-4) evolve both the total and the internal energy in each zone, and use the internal energy where the kinetic energy dominates. The cost is conservation, since the internal energy is not a conserved quantity, and switching to it introduces errors in the conservation of energy.

#### 2.5.22 General equations of state

The methods above assume a constant-gamma equation of state. A general one, such as that of degenerate electrons, is costly to call, and methods reduce the calls by carrying extra thermodynamic information with the primitive variables, into both the interface states and the Riemann problem (§8.11.2). The classic prescription of Colella and Glaz (1985) carries the index $\gamma_e = p/(\rho e) + 1$ (eq. 8.125), which by eqs. 7.22 and 7.99 evolves as $D\gamma_e/Dt = (\gamma_e - 1)(\gamma_e - \Gamma_1) \nabla \cdot \mathbf{U}$ (eq. 8.126; Colella and Glaz 1985, eq. 26). Its interface values enter their Riemann solver. $\Gamma_1$, which sets the sound speed, can be carried to the interfaces piecewise constant without losing second order, since it does not appear in the fluxes (Colella and Glaz 1985, p. 277). For tracing in $\mathbf{q} = (\tau, u, p, \gamma_e)^\intercal$, the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ has the rows $(u, -\tau, 0, 0)$, $(0, u, \tau, 0)$, $(0, c^2/\tau, u, 0)$ and $(0, -\alpha, 0, u)$, with $\alpha = (\gamma_e - 1)(\gamma_e - \Gamma_1)$ (eqs. 8.127 and 8.128). Its right eigenvectors are $\mathbf{r}^{(\mp)} = (1, \pm c/\tau, -c^2/\tau^2, \alpha/\tau)^\intercal$, $\mathbf{r}^{(\circ)} = (1, 0, 0, 0)^\intercal$ and $\mathbf{r}^{(\circ,\gamma_e)} = (0, 0, 0, 1)^\intercal$, and its left eigenvectors include $\mathbf{l}^{(\mp)} = (0, \pm \tau/2c, -\tau^2/2c^2, 0)$ and $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ,\gamma_e)} = (0, 0, \alpha\tau/c^2, 1)$ (eqs. 8.129 to 8.133).

Alternatively, Castro follows an unpublished manuscript of Colella, Glaz and Ferguson and predicts $\rho e$ to the interfaces along with $\rho$, $u$ and $p$, which is simple since $\rho e$ obeys the conservation-like eq. 7.99. This over-specifies the thermodynamics but removes the need for $\gamma_e$. For $\mathbf{q} = (\rho, u, p, \rho e)^\intercal$, the new row of $\mathbf{A}$ is $(0, \rho h, 0, u)$, with the specific enthalpy $h$, and the eigenvalues are $u - c$, $u$, $u$ and $u + c$. The outer right eigenvectors gain the component $h$, and $\mathbf{r}^{(\circ,\rho e)} = (0, 0, 0, 1)^\intercal$ and $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ,\rho e)} = (0, 0, -h/c^2, 1)$ join the eigenvectors of the Euler equations (eqs. 8.134 to 8.137). Only $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ,\rho e)}$ has a $\rho e$ component, so $\rho e$ does not mix into the other variables, but it jumps across three waves. Summing the projection gives the interface state of $\rho e$ used in Castro (eq. 8.138). In simple geometries, $\rho e$ gains the source $-\alpha \rho h u/r$, where $\alpha$ is now the geometric index of eq. 8.107, handled as in 2.5.18 (eqs. 8.139 and 8.140). An unsplit formulation carries the extra variable from the primitive back to the conserved state and adds transverse terms to its interface states. Castro uses the state $(\rho, \rho \mathbf{U}, \rho E, p)$ and adds the transverse terms of the multi-dimensional form of eq. 7.22 to the normal states of $p$. **[GK]** Eq. 8.131 repeats $\mathbf{l}^{(-)}$ for $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ)}$. Eq. 8.128 and the normalisation $\mathbf{l}^{(i)} \cdot \mathbf{r}^{(j)} = \delta_{ij}$ give $\mathbf{l}^{(\circ)} = (1, 0, \tau^2/c^2, 0)$.

#### 2.5.23 WENO for the Euler equations

The flux splitting of 2.4.6 generalises directly to systems (§8.12). The simplest and most diffusive form takes $\alpha$ as the largest characteristic speed over all waves and reconstructs the split fluxes $\mathbf{F}^{(\pm)}$ component by component with WENO. This can oscillate even in moderate tests. A safer approach projects the split fluxes onto the characteristic fields with the left eigenvectors of $\mathbf{A} = \partial \mathbf{F}/\partial \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}$, reconstructs these characteristic fluxes, and transforms back with the right eigenvectors. Each characteristic flux carries a single wave, so the reconstruction behaves as in the scalar case, and close waves of different families interfere less. The Jacobian is needed at every point where a flux is computed. The best state for it is unclear: the arithmetic average of the neighbouring states is common, but more complex choices can do better. On Sod's problem with 64 zones and a CFL number of 0.5, WENO with $r = 3$ and $r = 5$ captures the discontinuities, especially the contact, less cleanly than PPM, which is designed for hydrodynamics (figs. 8.19 and 8.20, against fig. 8.10). Its higher order would show best on smoother tests. Its oscillations grow with the order and are reduced by characteristic-wise reconstruction, most visibly in the internal energy for $r = 5$. On the double rarefaction, WENO captures the edges of the rarefactions well with few points, despite the diffusive Lax-Friedrichs splitting. Its artificial heating at the trivial contact in the centre, however, is about as bad as with piecewise constant reconstruction and far from PPM; a higher order helps little, and a less diffusive splitting would be needed (fig. 8.21).

The main advantage of WENO is that it keeps its high order in several dimensions with dimensional splitting (§8.12.1). The finite-difference form needs no transverse Riemann problems, and with the method of lines the directional updates are computed separately and applied together by the integrator, so the order of the sweeps does not matter. The formal order is kept, but absolute accuracy can suffer, as the advection of a top hat shows against lower-order methods with transverse Riemann problems. The global Lax-Friedrichs splitting can be too diffusive. A local splitting, with $\alpha$ the largest characteristic speed within the stencil of each point, is less diffusive and slightly more expensive, and Roe-type splittings are possible but not always stable. **[GK]** Shu (1997, §2.3.5, Procedure 2.10) sets out the characteristic-wise splitting. He takes the state of the Jacobian at $x_{i+1/2}$ as the arithmetic mean of the neighbours or as a Roe average, which satisfies $\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1}) - \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_i) = \mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1/2})(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{i+1} - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_i)$ (his eqs. 2.98 and 2.99; Roe 1981, doi:10.1016/0021-9991(81)90128-5). Each characteristic field gets its own splitting coefficient, the largest magnitude of its eigenvalue over the grid.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture draws on §3.4, §13.6, §13.7 and appendices A to C of the text.

### 3.1 Data layout on the grid

A finite-volume grid maps onto a multi-dimensional array (§3.4). In NumPy, the state of a one-dimensional grid with $n_x$ zones and $n_g$ ghost cells at each end is an array of shape $(n_x + 2 n_g, n_\mathrm{var})$, for $n_\mathrm{var}$ state variables per zone. The interior runs from index $i_\mathrm{lo} = n_g$ to $i_\mathrm{hi} = n_x + n_g - 1$, and loops over the domain use these bounds. Fluxes live on the zone edges, which half-integer indices cannot address, so by convention element $i$ of a flux array holds the flux $F_{i-1/2}$ on the left edge of zone $i$.
