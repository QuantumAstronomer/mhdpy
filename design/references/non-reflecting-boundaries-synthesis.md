# Synthesis of non-reflecting boundaries

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on boundary conditions that let waves leave a computational domain, above all where the flow crosses the boundary subsonically, kept in `reference-works/Non-Reflecting Boundaries/Docs`. The collection is gathered around the review of [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, [doi:10.1146/annurev.fluid.36.050802.121930](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.fluid.36.050802.121930)) on artificial boundary conditions for compressible flow (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §2.3, eq. 8), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Works that the papers cite are cited as the papers cite them. Four classical papers have no free copy: those of [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), [Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) (1980) and [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987, [1990](#thompson-1990)). They are known here only as the papers of the collection restate them, and each citation of them names the paper that restates it.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004) wrote the review at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena. It appeared in volume 36 of the Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics, on pp. 315–345. The local copy is the published version, which the repository CaltechAUTHORS holds with the permission of Annual Reviews. Its 33 pages hold the text in six sections up to p. 341, with five figures and nineteen numbered equations, the literature cited up to p. 345, and two pages of the table of contents of the volume. The key words are non-reflecting, absorbing and direct numerical simulation. Colonius thanks Jeff Eldredge and Tom Hagstrom for their comments on a draft. Much of the review was written during a sabbatical at the University of Cambridge, where he thanks Ann Dowling among others (acknowledgements).

### 1.2 Aims and scope

The review treats artificial boundary conditions for inflow, outflow and far-field, or radiation, problems, with an emphasis on techniques for compressible turbulent shear flows and the sound they generate ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §1). It addresses the unsteady compressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, and its techniques largely carry over to large-eddy simulation and to the unsteady Reynolds-averaged equations. Acoustic waves matter even where the radiated sound does not, as in instabilities of combustion and in the receptivity of shear layers. A boundary that encloses the region of interest closely also saves effort, provided that it does not damage the solution.

Two situations are distinguished. Where the flow near the boundary consists of small fluctuations about a uniform state, analytical solutions of the problem outside the domain yield the boundary conditions. The solution should not depend on where the domain is truncated, which is equivalent to requiring that no waves enter through the boundary. These non-reflecting boundary conditions have been studied for some thirty years. Their conceptual issues are largely solved, although demanding extensions and details of implementation remain, and earlier reviews cover them ([Givoli](#givoli-1991) 1991, [Tsynkov](#tsynkov-1998) 1998, [Hagstrom](#hagstrom-1999) 1999). Where the flow near the boundary is not a small disturbance of a uniform state, as in turbulent shear flows and in flows through pipes, nozzles, diffusers and turbomachinery, little theory exists. Truncating the domain then ignores the interaction of the region with its surroundings, often with unrealistic effects. A truncated mixing layer, for instance, can oscillate by itself through feedback from downstream ([Buell and Huerre](#buell-huerre-1988) 1988). Practical computations therefore use boundary conditions of low order. For better accuracy they sometimes add sacrificial regions next to the boundary, in which the equations are modified, called absorbing layers, fringe regions, buffer zones or sponges. Their free parameters must be tuned anew for each flow.

The review explores the gap between the largely mathematical theory of non-reflecting conditions and the largely empirical practice of crude conditions with buffer regions. It stresses that mathematics alone cannot fill this gap, which it calls an open problem of modelling, no less challenging, and arguably no less important, than the modelling of turbulence below the grid scale. §2 treats boundary conditions linearised about a uniform flow, §3 the effects of discretisation, §4 nonlinear characteristic conditions and §5 absorbing layers, and §6 summarises.

The summary finds the theory largely settled where the flow near the boundary can be linearised: local and non-local techniques give both accuracy and stability (§6). Stubborn problems remain: large gradients of the base flow, compatibility at the corners and edges of Cartesian domains, and places on a boundary where the velocity changes sign or the flow becomes sonic. Throughout, the continuous conditions interact subtly with the closures of the discrete equations, which have more degrees of freedom and so reflect additional, spurious modes, and this ties the well-posedness of the continuous problem to the stability of its discretisation. Where nonlinear effects near the boundary are significant, chiefly at the outflow of turbulent flow, little theory guides the conditions. Crude one-dimensional characteristic conditions used to be the rule, and ad hoc absorbing layers have lately improved the accuracy, but all of them need several parameters and blending functions, tuned by trial and error. The review traces this to a close analogy between artificial boundary conditions and the modelling of turbulence below the grid scale: one deals with scales too large for the domain, the other with scales too small for the mesh. It calls for new approaches to this largely unexplored problem of modelling.

## 2. Numerical methods

The methods fall into eight subjects: 2.1 characteristics and the number of boundary conditions, 2.2 absorbing conditions for waves at oblique incidence, 2.3 characteristic boundary conditions, 2.4 relaxation towards the far field at subsonic outflow and inflow, 2.5 absorbing layers, 2.6 characteristic conditions in finite-volume codes with ghost cells, 2.7 magnetohydrodynamics, and 2.8 open boundaries in astrophysical simulations of clouds and winds.

### 2.1 Characteristics and the number of boundary conditions

#### 2.1.1 Waves of a uniform flow at a planar boundary

Most artificial boundary conditions for compressible flow linearise the equations about a nearly uniform ambient state, the base flow ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2, fig. 1). A source region, which may be nonlinear and may hold bodies and forces, is surrounded by a region in which its disturbances have decayed, by geometric spreading or viscous diffusion, so far that they are linear. The aim is to simulate this linear region as if it extended to infinity, either by stretching the grid without end (§5.3) or by truncating the domain at an artificial boundary. The names absorbing, non-reflecting, radiation, characteristic, artificial and transparent all denote this aim, although they sometimes imply different techniques. For an inviscid gas without heat conduction and with constant specific heats, the linearised equations decouple into an advection equation for the vorticity, an advection equation for the entropy and a convected wave equation for the pressure. These couple only through boundary conditions or nonlinear interaction. Boundary conditions for the two advection equations are almost trivial: the fluctuations of vorticity and entropy are prescribed wherever the base flow enters the domain. Codes, however, solve for the velocity, which superposes acoustic and vortical motion, and splitting it into its irrotational and solenoidal parts requires the solution of Poisson equations, which is expensive.

At a locally planar boundary, a Fourier transform along the boundary and a Laplace transform in time decouple the linearised equations (§2.1). In two dimensions, with the boundary normal to $x$, a base flow $(u_o, v_o)$ with $u_o > 0$ and fluctuations $(u', v')$ of the velocity and $p'$ of the pressure, the equations read $\mathbf{q}_t + A\mathbf{q}_x + (B + v_oI)\mathbf{q}_y = 0$ for $\mathbf{q} = (v', u' + p', u' - p')^\mathrm{T}$ (eqs. 1 and 2). Here $A = \mathrm{diag}(u_o, u_o + 1, u_o - 1)$, and $B$ has the first row $(0, 1/2, -1/2)$ and the first column $(0, 1, -1)^\mathrm{T}$, its other entries being zero. The scales make the ambient density and speed of sound 1 and the ambient pressure $1/\gamma$. The entropy fluctuation $s' = p' - \rho'$ obeys an advection equation of its own and is set aside. With the variables $k$ dual to $y$ and $s$ dual to $t$, the transformed equations become $\hat{\mathbf{q}}_x = -(s + iv_ok)M(z)\hat{\mathbf{q}}$, with $z = ik/(s + iv_ok)$ and $M(z) = A^{-1}(I + zB)$ (eq. 3). The eigenvalues of $M$ are $\lambda_1 = 1/u_o$ for the vorticity wave and $\lambda_{2,3} = (u_o \mp \mu)/(u_o^2 - 1)$ for the two acoustic waves, with $\mu(z) = [1 - z^2(1 - u_o^2)]^{1/2}$ on the standard branch. Where $\mu$ is real, the solutions are propagating waves. Waves that meet the boundary at normal incidence, $k = z = 0$, recover the one-dimensional eigenvalues $1/u_o$, $1/(u_o + 1)$ and $1/(u_o - 1)$.

With $u_o > 0$, the boundary on the left is an inflow and that on the right an outflow. In supersonic flow, $u_o > 1$, all waves enter at the left and leave at the right. In subsonic flow, $0 < u_o < 1$, the vorticity wave and one acoustic wave travel to the right and enter at the inflow, while the other acoustic wave travels to the left, enters at the outflow and leaves at the inflow. A non-reflecting condition sets the amplitudes of the entering waves to zero. Solutions that do not propagate, where $\mu$ is imaginary, are labelled as right- or left-going algebraically, as in the theory of well-posedness ([Higdon](#higdon-1986) 1986). If $M$ can be block-diagonalised, as it can for the Euler equations, a matrix $Q$ with the blocks $Q^\mathrm{R}$ and $Q^\mathrm{L}$ of the right- and left-going solutions brings it into the form $QMQ^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(\Lambda^\mathrm{R}, \Lambda^\mathrm{L})$ (eq. 4). The entropy wave, which the analysis sets aside, travels with the flow like the vorticity wave. Counted with it, a subsonic inflow in two dimensions takes three conditions and a subsonic outflow one. If $u_o = 0$, the boundary is called characteristic, a degenerate case that needs a separate but straightforward treatment (§2.2). In turbulent shear flows, a small flow of entrainment usually keeps $u_o$ away from zero at the boundaries.

#### 2.1.2 Well-posedness

Boundary conditions must leave the problem well posed ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.2). The uniform Kreiss condition is sufficient ([Kreiss](#kreiss-1970) 1970, [Higdon](#higdon-1986) 1986), but stricter than necessary: it suffices that the reflection matrices of 2.2.1 stay bounded for all complex $z$ (Higdon 1986). Accuracy further demands that they be small on the imaginary axis of $z$, where the solutions are waves. As [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, [1990](#giles-1990)) first noted, the most direct approximation of the exact inflow condition is always ill posed. Relaxing the requirements on the matrix of the condition cures this, and yields a family of inflow conditions with three parameters ([Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) 2000), of which only a few have been tried.

#### 2.1.3 Characteristics in nonlinear and viscous flow

In one dimension, the classical method of characteristics identifies and separates the waves that travel to the left and to the right even in nonlinear flow ([Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) 1979, as restated by [Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). The three characteristic curves in the $x$–$t$ plane have the slopes $u$, $u + a$ and $u - a$, with the speed of sound $a$. A boundary condition is needed for every characteristic that enters the domain, by the sign of its slope. The quantities carried along the characteristics are the Riemann invariants, and conditions on them are called Riemann-invariant or characteristic conditions. Linearised about a uniform flow, they reduce to the one-dimensional conditions of 2.2.2 (eq. 8). In nonlinear flow, the incoming characteristics must carry information about the whole flow outside the domain, not merely about disturbances of a base state, and whether a characteristic enters or leaves can change along a boundary and in time. Only rarely does a solution of the exterior problem supply the incoming information, which must usually be set from little knowledge of the exterior.

Viscous flow takes a different number of conditions at inflow and outflow than inviscid flow ([Strikwerda](#strikwerda-1977) 1977, [Oliger and Sundström](#oliger-sundstrom-1978) 1978). The additional conditions usually set certain gradients, or combinations of them, to zero. Formulations so far seek a well-posed continuous problem and a stable discrete one, and avoid viscous boundary layers at the inflow and outflow by requiring that the inviscid conditions be recovered smoothly as the viscosity vanishes. Non-reflection of viscous conditions has been analysed only in one dimension, even when linearised. There, non-reflecting conditions exist that are strongly well posed, remain strongly stable when discretised, recover the inviscid conditions at infinite Reynolds number, and are maximally dissipative: without forcing at the boundary, they drive the energy down faster than any other one-dimensional condition of their general form. Many codes simply apply inviscid characteristic conditions and close the viscous derivatives with one-sided differences, which is probably justified only at high Reynolds numbers.

### 2.2 Absorbing conditions for waves at oblique incidence

#### 2.2.1 Exact conditions and their non-locality

Exact non-reflecting conditions set the amplitudes of all incoming waves to zero: $Q^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ at the inflow and $Q^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ at the outflow ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.2, eq. 5). A forcing on the right-hand sides would instead excite chosen entropic, vortical and acoustic waves. Since the block-diagonalisation of $M$ is not unique, eq. 5 is a family of exact conditions, whose matrices [Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) (2000) and [Hagstrom and Goodrich](#hagstrom-goodrich-2002) (2002) give in full. Approximate matrices $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}$ and $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}$ let some reflection through, measured by reflection coefficients that give the amplitudes of the incoming waves in terms of those of the outgoing ones. With the matrix $T(z)$ of the eigenvectors of $M$, the decoupled amplitudes are $\hat{\mathbf{f}} = T^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ (eq. 6). A subsonic outflow has the two coefficients $\rho_{\Omega \to a^-}$ and $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-}$, of the upstream acoustic wave $a^-$ produced by the outgoing vorticity wave $\Omega$ and by the outgoing downstream acoustic wave $a^+$. A subsonic inflow has $\rho_{a^- \to \Omega}$ and $\rho_{a^- \to a^+}$, of the incoming waves produced by the outgoing upstream acoustic wave (eq. 7).

**[GK]** Inserting $\hat{\mathbf{q}} = T^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{R} + T^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{L}$ into the approximate outflow condition $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{L} = -(\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}T^\mathrm{L})^{-1}\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{L}T^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{R}$, and the inflow condition $\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}\hat{\mathbf{q}} = 0$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{R} = -(\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}T^\mathrm{R})^{-1}\tilde{Q}^\mathrm{R}T^\mathrm{L}\hat{\mathbf{f}}^\mathrm{L}$. Eq. 6 states these matrices without the minus sign, which leaves the size of a reflection unchanged. It names the first $\varrho^\mathrm{L}$, as belonging to the outflow, whereas the text and eq. 7 give the name $\varrho^\mathrm{L}$ to the reflection at the inflow.

The exact conditions are non-local. Through $\mu(z)$ they involve convolution integrals along the boundary and in time, which must be discretised or further approximated ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.2). The wave equation poses the same problem. Efficient evaluation of the convolutions depends on the scale invariance of the boundary, which planar, spherical, cylindrical and conical boundaries possess ([Hagstrom](#hagstrom-1999) 1999). For the wave equation, the review lists non-local conditions from the Kirchhoff integral, which suit any smooth boundary, conditions in spherical harmonics on spheres, maps from Dirichlet to Neumann data for time-harmonic problems, and the sharp rear front of waves in odd numbers of dimensions, which bounds the memory in time. For the linearised Euler equations, fewer such techniques exist. For steady flows, the method of difference potentials uses non-local boundary operators with good results ([Tsynkov](#tsynkov-1998) 1998). For unsteady flows, the convolution kernel can be approximated by a sum of exponentials, which turns the convolution into recurrences that are local in time. This was done for the linearised equations of magnetohydrodynamics at a planar boundary ([Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2001) 2001).

#### 2.2.2 Local approximations

Polynomial or rational approximations of $\mu(z)$ in eq. 5 turn the exact conditions into local differential equations at each point of the boundary ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.3). Hierarchies of such conditions, of increasing order of accuracy, were first developed for the wave equation ([Lindman](#lindman-1975) 1975, [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) 1977, [1979](#engquist-majda-1979), [Bayliss and Turkel](#bayliss-turkel-1980) 1980). Locality keeps an advantage even where efficient non-local methods now exist: a local condition can follow a non-uniform base flow by taking the density, pressure and velocity of the base flow at each point of the boundary (2.2.3). A Taylor series of $\mu(z)$ about $z = 0$ expands about normal incidence, the only incidence in one dimension. Its crudest form, $z = 0$, gives the one-dimensional conditions $(v', u' + p')^\mathrm{T} = 0$ at the inflow and $u' - p' = 0$ at the outflow (eq. 8). These are the linearised quantities that are constant along characteristics, whose nonlinear counterparts are the characteristic conditions of 2.3.

[Trefethen and Halpern](#trefethen-halpern-1986) (1986) proved that rational approximations $\mu \approx p_m(z)/q_n(z)$ of the Padé, Chebyshev and least-squares kinds give well-posed conditions provided that $m = n$ or $m = n + 2$, so that Taylor series beyond the second order are not admissible. [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, [1990](#giles-1990)) derived local conditions for the Euler equations from Taylor expansions of low order. They are strongly well posed, cause no reflection between the vorticity wave and the acoustic waves, $\rho_{a^- \to \Omega} = \rho_{\Omega \to a^-} = 0$, and reflect acoustic waves with $\rho_{a^- \to a^+} = O(z^4)$ and $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-} = O(z^2)$. [Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) (2000) obtained strongly well-posed conditions of any order under the same conditions on the rational approximation. The Padé approximant of degrees $(m, n)$ again causes no reflection between vorticity and sound, while $\rho_{a^- \to a^+} = O(z^{m+n+4})$ and $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-} = O(z^{m+n+2})$. The degrees $m = n = 0$ recover the conditions of Giles, and [Hagstrom and Goodrich](#hagstrom-goodrich-2002) (2002) derived similar conditions. The conditions of high order contain mixed derivatives in $y$ and $t$, which are discretised as systems of first-order equations with auxiliary variables.

Fig. 2 compares the reflection coefficients of local conditions for $u_o = 0.5$, against the angle between the $x$-axis and the acoustic wave that causes the reflection, or the upstream acoustic wave that the vorticity wave generates. The Padé approximants of higher order are better at every angle. The approximant of degrees $(4, 4)$ reflects less than 1 per cent of acoustic waves that meet the outflow at angles below $100^\circ$ and the inflow at angles below $45^\circ$, whereas the one-dimensional conditions keep reflections to that level only below $20^\circ$ and $5^\circ$. Every well-posed local condition reflects completely the waves whose group velocity is tangent to the boundary, where $\mu = 0$. With periodic boundaries or walls across the non-reflecting direction, this degrades long runs, since after a while only such tangent waves remain.

In domains that are not periodic, the edges and corners of a rectangular domain pose a more serious problem, since compatibility conditions must blend the conditions of the boundaries that meet. Stable treatments of corners are known only for the conditions of lowest order, although compatibility conditions exist for the wave equation. One treatment writes the conditions as closures for the derivatives normal to each boundary, using the full equations, and imposes the conditions of both boundaries at a corner. No proof of its stability exists, but experiments suggest that it is stable for the Padé approximants of degrees $(0, 0)$, $(2, 0)$ and $(2, 2)$ (§2.3, §5.1). Smooth boundaries, such as circles and spheres, would avoid corners, but the normal velocity of the base flow then changes sign along the boundary, and with it the labelling of the waves. Corners and boundaries with both inflow and outflow remain open problems.

Asymptotic expansions of the solution at large distance from the source give another kind of local condition ([Bayliss and Turkel](#bayliss-turkel-1982) 1982). Their first term is the Sommerfeld radiation condition, applied at a finite distance. For the linearised Euler equations they give conditions for outflow and for the characteristic boundary, $u_o = 0$, and [Tam and Webb](#tam-webb-1993) (1993) extended the first term to inflow boundaries. The expansion converges slowly unless the source lies at the centre of the boundary, so that a single term is inaccurate in general.

#### 2.2.3 Non-uniform base flows

A local condition can follow a non-uniform base flow through its local density, pressure and velocity, provided that the base flow varies over lengths long compared with the disturbances ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §2.4). At the inflow and outflow of turbulent shear flows this approximation of high frequency fails: the largest vortices are as large as the variations of the mean flow, and sound radiates most strongly at long wavelengths. Exact or high-order conditions then gain little, and computations of mixing layers and jets have used conditions of low order, namely one-dimensional ones, those of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990) and the first term of the asymptotic expansion. Several of them add an absorbing buffer near the outflow (2.5). In arbitrary gradients of the mean flow, acoustic, vortical and entropic waves lose their separate identities. Exact non-reflecting conditions exist for a quiescent base state stratified normal to the boundary ([Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2001) 2001). Conditions for parallel sheared flows may be tractable through a third-order wave equation for the pressure, which also governs the linear instability of such flows. For turbulent flows, the nonlinear effects of 2.3 may matter as much, especially at the outflow.

#### 2.2.4 The unified theory of Giles

[Giles](#giles-1988) (1988) sets out non-reflecting conditions for the Euler equations in a report for researchers in computational fluid dynamics who wish to implement them and to understand their theory (§1.3). The motivation is the flow through turbomachinery, whose far-field boundaries lie less than a chord from the blades (§1.1). The steady far field there holds several spatial wavenumbers, and where the flow is supersonic along its direction but subsonic along the axis, shocks propagate indefinitely and are reflected by improper conditions. Unsteadiness, moreover, enters as shocks and wakes from upstream rows of blades, which rules out the coarse far-field grids that dissipate outgoing waves around isolated airfoils. The report therefore seeks steady conditions that do not reflect steady waves such as shocks, and accurate conditions for unsteady waves. Turbomachinery also simplifies the problem: the inflow and the outflow are separate boundaries of trivial geometry, normal to the axial coordinate $x$, and the flow is periodic in the circumferential coordinate $y$, which permits a Fourier decomposition about a uniform flow. Four kinds of conditions are derived: quasi-one-dimensional ones, exact ones for a single frequency, exact steady ones and approximate unsteady ones (§1.3). Exact refers to the linear problem, whose own errors are of second order in the amplitude of the disturbances at the boundaries. Giles counts the theory a condensed restatement of earlier work, and the analysis of well-posedness its one original contribution, since the Euler equations have a multiple root with distinct eigenvectors and, at one complex frequency, a coalescence of eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The journal paper of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990) summarises this report and a second one on the implementation in a code for turbomachinery, and omits the analysis of well-posedness.

By the history of the report (§1.2), the firm foundation of the field was the analysis by [Kreiss](#kreiss-1970) (1970) of the well-posedness of initial-boundary-value problems for hyperbolic systems in several dimensions, which [Higdon](#higdon-1986) (1986) reviewed with an interpretation in terms of waves. For one-dimensional flow, characteristic theory gave the correct non-reflecting conditions early: [Hedstrom](#hedstrom-1979) (1979), as Giles restates him, derived their linear and nonlinear forms for the Euler equations with the approach through eigenvectors that the report follows. Applied in two and three dimensions by an analysis normal to the boundary that ignores all tangential derivatives, they are the quasi-one-dimensional, or normal one-dimensional, conditions, also called the method of characteristics, and remain the most common conditions in unsteady computations. [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977) derived a hierarchy of approximate conditions in several dimensions, whose first member is the one-dimensional condition. Written for mathematicians, their paper had been little taken up in computational fluid dynamics, and its conditions of higher order had been implemented only for acoustic and elastic waves and for the scalar unsteady potential equation. For linearised harmonic flows, exact conditions can be built. For steady flow past isolated airfoils, a vortex correction for the lift, and then source and doublet terms for the drag, brought the far-field boundary in from many chords to a few. The same followed from the limit of zero frequency of the ideal non-reflecting conditions, the view that unites the steady, single-frequency and unsteady conditions of the report.

The general analysis treats $\mathbf{U}_t + A\mathbf{U}_x + B\mathbf{U}_y = 0$ for a vector $\mathbf{U}$ of $N$ components and constant matrices $A$ and $B$ (§2). Waves $\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{u}e^{i(kx + ly - \omega t)}$ obey the dispersion relation $\det(-\omega I + kA + lB) = 0$, a polynomial of degree $N$ in $k$ and in $l$ (§2.1). For a growing complex frequency, $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega > 0$, the waves that travel to the right have $\mathrm{Im}\,k > 0$, and for real $\omega$ and $k$ a wave travels in the direction of its group velocity $(\partial\omega/\partial k, \partial\omega/\partial l)$. At the boundary, a solution is a sum of Fourier modes in $y$ and $t$, each a group of waves with common $\omega$ and $l$ and different $k$. Their amplitudes are measured not by the usual left eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{L}$ of $kA + lB$, which are orthogonal across different $\omega$, but by the left eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}$ of $A^{-1}(\omega I - lB)$, which are orthogonal to the right eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{R}$ of the other waves with the same $\omega$ and $l$ (§2.2). The ideal condition sets $\mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}_n\mathbf{U} = 0$ for every incoming wave $n$ (§2.3). It needs a Fourier transform in $y$ and a Laplace transform in $t$, which is difficult and expensive. Since $k/\omega$ and all eigenvectors depend only on $\lambda = l/\omega$, simpler conditions follow from assumptions on $\lambda$:

- The one-dimensional conditions take $\lambda = 0$ (§2.4). With the right and left eigenvectors $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{R}_n$ and $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_n$ of $A$, normalised so that $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_m\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{R}_n = \delta_{mn}$, the characteristic variables $c_n = \mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_n\mathbf{U}$ give $\mathbf{U} = \sum_n c_n\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{R}_n$, and the conditions set $c_n = 0$ for the incoming waves, those whose eigenvalue of $A$ points into the domain. Codes usually also extrapolate the outgoing characteristic variables from the interior, which completes the state on the boundary.
- The exact conditions for a single frequency apply the ideal condition to each Fourier mode along a boundary on which the solution is periodic in $y$, as an algebraic equation in the Fourier domain (§2.5). Their implementation depends on the interior method, and a principal difficulty is to decide which waves enter.
- The exact steady conditions are their limit as $\omega \to 0$ (§2.6). For the mean of the solution along the boundary, the mode $l = 0$, they reduce to the one-dimensional conditions, whose right-hand sides the user may set to prescribe the mean incoming characteristics, such as the mean inlet flow angle, stagnation enthalpy and entropy (2.4.3).
- The approximate unsteady conditions expand $\mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}_n$ in a Taylor series in $\lambda$ (§2.7). The first term gives the one-dimensional conditions. The second, after multiplying by $\omega$ and replacing $\omega$ and $l$ by derivatives in $t$ and $y$, gives a local condition with first derivatives in $t$ and $y$, which needs no Fourier decomposition and which the interior method can probably also integrate.

The one-dimensional conditions are always well posed (§2.8). After a transformation that makes $A$ and $B$ symmetric, the energy, the integral of $|\mathbf{u}|^2$ over the domain, changes only through a boundary integral of $\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{T}A\mathbf{u} = \sum_n \lambda_n c_n^2$, with the eigenvalues $\lambda_n$ of $A$. Once the conditions remove the incoming terms, the remaining ones all carry energy out, so that the energy cannot grow. For the approximate conditions, the theory of Kreiss requires instead that no incoming mode satisfy the conditions: the matrix $C_{mn} = \bar{\mathbf{v}}^\mathrm{L}_m\mathbf{u}^\mathrm{R}_n$ of the approximate left and exact right eigenvectors of the incoming waves must be non-singular for all real $l$ and all complex $\omega$ with $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega > 0$. Where right eigenvectors coalesce at a critical frequency, Giles extends the condition: the determinant may vanish there, but no faster than the coalescence permits, and he conjectures a general rule for several coalescing eigenvectors. [Engquist and Majda](#engquist-majda-1977) (1977) had conjectured that the approximation of second order is always well posed, which §3.7 disproves for the Euler equations. A general solution of given $\omega$ and $l$ splits into incoming and outgoing modes, and the approximate conditions give the incoming amplitudes as $-C^{-1}D$ times the outgoing ones, with a matrix $D$ of the approximate left eigenvectors and the right eigenvectors of the outgoing waves (§2.9). Since $\mathbf{w}^\mathrm{L}_m - \mathbf{v}^\mathrm{L}_m = O(l/\omega)$, the one-dimensional conditions reflect waves to $O(l/\omega)$, and the approximate conditions of second order to $O((l/\omega)^2)$.

#### 2.2.5 The conditions of Giles for the Euler equations

For the Euler equations, the perturbations $\mathbf{U} = (\delta\rho, \delta u, \delta v, \delta p)^\mathrm{T}$ of a uniform flow obey the linear system of 2.2.4, with $A$ and $B$ built from that flow ([Giles](#giles-1988) 1988, §3.1). Scaled by its density and speed of sound, $A$ has the rows $(u, 1, 0, 0)$, $(0, u, 0, 1)$, $(0, 0, u, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0, u)$, and $B$ the rows $(v, 0, 1, 0)$, $(0, v, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, v, 1)$ and $(0, 0, 1, v)$, where $u$ and $v$ are now the Mach numbers along $x$ and $y$ (eq. 63). Linearising introduces errors. In steady flow they are proportional to the square of the steady perturbation at the boundaries, and small except for an oblique shock at the outflow. In unsteady flow they are proportional to the square of the unsteady perturbation, and to its product with the non-uniformity of the steady flow, since $A$ and $B$ must be built from a mean of the steady state. The last is negligible at the inflow, but may dominate at the outflow, behind wakes and shock losses. All these errors are of second order, much smaller than the first-order gains of the conditions.

The dispersion relation $(uk + vl - \omega)^2[(uk + vl - \omega)^2 - k^2 - l^2] = 0$ has the double root $k_{1,2} = (\omega - vl)/u$ of the entropy and vorticity waves, and the acoustic roots $k_{3,4} = (\omega - vl)(-u \pm S)/(1 - u^2)$, with $S = [1 - (1 - u^2)l^2/(\omega - vl)^2]^{1/2}$ (§3.2, eqs. 64 to 70). In the domain $0 \le x \le 1$ with $0 < u < 1$, the inflow at $x = 0$ has three incoming waves, the entropy, vorticity and downstream acoustic waves, and the outflow at $x = 1$ one, the upstream acoustic wave (§3.4). The branch of $S$ must be chosen with care. For real $\omega$ and real positive $S$ the third wave runs to the right and the fourth to the left, and for complex $\omega$ or $S$ the third root is the one with positive imaginary part, so that it remains the right-running wave (§3.2). The report assumes the flow axially subsonic, as in almost all turbomachines. The eigenvectors identify the waves (§3.3). The entropy wave changes the density alone, at constant pressure and without vorticity, the vorticity wave carries vorticity at uniform entropy and pressure, and the acoustic waves are isentropic and irrotational. The eigenvectors of the double root are not unique, and Giles chooses those that separate entropy from vorticity, as in fluid dynamics, which simplifies the later algebra.

In dimensional variables, with the speed of sound $c$, the one-dimensional characteristic variables are $c_1 = -c^2\delta\rho + \delta p$ for the entropy wave, $c_2 = \rho c\,\delta v$ for the vorticity wave, $c_3 = \rho c\,\delta u + \delta p$ for the downstream and $c_4 = -\rho c\,\delta u + \delta p$ for the upstream acoustic wave (§3.8, eq. 162). The one-dimensional conditions set $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = 0$ at the inflow and $c_4 = 0$ at the outflow (eqs. 164 and 165). The outgoing characteristics are computed or extrapolated from the interior, and the state on the boundary follows from all four (§3.4).

The approximate conditions of second order are exact for the entropy and vorticity waves at the inflow, since their only approximation, $S \approx 1$, enters the acoustic waves alone (§3.7.1). At the outflow they read $\partial c_4/\partial t + u\,\partial c_2/\partial y + v\,\partial c_4/\partial y = 0$ (eq. 174), a local equation for the incoming characteristic that can be integrated in time like the interior equations. Before use, they must be checked for well-posedness, without which any implementation diverges on a fine enough grid (§3.7.2). In a frame that moves with the tangential velocity $v$, which leaves well-posedness unchanged, the inflow conditions of second order admit an incoming mode at the complex frequency $\omega = iu|l|$, where two eigenvectors coalesce, and are ill posed. The outflow condition is well posed. Giles cures the inflow by relaxing its third condition (§3.7.3). Since the first two already remove the entropy and vorticity waves, its left eigenvector need not be orthogonal to theirs, and a correction proportional to $\lambda$ is chosen that makes the reflection of fourth order and the problem well posed. In dimensional variables this modified inflow condition reads $\partial(c_1, c_2, c_3)^\mathrm{T}/\partial t + M_\mathrm{in}\,\partial(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4)^\mathrm{T}/\partial y = 0$, where $M_\mathrm{in}$ has the rows $(v, 0, 0, 0)$, $(0, v, (c + u)/2, (c - u)/2)$ and $(0, (c - u)/2, v, 0)$ (eq. 173). A modified outflow condition, $\partial c_4/\partial t + \frac{1}{2}(c + u)\,\partial c_2/\partial y + v\,\partial c_4/\partial y = 0$, is well posed as well (eq. 175).

The reflection coefficients show the trade-offs (§3.7.4, eqs. 159 to 161). At the modified inflow, the outgoing pressure wave reflects no entropy or vorticity, and the pressure wave it reflects has an amplitude of $O((l/\omega)^4)$. At the outflow of second order, outgoing entropy and vorticity cause no reflection, and the pressure wave a reflection of second order, with the coefficient $[(1 + u)/(1 - u)][S - 1 + u(1 - u)\lambda^2]/[S + 1 - u(1 - u)\lambda^2]$ in the moving frame. At the cut-off frequency, where $\lambda^2 = 1/(1 - u^2)$ and the group velocity has no component along $x$, the coefficients are $-(1 - u)/(1 + u)$ at the inflow and $-(1 + u)/(1 - u)$ at the outflow. Their product is 1, as it must be, since two pressure waves without group velocity along $x$ cannot be told apart. The modified outflow reflects the pressure wave only to fourth order, but outgoing vorticity to first order. It therefore suits only boundaries without outgoing vorticity, such as the far field of an oscillating transonic airfoil away from its wake, since only a shock and the unsteady Kutta condition create vorticity there (§3.7.4).

### 2.3 Characteristic boundary conditions

#### 2.3.1 The form of Thompson

The one-dimensional characteristic conditions are exact only in one dimension, but serve as an approximation in several ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987, [1990](#thompson-1990)), as Colonius restates him, writes the conservation laws at a boundary normal to $x$ as $\mathbf{q}_t + P(\mathbf{u})S(\mathbf{u})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})_y = 0$ (eq. 12). Here $\mathbf{q}$ are the conserved variables, $\mathbf{g}$ their fluxes along the boundary, $\mathbf{u} = (\rho, u, v, p)^\mathrm{T}$ the primitive variables, $P = \partial\mathbf{q}/\partial\mathbf{u}$ the Jacobian between the two sets, and $S$ the matrix of the right eigenvectors of the Jacobian in $x$. The vector $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ has the components $\mathcal{L}_1 = \lambda_1v_x$, $\mathcal{L}_2 = \lambda_1(p_x - a^2\rho_x)$, $\mathcal{L}_3 = \lambda_2[u_x + (\rho a)^{-1}p_x]$ and $\mathcal{L}_4 = \lambda_3[u_x - (\rho a)^{-1}p_x]$, with $\lambda_1 = u$, $\lambda_2 = u + a$ and $\lambda_3 = u - a$ (eq. 13). Each is the slope of a characteristic times the derivative in $x$ of its Riemann invariant: those of the shear wave, the entropy wave and the two acoustic waves. Where a characteristic leaves the domain, its component is computed from the interior with one-sided differences. Where it enters, its component is prescribed.

Linearised, the conditions of Thompson differ from eq. 8, since eq. 12 keeps the derivatives along the boundary. Fig. 2 shows their reflection coefficients for $u_o = a_o/2$. At a subsonic outflow they reflect outgoing sound, $\rho_{a^+ \to a^-}$, less than the one-dimensional conditions. Plane acoustic waves that travel along the boundary, which differ from the tangent waves of 2.2.2 unless $u_o = 0$, are not reflected at the outflow at all, since all derivatives in $x$ in eq. 13 then vanish. Outgoing vorticity waves, however, generate more sound than under the one-dimensional conditions, and the acoustic reflections at the inflow exceed theirs. Except where $u_o = 0$, the linearised conditions are not strongly well posed. Their reflection coefficient at the inflow becomes infinite for plane acoustic waves that travel along the boundary. Since $s$ is then purely imaginary, the ill-posedness is weak, and without other dissipation the computation is marginally stable. The same property helps at the corners of a domain that is not periodic, since a plane wave along one boundary is absorbed completely by the boundary across its path. The conditions at a corner are compatible: they are imposed on $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ and on the corresponding transformation of the fluxes in $y$ at once. This is robust in tests and in practice, although with large errors near the corner (Thompson 1990, [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) 1992, as cited by Colonius).

#### 2.3.2 Nonlinear outflow

At a subsonic outflow, the inviscid equations take one condition, on the sole incoming characteristic $\mathcal{L}_4$ ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). The other variables follow from the analysis of [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987), as Colonius restates it, or, equivalently, from the locally one-dimensional inviscid (LODI) relations of [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992). If the flow at the boundary could be linearised about a uniform state, which it seldom can at an outflow, a non-reflecting condition of 2.2 would set the fluctuation $\mathcal{L}_4'$, and the linearised conditions of Thompson take $\mathcal{L}_4' = 0$, with the reflections of fig. 2. Usually the fluctuations are not small, and the mean flow is not known. A steady Reynolds-averaged solution has been proposed for it ([Morris et al.](#morris-et-al-1997) 1997), but nonlinearity still prevents accurate conditions.

A vortex without circulation that a uniform flow carries through the outflow boundary shows the size of the nonlinear errors ([Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) 1993). For small viscosity, the vortex should leave unaltered at constant speed. [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, fig. 4) compares two linearised outflow conditions for it ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002): that of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990), under which outgoing vorticity produces no incoming sound in the linear limit, and a modified condition of Giles, under which it produces incoming sound in proportion to the amplitude of the vortex. Once the velocity that the vortex induces is a sizeable fraction of the speed of the flow, the nonlinear reflections of the first condition are as large as the linear reflections of the second. Shear at the boundary would likely make this worse. A vortex strong enough can even reverse the sign of $\lambda_1$ for a time, turning the outflow into an inflow, and the linearised treatment then fails. Prescribing the incoming wave is thus a matter of modelling, which analysis alone cannot settle, and buffer techniques (2.5) are at present the most accurate at outflow boundaries.

#### 2.3.3 Extensions

By [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §4), the characteristic conditions of [Thompson](#thompson-1987) (1987) and [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992) have proved robust, and are therefore widely used and extended. [Thompson](#thompson-1990) (1990), as Colonius restates him, applies the same framework to walls, both inviscid and without slip, to boundaries at constant pressure, and to a force-free boundary. The last sets to zero all forces on the fluid normal to the boundary and lets the fluid be carried at its own velocity, much like the convective condition of incompressible flow. Poinsot and Lele (1992) generalise their LODI relations alike. [Okong'o and Bellan](#okongo-bellan-2002) (2002) extend the analysis to mixtures of real gases, and [Kim and Lee](#kim-lee-2000) (2000) to curvilinear coordinates.

### 2.4 Relaxation towards the far field at subsonic outflow and inflow

#### 2.4.1 The drift of the mean pressure

For nonlinear computations, [Thompson](#thompson-1990) (1990), as restated by [Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §4), sets $\mathcal{L}_4 = 0$ at a subsonic outflow. Nothing then keeps the time-averaged pressure from drifting, the less so when characteristic conditions are imposed at the inflow as well ([Rudy and Strikwerda](#rudy-strikwerda-1980) 1980, [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) 1992, as cited by Colonius). Physically, the upstream characteristic would carry whatever acoustic reflection is needed to hold the pressure downstream, at least in steady flow. The Dirichlet condition $p = p_\infty$ fixes the pressure, and is still standard in many codes, but it reflects badly, completely for plane waves. Rudy and Strikwerda (1980), as Colonius restates them, therefore modify the one-dimensional characteristic condition to $p_t - \rho au_t + \alpha(p - p_\infty) = 0$, which imposes the pressure in a steady state (eq. 14). They optimised the parameter $\alpha$ to reach the steady state fastest in a linearised one-dimensional problem. Poinsot and Lele (1992) adopt the condition in their characteristic formulation, and [Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) (1993) with the conditions of Giles in sheared flows. No systematic study tells how to set $\alpha$ in general, and the right value probably depends on several factors, among them the discrete stability of 2.6.

#### 2.4.2 Subsonic inflow

At an inflow, a target profile of velocity, and perhaps of temperature or another thermodynamic quantity, is often wanted ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §4). The traditional approach imposes Dirichlet conditions on the right number of variables, four at a subsonic inflow of inviscid flow in three dimensions, and well-posedness has been proved for several such sets, inviscid and viscous. [Poinsot and Lele](#poinsot-lele-1992) (1992) combine Dirichlet conditions on the velocity and the temperature with a characteristic analysis for the remaining component of $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}$ that closes the equation of continuity. All Dirichlet conditions, however, reflect the upstream acoustic wave badly, since they force fluctuations of acoustic variables to zero. Linearised, imposing the one-dimensional characteristics equals the one-dimensional non-reflecting condition and lets plane waves pass upstream, but fixing the velocity and the density perturbations reflects even plane waves completely. An inflow reflects far less where more is known of the incoming flow. If it is laminar with small disturbances, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_o + \boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}'$ can be linearised. Steady, unidirectional, transversely sheared flows at constant pressure are exact inviscid solutions, so that $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_o = 0$ and all incoming fluctuations $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}'$ are prescribed. For viscous flow a solution of the boundary-layer equations serves, whose $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_o$ follows the slow growth of the layer along the flow. The conditions of 2.2 can then set $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}'$, with the caveats stated there.

#### 2.4.3 Relaxed steady and single-frequency conditions

[Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, §3.5, §3.6) imposes the exact conditions of each Fourier mode along the boundary in a time-marching code by relaxing the incoming characteristics of the mode towards their exact values at a rate $\alpha$. The lag keeps the evolution well posed, as conditions that set the incoming one-dimensional characteristics are. For the steady conditions, the Fourier coefficients $\hat{c}_n$ of the characteristics of a mode with wavenumber $l \neq 0$ obey $\partial\hat{c}_1/\partial t = -\alpha\hat{c}_1$, $\partial\hat{c}_2/\partial t = \alpha\{-[(c\beta + v)/(c + u)]\hat{c}_4 - \hat{c}_2\}$ and $\partial\hat{c}_3/\partial t = \alpha\{[(c\beta + v)/(c + u)]^2\hat{c}_4 - \hat{c}_3\}$ at the inflow, and $\partial\hat{c}_4/\partial t = \alpha\{[2u/(c\beta - v)]\hat{c}_2 - [(c\beta + v)/(c\beta - v)]\hat{c}_3 - \hat{c}_4\}$ at the outflow, with $\beta = i\,\mathrm{sign}(l)(1 - M^2)^{1/2}$ for a Mach number $M < 1$ and $\beta = -\mathrm{sign}(v)(M^2 - 1)^{1/2}$ for $M > 1$ (eqs. 170 to 172). Numerical experience suggests $\alpha = 1/P$ in the non-dimensional variables, with the pitch $P$ of the periodic boundary (§3.6). For the mean mode, $l = 0$, the user prescribes the changes of the incoming one-dimensional characteristics needed to reach chosen mean conditions: the mean entropy, flow angle and stagnation enthalpy at the inflow, and the mean exit pressure at the outflow. A separate report gives the details. It also removes the second-order non-uniformities of entropy and stagnation enthalpy across the inflow that the linear theory leaves, by modifying one inflow condition and replacing another by the constraint of uniform stagnation enthalpy. The single-frequency conditions take the same form, with $\lambda = cl/\omega$ and $S = [1 - (c^2 - u^2)\lambda^2/(c - v\lambda)^2]^{1/2}$ in place of $\beta$ (eqs. 166 to 169). Giles had not yet implemented them, and the right $\alpha$ was unknown: too large a value may make the problem ill posed and the scheme unstable, and too small a value slows the convergence (§3.5).

### 2.5 Absorbing layers

#### 2.5.1 Damping in a layer

The nonlinear outflow of turbulent flow, at which linearised conditions err badly (2.3.2), motivates absorbing layers: regions next to the boundary in which the physics of the flow is modified ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5). Many of them can also serve at inflows and in the far field. They either improve the condition that closes the domain or make one unnecessary. Many of their ideas go back to [Israeli and Orszag](#israeli-orszag-1981) (1981), who devised them for linear wave equations when local non-reflecting conditions were still hard to specify. For linear systems those difficulties have since been resolved in other ways (2.2), but the ideas remain in wide use, in linear and nonlinear computations alike. An absorbing layer damps disturbances before they meet the boundary condition: by artificial dissipation through upwinding, by a raised physical viscosity or a hyperviscosity, or most simply by a linear friction, $\mathbf{q}_t + N(\mathbf{q}) = -\sigma(x)(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{q}_o)$ (eq. 15). Here $N$ is a possibly nonlinear operator, $\sigma(x)$ a friction coefficient that usually rises smoothly from zero in the physical domain to a positive value in the layer, and $\mathbf{q}_o$ a nominal state about which disturbances are defined, zero for linear equations. Disturbances decay exponentially while they stay in the layer, and whatever the boundary reflects is damped again on its way back. The inner edge of the layer, however, where $\sigma$ varies, itself reflects (Israeli and Orszag 1981). Without further measures, only a slow rise of the damping over a long distance gives satisfactory results, and the layers become thick and costly.

#### 2.5.2 The perfectly matched layer

A layer becomes non-reflecting if the eigenfunctions of the modes of the interior equations stay unchanged in it, except that their phase speeds become complex so that they decay ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.1). This strictly applies only to linearised systems. The perfectly matched layer (PML) achieves it and damps all disturbances within the layer, which a non-reflecting condition of low order, usually the one-dimensional characteristic one, closes at its outer edge. It was first formulated for electromagnetic radiation ([Berenger](#berenger-1994) 1994), and extended to the linearised Euler equations by [Hu](#hu-1996) (1996), following the modal analysis of 2.2. Early versions became unstable over long times. Later work resolved this, and strongly well-posed layers now exist for inviscid disturbances of a uniform mean flow ([Abarbanel, Gottlieb and Hesthaven](#abarbanel-et-al-1999) 1999, [Hu](#hu-2001) 2001, [Hagstrom](#hagstrom-2003) 2003).

Unlike the local conditions of 2.2.2, the PML has parameters to tune: the thickness of the layer, or the number of grid points that it costs, and the profile $\sigma(x)$ of the absorption. Unless the inner edge is treated as an internal boundary, for instance by characteristic matching (Hagstrom 2003), the profile must be smooth to the order of the scheme, or the edge reflects numerically. [Abarbanel et al.](#abarbanel-et-al-1999) (1999) use $\sigma(x) = C(|x - L|/\delta)^n$, with the width $L$ of the domain, the width $\delta$ of the layer, a constant strength $C$ and $n = 4$ for their method of fourth order. With $C = 1$, acoustic and vortical pulses in two dimensions, in a uniform flow at Mach 0.5 along $x$, leave more accurately through layers of only 6 points than through one-dimensional characteristic conditions, and dramatically better, at greater cost, through layers of 20 to 30 points.

Fig. 5 compares the error over time of an acoustic pulse in a flow at Mach 0.5 under the Padé conditions of degrees $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 2)$, with the treatment of corners of 2.2.2, the PML of Abarbanel et al. (1999) and the supergrid layer of 2.5.5 ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002). The pulse is tested apart from vortical disturbances, since the local Padé conditions are exactly non-reflecting for linear vortical disturbances. The error is measured against a reference solution on a domain so large that no reflection returns within the time considered, which also isolates the error of the boundary from other errors of discretisation. Thick PML layers are needed to match the errors of the Padé condition of degrees $(0, 0)$. That of degrees $(2, 2)$ is best at short times, but no better than $(0, 0)$ at long times, when the error comes from waves tangent to the boundaries, which every local condition reflects completely. Padé conditions of higher order cannot be compared, for lack of a stable treatment of corners. [Hagstrom and Nazarov](#hagstrom-nazarov-2002) (2002) extend the PML to non-uniform flows, in particular to parallel shear flows. The interface between domain and layer can still be kept free of reflection, but the physical instabilities of the mean profile prevent any simple guarantee that all waves decay in the layer. Optimising the two free parameters of the layer, besides its thickness, nevertheless gave satisfactory results.

#### 2.5.3 Layers for nonlinear flow

Applied to nonlinear equations, a linearised PML produces quadratic errors, which make its interface reflect, just as linearised local conditions do ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.2). Whether its non-reflection repays the effort is therefore questionable. Simple modifications of eq. 15 give reasonable results in nonlinear flow, though with thicker layers than in the linear case ([Freund](#freund-1997) 1997). A layer performs better the better the condition that closes it ([Israeli and Orszag](#israeli-orszag-1981) 1981), and needs no layer at all if that condition is exact. Supersonic flow has exact conditions, at least for inviscid flow and without discrete effects, since all waves enter at a supersonic inflow and all leave at a supersonic outflow. Following [Ta'asan and Nark](#taasan-nark-1995) (1995), an artificial convection added to the equations makes the flow supersonic in layers near the inflow and the outflow. At the outflow, waves that the inner edge of the layer lets through cause no reflection at the outer boundary, and those that it reflects are partly damped by the friction of eq. 15 before they return. Applied smoothly, the damping and the convection keep reflections small. At the inflow the friction can be weaker, since waves running upstream slow to zero speed at the sonic point and stay long in the layer, while incoming vortical forcing passes quickly and is damped less. [Karni](#karni-1996) (1996) instead slows the convection in the layer by slowing-down operators, so that the damping has long to act. Like the PML, these act only on the outgoing waves, but the non-reflection is approximate, since the decomposition into modes is one-dimensional, applied at an angle to the boundary that is computed locally from the interior solution. All such layers depend strongly on their width, and have even more parameters and profiles than the PML, to be set by experiment.

#### 2.5.4 Grid stretching and filtering

A mapping of an infinite domain onto a finite one avoids a boundary condition altogether, and is accurate for smooth solutions that neither radiate nor advect ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.3). Waves of a fixed wavelength, however, become ever more poorly resolved where the grid stretches, until the discretisation no longer supports them and aliases them back into the domain. Since the most poorly resolved waves of dispersive differences travel backwards (2.6.1), an infinitely stretched grid returns every disturbance, and infinity reflects perfectly. If the poorly resolved disturbances are dissipated, by dealiasing, artificial viscosity, explicit damping or filtering, the dispersion of the stretched grid even helps, since a gradual stretching lets disturbances stay there as long as wanted. In one dimension, the equations read $\mathbf{q}_t + \xi_x\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})_\xi = 0$, with the physical coordinate $x$ and the finite computational coordinate $\xi$ (eq. 16). The metric $\xi_x$ falls from about 1 near the source to zero as $x \to \infty$, so that, read with $\xi$ as the physical coordinate, the stretching slows every disturbance to rest, like the operators of [Karni](#karni-1996) (1996). [Rai and Moin](#rai-moin-1991) (1991) combined stretching with filtering into an absorbing layer for incompressible flow, and [Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) (1993) applied it to compressible aeroacoustics. In both, the grid was stretched to a large but finite distance and closed by an artificial boundary condition. Colonius et al. switched on an explicit filter gradually in the layer, through variable coefficients, and reduced the reflections of nonlinear vortical structures at the outflow by several orders of magnitude below those of the linearised outflow condition of [Giles](#giles-1990) (1990). The layer was, however, wide compared with a PML, and its parameters and profiles could again be set only by trial and error.

#### 2.5.5 Fringes, windowing and the supergrid model

The fringe method of [Spalart](#spalart-1988) (1988) for spatially developing boundary layers avoids conditions at inflow and outflow altogether ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.4). The boundaries are periodic, and an absorbing layer near them changes the flow before it is recycled as inflow. A linear friction like that of eq. 15 is added to the momentum equations, and the difficulty lies in the choice of $\mathbf{q}_o$. The streamwise velocity is driven towards a solution of the Navier–Stokes equations, such as a laminar boundary layer $Q(x, y)$ in two dimensions, made periodic by blending through a smoothed step $S$ that rises from 0 at the start of the fringe, $x = L - \Delta$, to 1 at the edge of the periodic box, $x = L$ (eq. 17). With a Fourier spectral method, the blending functions, usually combinations of Gaussians or hyperbolic tangents, must preserve periodicity and continuous derivatives to round-off. In incompressible flow, the transverse velocity is chosen so that the target is free of divergence. [Nordström, Nordin and Henningson](#nordstrom-et-al-1999) (1999) argue from linear model equations with constant coefficients, and confirm by experiments with the full problem, that the periodic fringe converges, in incompressible flow at least, to the problem with inflow and outflow: disturbances decay before they are recycled, and the fringe has little influence upstream.

**[GK]** As printed, eq. 17 reads $q_o = Q(x, y) - [Q(x - L, y) - Q(x, y)]\,S((x - L + \Delta)/\Delta)$, which gives $2Q(L, y) - Q(0, y)$ at the edge of the box. Periodicity requires the inflow profile $Q(0, y)$ there, which the sign $+$ in front of the bracket gives.

The spatial windowing of [Guo, Adams and Kleiser](#guo-et-al-1994) (1994), revisited by [Schlatter, Adams and Kleiser](#schlatter-et-al-2003) (2003), also combines periodic boundaries with an absorbing layer ([Colonius](#colonius-2004) 2004, §5.4). In one dimension, the equations are multiplied by a window $w(x)$, which is zero at the edges of the domain, rises to unity across the fringe and is unity inside: $w\mathbf{q}_t + w\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})_x = 0$, or $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t + (w\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}))_x = w'\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})$ for the windowed variables $\tilde{\mathbf{q}} = w\mathbf{q}$ (eq. 18). If the window meets constraints of differentiability and periodicity like those on $S$, a Fourier expansion of $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}$ keeps spectral accuracy. Since $w$ vanishes at the boundary, $\mathbf{q}$ cannot in general be recovered from $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}$. Where the flow near the boundaries is a uniform base flow with perturbations, the regularised dewindowing $\mathbf{q} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{q}}$ turns eq. 18 into $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t + w\mathbf{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})_x = 0$ (eq. 19), the exact analogue of an infinite stretching of the grid. Disturbances must therefore be dissipated in the fringe, lest they be aliased back. Dealiasing is possible with periodic boundaries, but additional absorption in the fringe works much better ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002), such as the friction of eq. 15 that Guo et al. (1994) add. For a prescribed inflow profile, Schlatter et al. (2003) dewindow by $\mathbf{q} \approx (1 - w)\mathbf{q}_o + \tilde{\mathbf{q}}$, with $\mathbf{q}_o$ as in the fringe method.

Windowing in physical space is filtering in Fourier space, and the reverse, so that windowing filters out the scales larger than the domain ([Colonius and Ran](#colonius-ran-2002) 2002). By analogy with large-eddy simulation, which filters small scales and supplies a model for those below the grid, a supergrid model should then correct for the unphysical effects of truncating the domain. Colonius and Ran (2002) take $\mathbf{q} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{q}}$ and filter eq. 19 in physical space, which amounts to dewindowing, since large scales are shortened continually as they travel into the fringe, as on a stretched grid. The filtered equations then need models for the nonlinear products in $\mathbf{f}$, as in large-eddy simulation, and for the products of the window with the fluxes, for which a Gaussian filter and a model of tensor diffusivity were chosen, modified to be globally diffusive. In model problems the boundary is then highly transparent (figs. 4 and 5). For the acoustic pulse it performs a little better than a PML of the same number of points, and so of about the same cost. For the nonlinear vortex of 2.3.2, a layer of 10 points reflects 1000 times less than the strength of the vortex, whatever that strength, even when the vortex induces inflow at the outflow boundary. The review grants that nothing yet favours windowing and the supergrid model over other absorbing layers, whose parameters and profiles could be optimised to a similar performance. The supergrid model, however, has fewer parameters to tune. They reduce to two scalars, the number of points across the layer and the maximum slope of the window, for which, properly scaled, only a small range of values comes into question, with little influence on the results. The analogy with the modelling of turbulence may also frame better models, and periodic boundaries may ease estimates of the energy ([Nordström et al.](#nordstrom-et-al-1999) 1999).

#### 2.5.6 Wave-damping zones in simulations of discs

[de Val-Borro et al.](#de-val-borro-et-al-2006) (2006) compare grid and particle codes on a planet that opens a gap in a two-dimensional disc, between the radii $0.4a$ and $2.5a$, with the orbital radius $a$ of the planet, on $128 \times 384$ cells in radius and azimuth (§3). The disc is isolated, without inflow of mass, and the codes in polar coordinates close it with solid boundaries, next to which zones kill the waves to reduce their reflection (§3.2). In the radial ranges $[0.4a, 0.5a]$ and $[2.1a, 2.5a]$, the equation $dx/dt = -(x - x_0)R(r)/\tau$ is solved after each time step for the surface density and the components of the velocity, $x$ (eq. 10). Here $\tau$ is the orbital period at the boundary concerned, and $R(r)$ a parabolic function that is 1 at the boundary of the domain and 0 at the inner edge of the zone. The paper does not define the values $x_0$ towards which the damping drives. The codes in Cartesian coordinates damp in the outer ring alike, and let gas flow out freely through the edges of their square grid.

The damping does not conserve mass, although the loss is small (§3.2). After 200 orbits, the disc of the heavier planet has lost about 8 per cent of its mass in two of the shock-capturing codes and about 3 per cent in three of the upwind codes (§5.1). With the lighter planet, the damping near the inner boundary may also add angular momentum to the disc artificially (§5.3). Only the region between $0.5a$ and $2.1a$ is meaningful for the comparison. Despite the damping, the vortensity shows reflected waves in four codes, NIRVANA-GDA, RH2D, GLOBAL and GENESIS, all of them among the upwind codes of §4.1.1 (§5.1 and §5.2, figs. 3 and 11), and several codes reflect waves at the outer boundary in the viscous runs with the lighter planet (§5.4, fig. 27).

### 2.6 Characteristic conditions in finite-volume codes with ghost cells

#### 2.6.1 Boundary closures and their stability

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004, §3) treats discretisation in the setting of finite differences, the most common, and notes that analogous effects arise in other discretisations. Two related problems of closure arise at a boundary: wide stencils do not fit near it, and the conditions fix only some of the variables. The first is met by one-sided stencils at the boundary and biased ones next to it, which may be one order less accurate than the interior scheme without lowering its overall order ([Gustafsson](#gustafsson-1975) 1975). The second is met either by extrapolating the unspecified variables from the interior or by imposing some of the full equations, or combinations of them, at the boundary, whose derivatives normal to it then need one-sided stencils. Both can be accurate, but stability needs more care. Von Neumann analysis with periodic boundaries is neither necessary nor sufficient in general. Schemes of first and second order, which need special treatment only at the boundary point, have caused little difficulty, but stable closures for schemes of high order, and compact ones above all, have been much harder to find.

Most analyses use the advection equation $u_t + cu_x = 0$ on $[0, 1]$, with $c > 0$ and an inflow at $x = 0$ (eq. 9). Lax stability keeps the solution bounded as the mesh is refined at a fixed time, and the normal-mode theory of [Gustafsson, Kreiss and Sundström](#gustafsson-et-al-1972) (1972) analyses it. Asymptotic stability keeps the error bounded in time, and requires that the spatial operator of the semi-discrete scheme have no eigenvalues in the right half of the complex plane. Stable closures are usually found a posteriori, by computing the eigenvalues numerically, and often only by sacrificing accuracy at the boundary. Since closures are combined with other approximations of the continuous conditions anyway, the order of accuracy near the boundary matters less than stability and conservation. Operators that satisfy summation by parts, with boundary conditions imposed by simultaneous approximation terms, reduce stability to algebraic constraints on the boundary stencils ([Carpenter, Gottlieb and Abarbanel](#carpenter-et-al-1994) 1994). Such a term imposes the condition non-locally: it adds a dissipative term that drives the solution towards the boundary condition near the boundary, in the spirit of the buffer regions of 2.5.

The dispersion of difference schemes causes reflections of another kind ([Trefethen](#trefethen-1982) 1982, [Vichnevetsky](#vichnevetsky-1987) 1987). The central difference $u_x \approx (u_{j+1} - u_{j-1})/(2\Delta x)$ applied to eq. 9 has two normal modes $u_j(t) = \hat{u}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\kappa^j$ at each frequency below the cut-off $c/\Delta x$, with $\kappa^2 + 2i\Omega\kappa - 1 = 0$ and $\Omega = \omega\Delta x/c$ (eqs. 10 and 11, fig. 3). One travels at nearly the correct group velocity $c$ at small wavenumbers and converges to the solution. The other, spurious mode has a negative group velocity and only a few points per wavelength. Spurious waves can feed back from downstream even in supersonic flow, and in subsonic flow they add a path of feedback to the physical sound that approximate outflow conditions excite. Such feedback can make a shear layer that is nominally only convectively unstable force itself ([Buell and Huerre](#buell-huerre-1988) 1988, [Colonius, Lele and Moin](#colonius-et-al-1993) 1993). Larger stencils add further spurious modes, which decay into the domain. Upwind-biased schemes damp the short spurious waves quickly, so that they seldom matter, but simulations that resolve turbulence usually prefer schemes of low dissipation and high order.

In the discrete case, even the trivially non-reflecting condition of eq. 9 reflects, since any local closure couples the physical and spurious modes: physical into spurious at the outflow, and spurious into physical at the inflow. Discretely non-reflecting closures minimise these reflections, and their reflection coefficients also decide their stability, which requires them to be bounded for all complex $\omega$. For the linearised Euler equations in several dimensions, an isentropic discretisation has nine reflection coefficients at a boundary instead of four, since each physical wave has a spurious partner of opposite group velocity ([Rowley and Colonius](#rowley-colonius-2000) 2000). Localising the exact continuous condition and the discretely exact closure are then two approximations whose errors can work against each other. By 2004, neither summation by parts nor discretely non-reflecting closures had been used in more general computations, with non-uniform mean flows, corners, nonlinearity or viscosity.

Some studies extrapolate all flow variables, or the fluxes, at an outflow (§4), which corresponds to no boundary condition at all for the continuous problem. The closure by extrapolation still amounts to some condition, even if its continuous counterpart is hard to establish.

### 2.8 Open boundaries in astrophysical simulations of clouds and winds

#### 2.8.1 Discs

In the comparison of codes on a planet in a disc of [de Val-Borro et al.](#de-val-borro-et-al-2006) (2006), FARGO tested a transmitted-wave condition against the damping zones of 2.5.6 (§4.1.1.4). The pitch angle of the wake of the planet at the inner and outer boundaries follows from the WKB approximation, and the ghost ring takes the content of the last ring of the domain, shifted in azimuth by the amount that this angle dictates. The condition removes reflected waves very efficiently, and gives results like those of the damping zones, since both aim to avoid the reflection of waves (§6).

## 3. Software architecture

[Colonius](#colonius-2004) (2004) describes no code. [Giles](#giles-1988) (1988, §1.3) implemented most of his conditions in his own program for steady and unsteady flows in turbomachinery, whose numerical details a separate report describes. The report itself gives the conditions in analytic form, from which implementations suited to other algorithms can be derived, and lists them in dimensional variables for that purpose (§3.8).
