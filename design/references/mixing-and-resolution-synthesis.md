# Synthesis of mixing and resolution

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on the mixing of cold clouds with hot gas and on the numerical resolution that this mixing demands, kept in `reference-works/Mixing and Resolution/Docs`. The collection is gathered around the review of [Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) (2026, [arXiv:2601.16566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16566)) on simulations of multiphase gas in and around galaxies (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) (2026, §2.1, eq. 2), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI or ADS bibcode, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Works that the papers cite are cited as the papers cite them, with slips in their years corrected, and the papers of the collection keep the year of their published version throughout. The list of sources (4) adds the published record of works that the papers cite as preprints.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

The review gives an overview of numerical simulations of multiphase gas in and around galaxies ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, abstract). It calls gas multiphase when gas at several temperatures lies roughly in the same place (§1). The classical theory of the interstellar medium (ISM) meant by phases the cold, neutral clouds of molecular and atomic hydrogen and the warmer, partly ionised medium that fills the volume around them ([Field](#field-1965) 1965; [McKee and Ostriker](#mckee-ostriker-1977) 1977). Work on the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and the intracluster medium (ICM), and simulations that do not track ionisation, often mean instead a fully ionised plasma with several equilibrium temperatures. Two long-lived phases, hot and cold, often coexist with transient gas at intermediate temperatures, made by mixing or by cooling, the two main themes of the review. Their temperatures depend on the system. Cold means neutral or molecular gas below about 300 K in the ISM, but photoionised gas near $10^4$ K in the CGM, which studies of the ISM call warm. The review follows the usage of each field and states the temperatures in each section. Hot means gas of low density at $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K unless stated otherwise.

The review leaves out the small scales of particle-in-cell simulations, the cosmological scales and the intergalactic medium, the solar corona, protostellar and protoplanetary discs, and the accretion discs of active galactic nuclei (AGN) ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1). It focuses on multiphase systems that act directly on the formation and evolution of galaxies, and on the techniques and results of simulations, connecting their scales. Other reviews cover parts of the field: processes on the scale of haloes, thermal instability among them ([Donahue and Voit](#donahue-voit-2022) 2022), mixing layers and the crushing of clouds ([Faucher-Giguère and Oh](#faucher-giguere-oh-2023) 2023), galactic winds ([Thompson and Heckman](#thompson-heckman-2024) 2024) and cosmic rays ([Ruszkowski and Pfrommer](#ruszkowski-pfrommer-2023) 2023).

Multiphase gas occurs in the ISM of the Milky Way and of other galaxies, from a few kelvin in the coldest molecular clouds to several $10^{10}$ K inside supernova remnants ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.1). In the CGM of large galaxies, a virial shock heats the infalling gas into the hot phase that fills the volume, while thermal instability, the stripping of satellites by ram pressure and winds from the central or the satellite galaxies seed the cooler phases. The ICM, at higher pressures, spans a still broader range. The interface between disc and halo, where the hotter and thinner CGM meets the warm and cool phases of the ISM, is multiphase too, driven among others by outflows. Outflows such as that of the starburst galaxy M82 show temperatures over eight orders of magnitude in the same place, and the winds of AGN carry multiphase gas under strong radiation and fast kinematics. Fig. 1 shows examples in galactic winds, gas stripped by ram pressure, high-velocity clouds and the CGM.

Thermal instability (TI) is the classical explanation for multiphase gas ([Field](#field-1965) 1965, after [Parker](#parker-1953) 1953). Gas in thermal equilibrium, whose heating does not depend on temperature, is unstable where its radiative losses grow as its temperature falls ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.1). This holds where the cooling function falls with temperature, from about $10^5$ to $10^7$ K, and the heating comes from photoionisation. Fig. 2, left, shows the rates of cooling and heating of gas of solar metallicity at two densities under the ultraviolet background at redshift zero, computed with the code Cloudy ([Ferland et al.](#ferland-et-al-2013) 2013). In gas near $10^7$ K at constant pressure, an overdense perturbation cools faster, is compressed by the surrounding pressure and cools faster still, since the losses scale as $n^2$. It settles where heating balances cooling again, near $10^4$ K for the pressure of the halo of the Milky Way, $P/k_\mathrm{B} \sim 10^2$ K cm$^{-3}$, at a temperature that depends on the density, the ionising radiation and the metallicity. Two stable phases then coexist in pressure equilibrium, $n_\mathrm{cool} T_\mathrm{cool} = n_\mathrm{hot} T_\mathrm{hot}$, as long as thermal pressure alone supports them. Magnetic fields or cosmic rays lower the density of the cool phase. The denser cool gas forms small clumps in the hot medium ([McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) 2018, Fig. 4). This origin is often called precipitation. Fig. 2, right, shows its regimes, from hot, thin gas through unstable gas to the dense, cold gas of the ISM, in the phase diagram of a simulated isolated galaxy.

Origins in the dynamics of the gas matter as much. Turbulent mixing at interfaces, through the Kelvin–Helmholtz (KH) and Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961), makes gas of intermediate temperature and density from gas that is already multiphase ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.1). Shocks compress and heat the gas that they sweep, and thermal conduction makes gas of intermediate temperature at interfaces between hot and cold gas. In real systems all of these act at once. Analytic work has taught much about each of them, but their combination and the non-linear evolution of the instabilities over long times drive the simulations. Most simulations that the review discusses assume solar metallicity. The cooling rates depend strongly on metallicity, above all between $10^5$ and $10^7$ K, but many studies express their results in dimensionless form or through cooling times, which can be rescaled, so that their conclusions apply more generally (footnote 3).

### 1.3 The collection

The review of 2026 stands at the centre of the collection. §1 introduces multiphase gas, where it occurs (§1.1), the challenges of computing it (§1.2) and the quantities that characterise its dynamics (§1.3) ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026). §2 describes the simulations, ordered roughly by scale and complexity (§2, Fig. 3): mixing layers (§2.1), clouds in a wind (§2.2), streams and jets (§2.3), thermal instability and turbulent boxes (§2.4), bubbles driven by supernovae and winds in the ISM (§2.5), stratified patches of discs (§2.6), isolated galaxies (§2.7) and haloes (§2.8). §3 summarises, with the technological advances (§3.1), models below the scale of the grid (§3.2) and the open questions (§3.3). The further papers fall into four groups. Two older, seminal works treat the crushing of clouds and turbulent mixing layers. Studies of radiative mixing layers and of the survival, growth and characteristic scale of cold gas follow, then studies of resolution. Each paper is digested in the subsections of the subjects that it serves (2), in the order of the steps of `plan.md`, and its numbers of sections, equations, figures and tables refer to its local copy.

### 2.2 Turbulent radiative mixing layers

#### 2.2.1 The shear layer and the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

Shear layers, or turbulent mixing layers, are among the simplest and most idealised set-ups for multiphase gas ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Two phases of densities $\rho_\mathrm{hot}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{cold}$ lie on either side of a perturbed interface and move along it with a relative velocity $v_\mathrm{shear}$. The KH instability develops and grows on a time $t_\mathrm{KH} \sim \chi^{1/2} \ell / v_\mathrm{shear}$ ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961), with the overdensity $\chi \equiv \rho_\mathrm{cold}/\rho_\mathrm{hot}$ and the length $\ell$ of the perturbation. It drives the mixing of the phases and their exchange of mass, momentum and energy. A sharp interface, smooth only on scales much smaller than $\ell$, is unstable at all wavelengths that the system allows. Such interfaces are common in multiphase media, where the cold phase cools much faster than on any dynamical time, so that shear between phases always brings mixing and an exchange of mass. Four effects can weaken or suppress the instability, and with it the mixing ([Landau](#landau-1944) 1944; [Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961):

- Gravity. With an acceleration $g$ that points from the hot to the cold medium, only scales $\ell < v_\mathrm{shear}^2/(g\chi)$ are unstable, so a strong effective gravity suppresses the instability ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961).
- The thickness of the shear layer. A velocity that changes smoothly over a width $d$ suppresses the modes below about $10d$, which do not feel the shear ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961; [Berlok and Pfrommer](#berlok-pfrommer-2019b) 2019b). In a viscous flow, the instability is thus suppressed where the viscous time $t_\nu \sim (\ell/10)^2/\nu$, with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$, is shorter than $t_\mathrm{KH}$ ([Roediger et al.](#roediger-et-al-2013) 2013; [Marin-Gilabert et al.](#marin-gilabert-et-al-2022) 2022).
- Supersonic flow. Above a critical Mach number, shocks that follow the growing perturbations damp them. With $\mathcal{M} \equiv v_\mathrm{shear}/c_\mathrm{s,hot}$, the threshold is $\mathcal{M}_\mathrm{crit} = (1 + \chi^{-1/3})^{3/2}$ ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, eq. 1; [Landau](#landau-1944) 1944; [Mandelker et al.](#mandelker-et-al-2016) 2016).
- Magnetic fields. By the review, a field component parallel to the interface stabilises the flow for $v_\mathrm{shear} < (v_\mathrm{A,hot}\,v_\mathrm{A,cold})^{1/2}$, with the Alfvén velocity $v_\mathrm{A} = B_\parallel/\rho^{1/2}$ of each medium ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961). A perpendicular field can suppress mixing as well, since the shear bends it into a stabilising parallel component ([Ryu, Jones and Frank](#ryu-et-al-2000) 2000; [Das and Gronke](#das-gronke-2023) 2023). **[GK]** The linear theory of an incompressible vortex sheet with parallel fields gives stability for $\rho_\mathrm{hot}\rho_\mathrm{cold}\,v_\mathrm{shear}^2 < (\rho_\mathrm{hot} + \rho_\mathrm{cold})(B_\mathrm{hot}^2 + B_\mathrm{cold}^2)$ in these units. A uniform field thus stabilises the flow for $v_\mathrm{shear} < [2(v_\mathrm{A,hot}^2 + v_\mathrm{A,cold}^2)]^{1/2}$, close to $\sqrt{2}\,v_\mathrm{A,hot}$ at large $\chi$. For a uniform field the geometric mean of the review equals $v_\mathrm{A,hot}\,\chi^{-1/4}$, below this threshold by a factor of about $\sqrt{2}\,\chi^{1/4}$.

The linear growth of the KH instability is well understood and serves as a benchmark for codes ([Lecoanet et al.](#lecoanet-et-al-2015) 2015). Its non-linear stage, in which turbulence and secondary instabilities develop the mixing layer in full, needs simulations ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Radiative cooling changes the nature of the instability, since it lets mass pass continuously between the phases, and the review turns to this case.

#### 2.2.2 The model of Begelman and Fabian

The first simulations of radiative turbulent mixing layers ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006; [Kwak and Shelton](#kwak-shelton-2010) 2010; [Kwak, Shelton and Henley](#kwak-et-al-2015) 2015; [Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) 2019) followed the analytic work of [Begelman and Fabian](#begelman-fabian-1990) (1990), which [Slavin, Shull and Begelman](#slavin-et-al-1993) (1993) extended ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Begelman and Fabian predicted that the mean temperature of the layer is the average weighted by the fluxes of mass into it across the interface, $\bar T = (\dot m_\mathrm{hot} T_\mathrm{hot} + \dot m_\mathrm{cold} T_\mathrm{cold})/(\dot m_\mathrm{hot} + \dot m_\mathrm{cold})$ (eq. 2). Most of the gas belongs to the hot or the cold phase, but the gas at $\bar T$ dominates the cooling, so that a mixing layer is usefully treated as a medium of three phases. The hot gas enters at $\dot m_\mathrm{hot} \sim \eta_\mathrm{hot}\,\rho_\mathrm{hot}\,v_\mathrm{mix} A$ (eq. 3), with the area $A$ of the layer parallel to the flow, a characteristic velocity of mixing $v_\mathrm{mix}$, and an efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{hot}$ for the imperfect entrainment of hot gas. It carries in enthalpy at the rate $\dot e \sim (5/2)\,\eta_\mathrm{hot} P v_\mathrm{mix}$, at the pressure $P$. The model assumes that this inflow forms eddies, of scales $l_\mathrm{cold}$ below the full thickness $l_\mathrm{t}$ of the layer, which pull cold gas in at $\dot m_\mathrm{cold} \sim \eta_\mathrm{cold}\,\rho_\mathrm{cold}\,l_\mathrm{cold} A/t_\mathrm{KH}$ (eq. 4), with an efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{cold}$ and the growth time $t_\mathrm{KH} \sim (\rho_\mathrm{cold}/\rho_\mathrm{hot})^{1/2}\,l_\mathrm{cold}/v_\mathrm{mix}$. With efficiencies of similar size, $\eta_\mathrm{cold} \sim \eta_\mathrm{hot}$, eq. 2 gives the geometric mean of the two temperatures, $\bar T \sim (T_\mathrm{cold} T_\mathrm{hot})^{1/2}$. Derivations from geometry or from the fluxes of mass and enthalpy reach the same temperature ([Hillier and Arregui](#hillier-arregui-2019) 2019; [Faucher-Giguère and Oh](#faucher-giguere-oh-2023) 2023, §3.4; footnote 7). **[GK]** The review writes eq. 4 without the area $A$, which a flux of mass needs, as in eq. 3. The two fluxes then stand in the ratio $\dot m_\mathrm{cold}/\dot m_\mathrm{hot} \sim \chi^{1/2}$, and with pressure equilibrium, $\chi = T_\mathrm{hot}/T_\mathrm{cold}$, eq. 2 yields the geometric mean exactly.

The model does not predict the efficiencies, so even with $\eta_\mathrm{hot} \sim \eta_\mathrm{cold}$ it cannot predict the inflow of eq. 3, on which the existence and growth of the phases depend ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). It also relies on rapid mixing and leaves out cooling and viscosity, which may act back on the turbulence ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006). Studies differ on its validity, but agree within a factor of about five that it predicts the abundances of the ions of intermediate temperature ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006; [Kwak and Shelton](#kwak-shelton-2010) 2010; [Kwak, Shelton and Henley](#kwak-et-al-2015) 2015), while [Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) (2019) may find larger deviations. Such simulations must reach a steady state in a box wide enough across the flow, which some runs of [Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) (2006) do not ([Kwak and Shelton](#kwak-shelton-2010) 2010; [Kwak, Shelton and Henley](#kwak-et-al-2015) 2015; footnote 8). Later work points out that the abundances of these ions depend on the distribution of temperature, which needs thermal conduction (2.2.3).

#### 2.2.3 Mixing with cooling

Later work maps how the inflow of hot gas, and the structure and dynamics of the layer, depend on the geometry of the flow and on other parameters ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). The hot gas flows in at about the sound speed of the cold gas, $v_\mathrm{mix} \sim c_\mathrm{s,cold}$. The inflow scales as $\dot m \propto t_\mathrm{cool}^{-1/2}$ when cooling is slow ([Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) 2019; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021), and as $\dot m \propto t_\mathrm{cool}^{-1/4}$ when it is fast ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) 2020a; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021; [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) 2020). Slow and fast refer to the time of mixing, $t_\mathrm{mix} \sim \ell/u'$ with the local turbulent velocity $u'$, and the Damköhler number $\mathrm{Da} \equiv t_\mathrm{mix}/t_\mathrm{cool}$ separates the two regimes at unity. [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) (2021) drew the analogy with the fronts of turbulent combustion, in which gas burns on a time of reaction where in a mixing layer it cools on $t_\mathrm{cool}$. Fig. 4 shows a schematic of a mixing layer after [Slavin, Shull and Begelman](#slavin-et-al-1993) (1993), a surface of constant temperature in a simulation of [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) (2020), and the two regimes of [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) (2021), which differ in morphology and in the energy that they radiate.

The interface of a mixing layer is fractal ([Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) 2020; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021). [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) (2020) showed this in simulations and built an analytic model on the fractal shape ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Its scalings differ slightly from those of the model of [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) (2021), which draws on combustion, but the simulations show that mixing, followed by cooling, is the process that matters under approximate pressure equilibrium ([Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) 2020; [Chen, Fielding and Bryan](#chen-et-al-2022) 2022).

The turnover time of an eddy grows with its size, as $t_\mathrm{eddy} \propto \ell^{2/3}$ in a Kolmogorov cascade, so the largest scales are the bottleneck of mixing and cooling ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). The morphology of the layers is therefore generally not converged, while the rates of mass transfer are ([Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021). Since turbulent diffusion dominates, thermal conduction matters little for the transfer of mass and for the total luminosity of the layer ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021). It matters much for the full distribution of temperature and for the emission of the single ions of intermediate temperature ([Tan and Oh](#tan-oh-2021) 2021). 2.6.3 draws the consequences for resolution.

#### 2.2.4 Higher Mach numbers and magnetic fields

Most of these studies treat subsonic and transonic shear ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). [Yang and Ji](#yang-ji-2022) (2022) extended them to higher Mach numbers, for slow cooling, $\mathrm{Da} < 1$. The turbulent velocity saturates at $\mathcal{M} \gtrsim 1$, and the flux of mass reverses, so that cold gas evaporates. How more efficient cooling changes this has not yet been explored. [Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) (2019), also for $\mathrm{Da} < 1$, and more recently [Das and Gronke](#das-gronke-2023) (2023) studied magnetic fields in radiative mixing layers. The fields grow to equipartition and, depending on their orientation, can strongly suppress the flux of mass from the hot to the cold medium. They do so above all for fast cooling, $\mathrm{Da} > 1$, where mixing is the bottleneck.

### 2.5 Measures of mixing

Most simulations of multiphase gas ask, in some form, for the thermal state of the medium ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.3). The full distribution of the gas in the plane of temperature and density captures it (Fig. 2, right). In practice it is often binned into ranges of temperature, which give the mass and the volume of each phase. The ranges differ between studies and between fields, which makes their results harder to compare. For the evolution in time, the fluxes between the phases, of mass, $\dot m$, or of momentum, $\dot p$, are of particular interest, and at the coarsest level whether a phase exists or vanishes.

The kinematics of the phases, how they move with respect to each other, is measured by the mean difference of velocity between two phases, $\Delta v$, weighted by mass or taken at given places, or by the distribution of the gas in temperature and velocity ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.3). The turbulent motions are analysed through velocity structure functions (VSF) and spectra of kinetic power. The spatial distribution and morphology of the phases link simulations to observations, through the filling fractions of area and volume, the distributions of the sizes of clumps and the coherence of the phases, which also show how gas fragments and mixes. Codes of radiative transfer, such as SKIRT ([Camps and Baes](#camps-baes-2015) 2015) and Trident ([Hummels, Smith and Silvia](#hummels-et-al-2017) 2017), turn simulations into synthetic spectra, to be compared with the lines of sight towards quasars and with other spectral diagnostics.

### 2.6 Numerical resolution and convergence

#### 2.6.1 The difficulty of multiphase media

The contrasts of temperature in astrophysics, and the contrasts of density that come with them, range from about $10^2$ to $\gtrsim 10^{10}$, which strains numerical methods and makes adaptive techniques such as smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) problematic ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.2). The phases have different length scales, so the problem is multi-scale. In the CGM and the ICM, cold gas can moreover lie in very small structures spread over a large volume. Adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), which adapts the size of the cells to the local density, then becomes inadequate or too expensive. Observations find cold structures in the CGM as small as tens of parsecs. With at least eight cells across a structure (footnote 5), a halo of the mass of the Milky Way would need $(8 \times 100\,\mathrm{kpc}/10\,\mathrm{pc})^3 \approx 5 \times 10^{14}$ resolution elements, of order $10^{15}$, against about $10^{11}$ in the largest simulations of fixed resolution to date (footnote 6, §3.1). Multiphase quantities such as the mass of cold gas are therefore not converged in typical cosmological simulations ([Faucher-Giguère et al.](#faucher-giguere-et-al-2016) 2016), as simulations that refine the CGM systematically have shown (§2.8.3).

The physics adds to the difficulty ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.2). Turbulence mixes and destroys the phases, and radiative cooling and heating exchange mass and momentum between them. Thermal conduction and viscosity shape the temperature and the turbulence, metals, molecules and their chemistry set the cooling, and magnetic fields and cosmic rays add pressure and act on the mixing directly. The nature of the heating and of the drivers of turbulence matters as well. Since no simulation can yet treat all the scales and processes together, most address particular questions or systems.

#### 2.6.2 No universal criterion

For gravitational collapse, the criterion of [Truelove et al.](#truelove-et-al-1997) (1997) sets a clear and widely adopted demand on resolution, but no single criterion holds for multiphase flows ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.3). The scale to resolve depends on the quantity of interest. Converged rates of mass transfer, morphologies or kinematics generally need the characteristic scale of the process resolved by at least a few cells. §2 of the review names these scales together with the diagnostics that they make converge, and the subjects of this synthesis take them up in turn. **[GK]** The criterion of [Truelove et al.](#truelove-et-al-1997) (1997) asks that the Jeans length span at least four cells.

#### 2.6.3 Mixing layers

In mixing layers, the largest eddies limit mixing and cooling (2.2.3), so that larger simulations need only the driving scale of a layer resolved for its rate of mass transfer to converge ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). A comparison with observations of ions such as O VI needs in addition the Field length, after [Field](#field-1965) (1965), to be resolved, or its effect to be added in post-processing. With the conduction of [Spitzer](#spitzer-1962) (1962), the Field length shrinks steeply towards low temperatures, as $T^{5/2}$ by the review, so a simulation may leave it unresolved below a cutoff temperature. [Tan and Oh](#tan-oh-2021) (2021) showed that the distribution of temperature converges only above this cutoff (footnote 10). [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) (2020) showed that a layer that does not resolve the smallest value of $c_\mathrm{s} t_\mathrm{cool}$, the shattering length that §2.4 of the review treats, develops numerical dips of pressure across it (footnote 9). Such simulations must also reach a steady state in a box wide enough across the flow (2.2.2). **[GK]** The Field length is the scale on which conduction balances cooling, $\kappa T/\lambda_\mathrm{F}^2 \sim n^2 \Lambda(T)$, with the conductivity $\kappa$ and the cooling function $\Lambda$. With $\kappa \propto T^{5/2}$ and $n \propto 1/T$ at fixed pressure, $\lambda_\mathrm{F} \propto T^{11/4}\,\Lambda(T)^{-1/2}$. The power $5/2$ that the review gives holds where $\Lambda \propto T^{1/2}$, as for bremsstrahlung.
