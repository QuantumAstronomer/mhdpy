# Elliptic solvers

## 1. Overview

Elliptic equations have no time dependence: their solution is set everywhere at once by the source and the boundaries. They arise for the gravitational potential, $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, for implicit steps of diffusion, as the Helmholtz equation $(\alpha_\mathrm{H} - \nabla\cdot\beta_\mathrm{H}\nabla)\psi = f$, and for projections onto divergence-free fields. Every cell couples to every other, so that the methods differ entirely from those of hyperbolic equations and need global communication. The references use Fourier transforms on periodic grids, in Zingale's text, Ulula and Athena++, relaxation and geometric multigrid, in Zingale's text, pyro and Athena++, and sparse-matrix solvers in ZEUS, which also bounds isolated systems by a multipole expansion, as Athena++ does. Athena++ runs multigrid on its refined meshes over all levels at once ([Tomida and Stone](#tomida-stone-2023) 2023). The coupling of gravity to the flow follows in topic 15, implicit diffusion in topic 16 and the transfer between refined levels in topic 40. Tree methods, the fast multipole method and algebraic multigrid are only named.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Discretisation and the Fourier method

On a cell-centred grid the gradient is differenced to the faces and back, which gives the five-point Laplacian of second order, seven-point in three dimensions ([Zingale's text](#zingale-2025), eqs. 9.5 to 9.10):

$$\frac{\psi_{i+1,j} - 2\psi_{i,j} + \psi_{i-1,j}}{\Delta x^2} + \frac{\psi_{i,j+1} - 2\psi_{i,j} + \psi_{i,j-1}}{\Delta y^2} = f_{i,j}.$$

With constant coefficients and periodic boundaries, the fast Fourier transform, at a cost of order $N\log N$ for $N$ cells, turns this into one algebraic equation per mode (eqs. 9.11 to 9.17). For a mode of wavenumbers $(\kappa_x, \kappa_y)$,

$$\hat\psi_\kappa = \frac{\hat f_\kappa}{2\left[(\cos\kappa_x\Delta x - 1)/\Delta x^2 + (\cos\kappa_y\Delta y - 1)/\Delta y^2\right]},$$

which with the eigenvalues of the discrete Laplacian solves the difference equation exactly, while $-|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|^2$ would solve the continuous one. The mode $\kappa = 0$ has a vanishing denominator and must be set apart: Athena++ sets it to zero, which removes the mean density, and Ulula sets its denominator to one. Ulula solves its self-gravity this way ([Diemer](#diemer-2025) 2025, eqs. 28 and 29), and Athena++ with FFTW and parallel transforms that remap the data between decompositions, on uniformly spaced meshes. The method fails for variable coefficients, whose modes couple, and suits other boundaries only through series of sines or cosines.

Detail: [Zingale 2.1.8](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#218-discrete-fourier-transforms), [Zingale 2.6.1](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#261-elliptic-equations-and-their-discretisation), [Zingale 2.6.2](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#262-the-fourier-method), [Ulula 2.7.3](../references/ulula-synthesis.md#273-self-gravity-by-fast-fourier-transform), [Athena 2.10.1](../references/athena-synthesis.md#2101-self-gravity-by-fast-fourier-transform).

### 2.2 Relaxation, boundaries and solvability

Relaxation solves the difference equation for $\psi_{i,j}$ and iterates, as Jacobi iteration from the old values everywhere or Gauss–Seidel in place, $\psi_{i,j} \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{4}(\psi_{i+1,j} + \psi_{i-1,j} + \psi_{i,j+1} + \psi_{i,j-1} - \Delta x^2 f_{i,j})$ for $\Delta x = \Delta y$ (eqs. 9.21 to 9.28). Red–black Gauss–Seidel updates the cells of one colour of a checkerboard and then the other, which parallelises. Tomida and Stone (2023, §2.2) read relaxation as the pseudo-time evolution $\partial\psi/\partial\tau = \nabla^2\psi - f$ towards its steady state, so that long wavelengths diffuse slowly: Jacobi needs of order $N_x^2$ iterations for $N_x$ cells per direction, and over-relaxation by a factor $\omega_\mathrm{r}$ near $2/(1 + \pi/N_x)$ of order $N_x$. The defect $d = f - L\psi$ of the discrete operator $L$ measures how well the discrete equation holds and falls to round-off, while the error against the true solution stalls at the truncation error. A Dirichlet value $\psi_\mathrm{b}$ on the boundary face enters the ghost cell as $\psi_{\mathrm{lo}-1} = 2\psi_\mathrm{b} - \psi_\mathrm{lo}$ and a Neumann gradient as $\psi_{\mathrm{lo}-1} = \psi_\mathrm{lo} - \Delta x\,\psi'_\mathrm{b}$, both of second order (topic 4). Setting the ghost cell to the boundary value itself drops the whole solution to first order, as every cell feels the boundary. With periodic or Neumann boundaries all round, the divergence theorem requires the source to integrate to the flux through the boundary, zero in the homogeneous case, and the solution is fixed only up to a constant (eq. 9.73), so that the codes subtract the mean of the source and of the solution.

Detail: [Zingale 2.6.3](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#263-relaxation-and-boundary-conditions), [Zingale 2.6.4](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#264-residual-error-and-the-cost-of-smoothing), [Zingale 2.6.7](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#267-solvability), [pyro 2.6.2](../references/pyro-synthesis.md#262-boundary-conditions), [Athena 2.10.2](../references/athena-synthesis.md#2102-multigrid-the-hierarchy-of-grids-and-its-operators).

### 2.3 Multigrid

Relaxation removes short wavelengths, measured in cells, within a few sweeps, but on a coarser grid a long wavelength becomes short, which is the idea of multigrid (Zingale's text, §9.4). The defect obeys the error equation $L\,\delta\psi = d$, which the coarser levels solve. A V-cycle on level $l$, with level 0 the coarsest, runs as follows.

1. Smooth the equation of the level a few times by red–black Gauss–Seidel.
2. Compute the defect $d_l = f_l - L_l\psi_l$.
3. Restrict it by the volume-weighted mean of the $2^D$ fine cells of each coarse cell in $D$ dimensions, as the source of the error equation on level $l - 1$, which starts from zero.
4. Repeat down to the coarsest level, a few cells or one, and solve it by smoothing or exactly.
5. Prolong the correction to the finer level, linearly, $\psi^\mathrm{f} = \psi^\mathrm{c} \pm m_x/4 \pm m_y/4$ with the centred slopes $m_x$ and $m_y$, or trilinearly in three dimensions, add it and smooth again.

Inhomogeneous boundaries apply on the finest level, homogeneous ones of the same type on the coarser levels, or boundary charges move them into the source. Cycles repeat until $\|d\| < \epsilon_\mathrm{tol}\|f\|$, each reducing the defect by about an order of magnitude at a cost of order $N$ in all. Full multigrid (FMG), after [Brandt](#brandt-1977) (1977), restricts the source to all levels, solves the coarsest and builds the solution upwards with an interpolation of third order, weights $(5, 30, -3)/32$ along each axis, and one V-cycle per level. It reaches the truncation error in one sweep and needs no initial guess. The full approximation scheme (FAS) restricts the solution as well and solves on each level for $f_{l-1} = d_{l-1} + L_{l-1}\bar\psi_{l-1}$, with $\bar\psi$ the restricted solution, which nonlinear problems and refined meshes require. For $\nabla\cdot(\eta\nabla\psi) = f$ the coefficient is averaged to the faces, $\eta_{i-1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{i-1} + \eta_i)$, and restricted as a face quantity. pyro runs V-cycles with ten smoothings per level and fifty on a grid of $2 \times 2$ cells, on square domains of $2^n$ cells, for constant, variable and general coefficients. Athena++ runs V(1,1) cycles with an over-relaxation of 1.15 in three dimensions, with FMG recommended, on blocks of $2^n$ cells per side, and gathers the coarsest cell of every block into a root grid on every process.

### 2.4 Sparse-matrix solvers and isolated boundaries

On its nonuniform curvilinear mesh ZEUS writes the covariant Laplacian as a five-point molecule, a sparse, banded matrix that is symmetric and positive definite, and solves it by the alternating direction implicit method, the incomplete Cholesky conjugate gradient or GMRES, the last two in practice ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). It solves only where a trial potential from the stored one changes by more than about $10^{-5}$, which cuts the solutions to about one cycle in ten. An isolated system takes its boundary values from a multipole expansion about the origin,

$$\Phi_\mathrm{b} = -G\sum_l \frac{P_l(\mu_\mathrm{b})}{r_\mathrm{b}^{l+1}}\,Q_l, \qquad Q_l = \int_V \rho\,r^lP_l(\mu)\,dV,$$

with the Legendre polynomials $P_l$ of the cosine $\mu$ of the angle to the axis, summed until successive boundary values agree to $10^{-3}$. Athena++ expands in real spherical harmonics up to $l = 4$ about the centre of mass, which removes the dipole, and sets each ghost value to twice the expansion on the face less the active value. The expansion suits a central concentration far from the boundaries and converges slowly where mass lies near them. **[GK]** The paper prints the expansion with a positive sign and the diagonal of its matrix with the sign of the neighbours, which holds for a potential of the opposite sign to that of its equation of motion, the convention the code adopts.

Detail: [ZEUS 2.6](../references/zeus-synthesis.md#26-self-gravity), [Athena 2.10.3](../references/athena-synthesis.md#2103-multigrid-the-driver-its-boundaries-and-its-parallelisation).

### 2.5 Multigrid on refined meshes

Solvers that treat each refined level by itself, with boundaries from the coarser one, are inconsistent, as the coarse solution misses the fine structure and hands wrong boundaries down (Tomida and Stone 2023, §3.3). Athena++ solves over all levels together with FAS. Below the level at which every block is one cell, sets of $2 \times 2 \times 2$ cells, octets, follow the tree of refined blocks down to the root grid. At a boundary between levels, the gradient on a coarse face must equal the mean of those on the four fine faces it covers,

$$\frac{\psi_{I,J,K} - \psi_{I-1,J,K}}{2h} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{a,b}\frac{\psi_{i,j+a,k+b} - \psi_{i-1,j+a,k+b}}{h},$$

with $h$ the fine width, which keeps the truncation error of the gradient free of divergence and so creates no spurious mass. The fine block sets each ghost cell to a third of its active neighbour plus two thirds of the coarse value interpolated to its position, and the coarse block extrapolates its ghost value as $\tfrac{4}{3}$ of the fine side less $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of its own cell. The time step is uniform on all levels, as gravity acts at once across the mesh.

Detail: [Athena 2.10.4](../references/athena-synthesis.md#2104-multigrid-on-refined-meshes-and-self-gravity-by-multigrid).

## 3. Tests

Problems with known potentials test the solvers. Zingale's text and pyro solve sources built from smooth functions that meet the boundary conditions, such as $\psi = (x^2 - x^4)(y^4 - y^2)$ on the unit square, and $\nabla\cdot(\eta\nabla\psi) = f$ with $\eta = 2 + \cos 2\pi x\cos 2\pi y$, on grids of 16 to 512 cells for the order of convergence. ZEUS computes a uniform sphere, a centrally condensed sphere and a homogeneous ellipsoid in cylindrical and spherical coordinates, with and without equatorial symmetry, and compares potential and gradient. Athena++ solves a periodic sinusoidal density on grids of $16^3$ to $1024^3$ cells and a binary of two uniform spheres on a mesh refined in four levels with multipole boundaries, following the defect and the errors against the discrete and the analytic solutions cycle by cycle.

Detail: [Zingale 2.6.2](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#262-the-fourier-method), [Zingale 2.6.6](../references/computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md#266-multigrid-cycles), [pyro 2.6.4](../references/pyro-synthesis.md#264-examples-and-tests), [ZEUS 2.7.5](../references/zeus-synthesis.md#275-tests-of-the-poisson-solver), [Athena 2.10.5](../references/athena-synthesis.md#2105-tests-of-multigrid).

## 4. Assessment

pyro's errors fall from $4.07 \times 10^{-4}$ on 16 cells to $1.60 \times 10^{-6}$ on 256, by factors of 3.97 to 4.00, the perfect second order, and Zingale's multigrid reaches round-off in the defect within a dozen V-cycles. In ZEUS the errors of the potential lie between $4 \times 10^{-5}$ and $8 \times 10^{-3}$, and the largest error of the gradient, 6.4 per cent for the ellipsoid without symmetry in cylindrical coordinates, falls to 1.7 per cent in spherical ones, most of it at the surface. In Athena++ each V-cycle reduces the defect by a factor of about 0.125, the first sweep of FMG already reaches the truncation error, and a few further cycles remove the noise of the red–black pattern. On the binary, the converged solution errs by a few $10^{-4}$ outside the refined blocks, against about one per cent for a solution of the root level alone, which a level-by-level solver would hand down. Multigrid takes a small part of the time of the MHD, but its performance falls within a node, as it is bound by the bandwidth of memory, and many small blocks make its serial root grid significant. **[GK]** Multigrid is the method of choice for the general case: it costs of order $N$, works with any boundaries, variable coefficients, curvilinear grids and refinement, and stays consistent across levels if built with FAS and conservative level boundaries. FFT is simpler and exact for the discrete operator on uniform periodic grids, where it is hard to beat, but it scales as $N\log N$, needs all-to-all communication and does not suit refinement or isolated systems without padding. Sparse-matrix solvers are general but need preconditioning on irregular grids. Open are how far the solver needs to converge per step, whether isolated boundaries need more than a multipole expansion, and how a solver is structured so that gravity, implicit diffusion and radiation share it.
