# Synthesis of Athena and Athena++

This synthesis digests Athena++, a code for astrophysical magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) on meshes with adaptive refinement, written in C++, and Athena, its predecessor written in C. The code of Athena++ is kept as `reference-works/Athena/Code`, in a copy that is cited at release v24.0 (1.1). Thirteen papers complete the sources, kept in `reference-works/Athena/Docs`: seven on Athena, whose code is not among the sources, and six on Athena++ (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in these sources or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. Code is cited by its path in the repository and the name of a function or class, as in (`src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/hlld.cpp`, `Hydro::RiemannSolver`). A paper is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local version, as in [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, [arXiv:0804.0402](https://arxiv.org/abs/0804.0402), §4.2.3, eq. 46), and its arXiv identifier is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Each method is described once, from the papers and the code together: the papers on Athena tell what Athena does, and the code and the papers on Athena++ what Athena++ does. Where the two codes differ, the text says so.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, §1) explain the need for Athena from the limits of ZEUS ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, [b](#stone-norman-1992b); [Clarke](#clarke-1996) 1996; [Hayes et al.](#hayes-et-al-2006) 2006), digested in `zeus-synthesis.md`. The operator-split algorithm of ZEUS, with upwind advection of higher order, centred differences for the other terms and artificial viscosity at shocks, had served in many hundreds of astrophysical studies and was easy to extend with further physics. In the fifteen years since, refining the mesh, statically (SMR) or adaptively (AMR), had become the means to resolve wide ranges of scale on grids. Conservation at the internal boundaries between coarse and fine meshes is needed to prevent spurious reflections there ([Berger and Colella](#berger-colella-1989) 1989), so a scheme that does not solve the equations in conservative form, such as that of ZEUS, does not suit refined meshes. This was the main motive for Athena. Its algorithms are unsplit Godunov methods of higher order, which suit SMR and AMR and capture shocks, contact discontinuities and rotational discontinuities better. Other MHD codes for AMR on this basis already existed: RIEMANN ([Balsara](#balsara-2000) 2000), BATS-R-US ([Powell et al.](#powell-et-al-1999) 1999; [Gombosi et al.](#gombosi-et-al-2004) 2004), AMRVAC ([Tóth](#toth-1996) 1996; [Nool and Keppens](#nool-keppens-2002) 2002), Nirvana ([Ziegler](#ziegler-2005) 2005), RAMSES ([Fromang, Hennebelle and Teyssier](#fromang-et-al-2006) 2006), PLUTO ([Mignone et al.](#mignone-et-al-2007) 2007) and AstroBEAR ([Cunningham et al.](#cunningham-et-al-2007) 2007). The authors justify another code by their experience that the details of an algorithm matter, and those of Athena differ from the others in small or substantial ways. They wanted the code to be accurate, simple to use and to adapt, and easy to maintain, and meant the paper as a reference for anyone who adopts, modifies or extends it.

Godunov methods for MHD had advanced mainly in two respects, the integrator in several dimensions and the way the magnetic field is kept free of divergence (§1). The options tried ranged from integrators split by direction that do not constrain the divergence at all to split and unsplit integrators combined with a Hodge projection of the field, with a non-conservative form of the equations that lets errors in the constraint propagate and decay, or with constrained transport (CT) after [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988). [Tóth](#toth-2000) (2000) compared many of them on a broad suite of tests. Athena discretises mass, momentum and energy as volume averages over cells and the magnetic field as area averages over the faces of cells, which CT evolves (abstract). Its paper names three new elements. The first is a consistent way to obtain the electric fields that CT needs, averaged over time and over the edges of cells, from the Godunov fluxes, which are averages over time and faces. The second extends to MHD the reconstruction schemes whose prediction in time is split by direction, such as PPM after [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984). The third extends two unsplit integrators to MHD: the corner transport upwind (CTU) method of [Colella](#colella-1990) (1990), which Athena combines with CT as CTU+CT, and a simpler predictor-corrector scheme from the appendix of [Falle](#falle-1991) (1991), close to the MUSCL–Hancock scheme ([van Leer](#van-leer-2006) 2006; [Toro](#toro-1999) 1999), as VL+CT (§1). [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, [arXiv:astro-ph/0501557](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0501557)) set out the mathematics in two dimensions and [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) (2008, [arXiv:0712.2634](https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.2634)) in three, while the paper on the code describes the implementation. CTU+CT proved less diffusive and more accurate, so that paper describes it and leaves VL+CT, which has advantages in some applications, to a companion paper, which it cites as submitted in 2008. **[GK]** That paper appeared as [Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2009) (2009), which is not among the sources, as 1.3 explains.

The summary of the paper on Athena stresses what the combination achieves (§9). With volume and area averages, mass, momentum, energy and magnetic flux stay exactly conserved on the grid, which AMR requires. The tests show that smooth flows of every family of MHD waves converge at second order in space and time, also in several dimensions. The unsplit methods keep the symmetries of a flow: the CTU+CT method in two dimensions reduces exactly to the method in one dimension for plane-parallel flows aligned with the grid, and in three dimensions to those in two or one. On this property rests a sensitive test of the stencil that keeps the field free of divergence. A planar loop of field advected by a velocity field in three dimensions must stay planar, since any field perpendicular to its plane grows in proportion to $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$. The refinement of the mesh, the main motive for the code, was left to a later paper, together with the CTU+CT integrator on refined meshes. Gravity from a fixed potential and self-gravity, the shearing box ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2006) 2006), anisotropic thermal conduction ([Parrish and Stone](#parrish-stone-2005) 2005, [2007](#parrish-stone-2007)) and the transfer of ionising radiation ([Krumholz, Stone and Gardiner](#krumholz-et-al-2007) 2007) had been added, and curvilinear coordinates, relativistic MHD and radiation MHD with full transport were under way. Athena already served to study the magnetorotational instability (MRI) in the shearing box, colliding winds in close binaries, the decay of turbulence, the magnetic Rayleigh–Taylor instability, shocks striking magnetised clouds and supersonic turbulence in molecular clouds. The code was available on the web, with documentation and further test problems.

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020, §1) place Athena++ in a trend towards ever greater complexity among the codes of astrophysical fluid dynamics. Realistic models need further physics, among it the transfer of radiation, self-gravity, networks of chemical or nuclear reactions and dynamical spacetimes, grids adopt one of several strategies of AMR, codes offer many options of coordinates, Riemann solvers and orders of accuracy, and computers grow more heterogeneous. Among the widely used grid codes they name ZEUS ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, [b](#stone-norman-1992b); [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) 1992; [Hayes et al.](#hayes-et-al-2006) 2006), ART ([Kravtsov, Klypin and Khokhlov](#kravtsov-et-al-1997) 1997), FLASH ([Fryxell et al.](#fryxell-et-al-2000) 2000), RAMSES ([Teyssier](#teyssier-2002) 2002), HARM ([Gammie, McKinney and Tóth](#gammie-et-al-2003) 2003), PLUTO ([Mignone et al.](#mignone-et-al-2007) 2007, [2012](#mignone-et-al-2012)), Athena and Enzo ([Bryan et al.](#bryan-et-al-2014) 2014). Athena itself had grown in this way. It gained further time integrators ([Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2009) 2009), the shearing box ([Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2010) 2010, [arXiv:1006.0139](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.0139)), cylindrical coordinates ([Skinner and Ostriker](#skinner-ostriker-2010) 2010, [arXiv:1004.2487](https://arxiv.org/abs/1004.2487)), special relativity ([Beckwith and Stone](#beckwith-stone-2011) 2011, [arXiv:1101.3573](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.3573)), particles ([Bai and Stone](#bai-stone-2010) 2010, [arXiv:1005.4980](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.4980)) and sink particles ([Gong and Ostriker](#gong-ostriker-2013) 2013). It also gained a formulation of self-gravity that conserves the total energy ([Jiang et al.](#jiang-et-al-2013) 2013) and the transport of radiation ([Skinner and Ostriker](#skinner-ostriker-2013) 2013; [Davis, Stone and Jiang](#davis-et-al-2012) 2012; [Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2012) 2012, [2014a](#jiang-et-al-2014a)), among other features. Maintaining it had become untenable, and its AMR, built on overlapping patches ([Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) 1984; [Berger and Colella](#berger-colella-1989) 1989), performed poorly on highly parallel machines. Athena++ is therefore a complete redesign. Its framework separates the mesh from the physics solved on it, refines the mesh by blocks rather than patches, and schedules the steps of an algorithm dynamically through lists of tasks (3). The language C++ was chosen for the modularity and organisation that its extensions allow.

The paper on Athena++ introduces its framework for AMR, with refinement by blocks of variables at cell centres and on faces, as MHD requires, its use in various coordinate systems and its dynamic execution by task lists (§1). As examples of modules of physics, it describes solvers for MHD, both non-relativistic and relativistic, based on the methods of Athena. It confines itself to what is new in the framework, since [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, [2008](#gardiner-stone-2008)), [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008), [Beckwith and Stone](#beckwith-stone-2011) (2011) and [White, Stone and Gammie](#white-et-al-2016) (2016, [arXiv:1511.00943](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.00943)) describe the algorithms in detail. Its abstract adds that these modules support curvilinear coordinates, time integrators of higher order, a general EOS and diffusion that super time-stepping can integrate, and that they run with well over 80% parallel efficiency on more than half a million threads. Further modules were in preparation (§5). Self-gravity was solved by FFT, following Athena, in the public version, and by full multigrid (FMG), which costs $O(N)$ operations against $O(N \log N)$ for FFT. FMG was being extended to AMR ([Tomida et al.](#tomida-et-al-2023), in preparation), and was to serve implicit moment methods of radiation transfer as well ([Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2012) 2012). The time-dependent radiation transport of [Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2014a) (2014a) was implemented and used for radiation-dominated accretion discs ([Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2014b) 2014b) and massive stars ([Jiang et al.](#jiang-et-al-2018) 2018), and was being extended to general relativity. Flux-limited diffusion and the variable Eddington tensor ([Davis, Stone and Jiang](#davis-et-al-2012) 2012; [Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2012) 2012), a Monte Carlo solver for post-processing and the adaptive ray tracing of Athena ([Kim et al.](#kim-et-al-2017) 2017) were to follow. Chemical reaction networks were implemented with a public solver for sparse matrices ([Gong et al.](#gong-et-al-2023), in preparation), and nuclear networks were being built on the same basis ([Halevi et al.](#halevi-et-al), in preparation). Together with passive scalars, they make Athena++ a code for chemo-hydrodynamics. A general module for particles coupled to the gas, based on the methods of [Bai and Stone](#bai-stone-2010) (2010), was under development ([Yang et al.](#yang-et-al), in preparation). In the copy, the multigrid solver works on refined meshes (2.10), radiation transport is present with explicit and implicit integration and with groups of frequencies, and chemistry and cosmic rays have modules of their own (2.15), while particles have none. **[GK]** The works in preparation on multigrid and chemistry appeared as [Tomida and Stone](#tomida-stone-2023) (2023, [arXiv:2302.13903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13903)), one of the sources, and as [Gong et al.](#gong-et-al-2023) (2023), listed under [Gong et al.](#gong-et-al-2023) in 4.

The summary of the paper on Athena++ restates the design and adds its results (§6). The framework accepts any logically rectangular coordinates and nonuniform spacing. Different regions of a calculation may carry different task lists, so that, for instance, kinetic MHD in dense, weakly collisional regions could be combined with particle-in-cell methods in diffuse, collisionless ones, and the Poisson equation could be solved on cores other than those for the MHD. The MHD modules extend the methods of Athena by reconstruction of higher order on curvilinear and nonuniform meshes, time integrators of higher order by the method of lines, and diffusion advanced by super time-stepping of the Runge–Kutta–Legendre type, and they work with AMR. Optimised for vector instructions, the MHD module is among the fastest public astrophysical MHD codes known to the authors, in tests of several codes built with the same compilers and optimisations, matched only by DISPATCH ([Nordlund et al.](#nordlund-et-al-2018) 2018). All cores of one Intel Skylake processor together are about five times slower than the same algorithm on an NVIDIA Volta GPU ([Grete, Glines and O'Shea](#grete-et-al-2019) 2019), as the ratio of their peak performance leads one to expect, and weak scaling keeps 84% parallel efficiency with as many as 500,000 threads. A fully fourth-order algorithm for MHD ([Felker and Stone](#felker-stone-2018) 2018, [arXiv:1711.07439](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07439)) had been implemented in the framework and was being compared with the existing algorithms on astrophysical applications. The copy offers the fourth order for hydrodynamics only, since its reconstruction refuses it with MHD while the fourth-order CT is not yet merged (`src/reconstruct/reconstruction.cpp`, `Reconstruction::Reconstruction`; 2.7). A version of the whole framework built on the library Kokkos ([Edwards, Trott and Sunderland](#edwards-et-al-2014) 2014) was being prepared for release as open source. Athena++ is public on GitHub under the BSD licence, and new modules join the public version once they are thoroughly tested.

## 2. Numerical methods

Sixteen subjects group the numerical methods by component. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

Both codes solve the equations of MHD by a Godunov method. The hydrodynamic variables are averages over the volumes of cells, updated by the fluxes through their faces (2.1), while the magnetic field consists of averages over the faces, updated by the electric fields on the edges of cells so that it stays free of divergence (2.3). Each flux comes from a Riemann problem at a face (2.5), between two states that a reconstruction builds from the averages of the neighbouring cells (2.4). Athena advances in time with the CTU or the VL integrator, Athena++ with the VL2 integrator or Runge–Kutta methods by the method of lines (2.6).

### 2.1 The equations and the finite-volume discretisation

#### 2.1.1 The equations of ideal MHD

Athena solves the equations of ideal MHD in conservative form ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §2, eqs. 1 to 4). They read $\partial \rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0$ for the mass, $\partial (\rho \mathbf{v})/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v} \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{B} \mathbf{B} + \mathsf{P}^*) = 0$ for the momentum, $\partial E/\partial t + \nabla \cdot [(E + P^*) \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{B} (\mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{v})] = 0$ for the total energy and $\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t - \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) = 0$ for the field. Here $\mathsf{P}^*$ is the diagonal tensor whose components are the total pressure $P^* = P + B^2/2$, with $P$ the pressure of the gas, and the total energy density is $E = P/(\gamma - 1) + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$ (eq. 5). The units make the magnetic permeability $\mu = 1$. This energy assumes an ideal gas, $P = (\gamma - 1) e$, where $\gamma$ is the adiabatic index and $e$ the internal energy density. For a barotropic gas, $P = P(\rho)$, such as an isothermal one with $P = C^2 \rho$ and $C$ the isothermal sound speed, the energy equation is dropped, and the total energy is no longer conserved. Athena thus solves four systems, hydrodynamics or MHD with an ideal or a barotropic gas, which differ in number and form but share the numerical methods. A tabulated EOS would fit the same methods.

The conserved variables $\mathbf{U} = (\rho, M_x, M_y, M_z, E, B_x, B_y, B_z)$, with the momentum density $\mathbf{M} = \rho \mathbf{v}$, and the primitive variables $\mathbf{W} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, P, B_x, B_y, B_z)$ describe adiabatic MHD in Cartesian coordinates (eq. 6). The system becomes $\partial \mathbf{U}/\partial t + \partial \mathbf{F}/\partial x + \partial \mathbf{G}/\partial y + \partial \mathbf{H}/\partial z = 0$ (eq. 7). The flux in $x$ is $\mathbf{F} = (\rho v_x, \rho v_x^2 + P + B^2/2 - B_x^2, \rho v_x v_y - B_x B_y, \rho v_x v_z - B_x B_z, (E + P^*) v_x - (\mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{v}) B_x, 0, B_y v_x - B_x v_y, B_z v_x - B_x v_z)$ (eq. 8), and $\mathbf{G}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ follow by permuting the directions (eqs. 9, 10), each with a zero for the field component along its own direction. In hydrodynamics, or for a barotropic gas, the corresponding components are dropped. The last three components are the induction equation written for Cartesian coordinates, but the field is evolved by a quite different method (2.3). In curvilinear coordinates the fluxes need the scale factors of the metric, or the curvature of the grid enters as source terms, or both (2.8).

#### 2.1.2 Non-ideal MHD in Athena++

Athena++ solves the same equations with terms for non-ideal MHD: isotropic viscosity, thermal conduction, Ohmic resistivity and ambipolar diffusion in the limit of strong coupling ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.1, eqs. 6a to 6d). The viscous stress tensor is $\Pi_{ij} = \rho \nu (\partial v_i/\partial x_j + \partial v_j/\partial x_i - \tfrac{2}{3} \delta_{ij} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v})$ (eq. 7), with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$. The heat flux is $\mathbf{Q} = -\kappa \nabla T$, with the thermal conductivity $\kappa$ and the temperature $T$, and $\mathbf{J} = \nabla \times \mathbf{B}$ is the current density. The momentum equation subtracts $\Pi$ from the flux of momentum. The energy equation adds to the flux of energy $-\Pi \cdot \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{Q}$, the Poynting flux of resistivity, $\eta \mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B}$, and that of ambipolar diffusion, $(\eta_{\mathrm{AD}}/|\mathbf{B}|^2) \{\mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B})\} \times \mathbf{B}$. The induction equation becomes $\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t - \nabla \times [\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} - \eta \mathbf{J} - (\eta_{\mathrm{AD}}/|\mathbf{B}|^2) \mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B})] = 0$. The total energy density is $E = e + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$ for any EOS (eq. 8). The coefficients $\nu$, $\kappa$ and $\eta$ are constant by default, but may be made functions of position and of the variables. The ambipolar coefficient $\eta_{\mathrm{AD}}$ has no default, as it depends on the rates of ionisation, recombination and collisions in the plasma, and the user supplies a function for it (2.11).

#### 2.1.3 The finite-volume discretisation

Athena integrates the equations on a regular Cartesian grid in three dimensions ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §3). The domain of size $(L_x, L_y, L_z)$ is divided into $(N_x, N_y, N_z)$ cells, and the cell $(i, j, k)$ is centred at $(x_i, y_j, z_k)$. The paper describes uniform cells of size $\delta x = L_x/N_x$, $\delta y = L_y/N_y$ and $\delta z = L_z/N_z$, and notes that the methods extend easily to nonuniform grids. Time advances from $t_0$ to $t_f$ in steps $\delta t^n = t^{n+1} - t^n$ that may vary, written $\delta t$ below. Methods built on the integral form of the equations suit flows with shocks and discontinuities ([LeVeque](#leveque-2002) 2002). Integrating eq. 7 over a cell and over a step and applying the divergence theorem gives $\mathbf{U}^{n+1}_{i,j,k} = \mathbf{U}^n_{i,j,k} - (\delta t/\delta x)(\mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,k} - \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2,j,k}) - (\delta t/\delta y)(\mathbf{G}^{n+1/2}_{i,j+1/2,k} - \mathbf{G}^{n+1/2}_{i,j-1/2,k}) - (\delta t/\delta z)(\mathbf{H}^{n+1/2}_{i,j,k+1/2} - \mathbf{H}^{n+1/2}_{i,j,k-1/2})$ (§3.1, eq. 11). Here $\mathbf{U}^n_{i,j,k}$ is the average of $\mathbf{U}$ over the cell at $t^n$ (eq. 12), and each flux is the average of $\mathbf{F}$, $\mathbf{G}$ or $\mathbf{H}$ over a face and over the step (eqs. 13 to 15). Half-integer subscripts mark the faces between cells, so that $x_{i-1/2}$ lies between the cells centred at $x_{i-1}$ and $x_i$. The half-integer superscript marks an average over time, not a value at $t^{n+1/2}$. The update is exact, and a method lies wholly in how it approximates the averaged fluxes. Orders above the second are possible, and were explored for MHD by [Londrillo and Del Zanna](#londrillo-del-zanna-2000) (2000) and with WENO schemes ([Balsara and Shu](#balsara-shu-2000) 2000), but they improve the accuracy mainly in smooth flow, not at shocks and discontinuities, and are harder to combine with AMR. On a set of hydrodynamic tests in one dimension, [Greenough and Rider](#greenough-rider-2003) (2003) found a Godunov scheme of second order more accurate for its cost than a WENO scheme of fifth order. Athena therefore keeps to second order in space and time.

Athena++ discretises the equations of mass, momentum and energy (eqs. 6a to 6c) by finite volumes, storing the averages of the conserved variables at the volume centres of cells ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.1). In curvilinear coordinates the volume centre differs from the geometric centre, which matters for methods above second order ([Blondin and Lufkin](#blondin-lufkin-1993) 1993; 2.8). The induction equation (eq. 6d) is discretised by the upwind CT of [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, [2008](#gardiner-stone-2008)), with the components of the field as averages over the faces of cells (2.3). An EOS supplies the pressure and the temperature from the conserved variables. Any EOS that gives $P(\rho, e)$ and the square of the sound speed $a^2(\rho, P)$, analytically or by interpolation in a table, can be used, as [Coleman](#coleman-2020) (2020) describes and tests after [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019) verify (2.2).

#### 2.1.4 The variables in the code

The class `Hydro` holds the variables of hydrodynamics on a mesh block (`src/hydro/hydro.hpp`, `Hydro`). The conserved variables `u` and the primitive variables `w` are arrays of four dimensions, indexed as `u(n,k,j,i)` by the variable $n$ and the cell, over the whole block with its ghost cells (3.2). The indices of the variables are `IDN` for the density, `IM1` to `IM3` for the momentum and `IEN` for the total energy, and in the primitive variables `IVX` to `IVZ` for the velocity and `IPR` for the pressure, which share the values 1 to 4 (`src/athena.hpp`). Their number `NHYDRO` is set when the code is configured: 5 for an adiabatic or general EOS and 4 for an isothermal one, which has no energy (`configure.py`). The states of a one-dimensional Riemann problem carry the two transverse components of the field after the hydrodynamic variables, at the indices `IBY` and `IBZ`, equal to `NHYDRO` and `NHYDRO + 1`. `NWAVE` counts the variables of such a state: 5 or 4 in hydrodynamics and 7 or 6 in MHD, for an adiabatic or isothermal gas. Further registers serve the time integrators: `u1` and `w1` always, `u2` for the integrator `ssprk5_4` or for super time-stepping, and `u0` and `fl_div` for super time-stepping of the type RKL2 (2.6, 2.11). The fluxes through the faces are kept in `flux[3]`. Its array in the direction $x_1$ has one more entry along $i$, and those in $x_2$ and $x_3$ one more along $j$ or $k$, allocated only when the mesh extends in their directions. With mesh refinement, `coarse_cons_` and `coarse_prim_` hold variables on the coarser grid that refinement uses (3.4), and the fourth-order method keeps approximations at cell centres in `u_cc` and `w_cc` (2.7). The class also holds the boundary variable of hydrodynamics, its source terms and its diffusion (`HydroBoundaryVariable`, `HydroSourceTerms`, `HydroDiffusion`), and its constructor registers the conserved variables for refinement and communication (`src/hydro/hydro.cpp`, `Hydro::Hydro`). The magnetic field is held by the class `Field`, as a structure `FaceField` of three arrays on the faces, each with one more entry along its normal, and as values at cell centres in `bcc` (`src/athena.hpp`, `FaceField`; 2.3). The integrator is chosen at run time by the parameter `integrator` of the block `time`, with VL2 as default (2.6).

### 2.2 Equations of state

#### 2.2.1 Ideal and isothermal gases

Athena++ chooses its EOS when the code is configured: adiabatic, isothermal or general, each with its own files for hydrodynamics and MHD (`configure.py`). The class `EquationOfState` converts between conserved and primitive variables, computes sound speeds and applies floors (`src/eos/eos.hpp`, `EquationOfState`). For an adiabatic gas, the adiabatic index $\gamma$ is read from the parameter `gamma` of the block `hydro`, which must be given (`src/eos/adiabatic_hydro.cpp`, `EquationOfState::EquationOfState`). The conversion to primitive variables first raises the conserved density to the floor `dfloor` where it is lower, without changing momentum or energy, then divides the momentum by the density and computes $P = (\gamma - 1)(E - \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2)$ (`EquationOfState::ConservedToPrimitive`). Where this pressure falls below the floor `pfloor`, it is set to the floor and the total energy is raised to match, so that the floors add mass and energy. In MHD the magnetic energy $B^2/2$ is subtracted as well, from the field at cell centres, which the conversion first computes from the field on the faces (`src/eos/adiabatic_mhd.cpp`, `EquationOfState::ConservedToPrimitive`; 2.3). Both floors default to $\sqrt{1024\, F}$, with $F$ the smallest normal number in single precision, which gives about $3.5 \times 10^{-18}$ in code units, and so does the floor `sfloor` of the passive scalars. The sound speed is $a = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$. The fast magnetosonic speed along $x$ is computed as $c_f^2 = [\gamma P + B^2 + \sqrt{(B^2 - \gamma P)^2 + 4 \gamma P B_\perp^2}]/(2\rho)$, with $B_\perp$ the transverse field (`EquationOfState::FastMagnetosonicSpeed`). This equals the usual form, with $(\gamma P + B^2)^2 - 4 \gamma P B_x^2$ under the root, but needs no difference of large terms. Floors are also applied to the reconstructed states at the faces, and to the primitive and conserved variables together in the fourth-order method (`EquationOfState::ApplyPrimitiveFloors`, `EquationOfState::ApplyPrimitiveConservedFloors`). For an isothermal gas the sound speed `iso_sound_speed` must be given. There is then no energy, only the density floor applies, and $c_s^2 \rho$ takes the place of $\gamma P$ in the fast speed (`src/eos/isothermal_hydro.cpp`, `src/eos/isothermal_mhd.cpp`).

#### 2.2.2 The general EOS

The ideal gas is a common assumption in astrophysical fluid dynamics but often an invalid one, and a realistic EOS matters, for instance, in supernovae, kilonovae and ionisation instabilities of accretion discs, and in models of the transients that surveys find ([Coleman](#coleman-2020) 2020, §1). A finite-difference code such as ZEUS ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, [b](#stone-norman-1992b)) takes a general EOS easily, but a Godunov code must solve its Riemann problems with the same EOS. [Colella and Glaz](#colella-glaz-1985) (1985) made this practical, and codes such as FLASH ([Fryxell et al.](#fryxell-et-al-2000) 2000), CASTRO ([Almgren et al.](#almgren-et-al-2010) 2010) and Chimera ([Bruenn et al.](#bruenn-et-al-2018) 2018) followed. Coleman extends Athena++ to non-relativistic hydrodynamics and MHD with a general EOS, not to its relativistic modules. He claims for it the first public MHD code whose general EOS has been verified by a suite of tests with a non-trivial EOS: FLASH has published no such test against a known solution, and CASTRO, which ran similar tests ([Zingale and Katz](#zingale-katz-2015) 2015), has no magnetic field. The tests use an EOS of hydrogen with an ionisation transition and a Helmholtz EOS of electrons and positrons (abstract).

The Riemann problem is recalled for the Euler equations in one dimension (§2, eqs. 1 to 3), closed by an EOS $p(\rho, e)$ or $e(\rho, p)$ with $(\partial p/\partial \rho)_e > 0$ and $(\partial p/\partial e)_\rho > 0$ in the frame where the fluid is at rest (eqs. 4, 5). These conditions suffice for a unique solution, without being necessary ([Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) 2019). Two constant states separated at $x = 0$ evolve into a fan of four constant states that depends on $x/t$ alone, separated by three waves: rarefactions or shocks on the outside and a contact discontinuity in the middle (eqs. 6, 7; Fig. 1). [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019) solve the problem exactly to arbitrary precision for a general EOS, at a cost too high for simulations, which therefore use approximate solvers. They also show that the generalisation needs the exact adiabatic sound speed, $a^2 = (\partial p/\partial \rho)_s$ at constant specific entropy (eq. 8).

With diffusion neglected, the equations are those of ideal MHD with $E = e + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$ (§3, eqs. 9 to 12). All calculations of the paper use piecewise linear reconstruction of the primitive variables and the VL2 integrator, and reconstruction in characteristic variables does not support a general EOS. An EOS fits the framework if it satisfies eqs. 4 and 5, with well-behaved derivatives, and supplies three functions: $p(\rho, e)$, $e(\rho, p)$ and $a^2(\rho, p)$ (§3.1, eqs. 13 to 15). The first converts conserved variables to primitive ones, after the kinetic and magnetic energies are subtracted from $E$, and the second converts back. The sound speed serves the CFL condition and the Riemann solvers, for their characteristic speeds and fluxes. Even a piecewise EOS will do. The generalisation replaces $p = (\gamma - 1) e$, $e = p/(\gamma - 1)$ and $a^2 = \gamma p/\rho$ by these functions (eqs. 16 to 18), and Coleman replaced every occurrence of $\gamma$ in Athena++ in this way. That the replacements keep all the characteristic speeds of the Riemann problem is not obvious, but follows from Appendix B of [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019). The one further change concerns the estimate of the wave speeds in the Riemann solvers (2.5).

#### 2.2.3 Tables and the EOS of hydrogen

The three functions may be analytic or interpolated in tables (§3.2). The table stores $\log(p/e)$ as a function of $\log \rho$ and $\log e + \eta \log \rho$, and $\log(e/p)$ and $\log \Gamma_1$ as functions of $\log \rho$ and $\log p + \eta \log \rho$, with $\eta$ a constant chosen by the user and $\Gamma_1 = (\partial \ln p/\partial \ln \rho)_s = \rho a^2/p$ the generalised adiabatic index (eqs. 19 to 22). All three quantities are dimensionless, and all depend on $\log \rho$ and on $\log \epsilon$, with $\epsilon$ either $e \rho^\eta$ or $p \rho^\eta$. A regular grid in $\log \rho$ and $\log \epsilon$ discretises them (eqs. 23, 24), with the same number of points for $e$ and $p$, which may differ by a constant factor $c_0$, one by default (eq. 25). Bilinear interpolation keeps monotonicity between the points, and off the table the values are extrapolated linearly, which floors on $p$, $e$ and $\rho$ and a large enough table should prevent. Another EOS beyond the table would be possible but is not implemented. For an ideal gas the three quantities are constants, $p/e = \gamma - 1$, $e/p = 1/(\gamma - 1)$ and $\Gamma_1 = \gamma$ (eqs. 26 to 28), so that a table of two points in each dimension reproduces the ideal gas to machine precision, as was verified.

In the code a general EOS is configured with `--eos=general/` and the name of its implementation, which sets `GENERAL_EOS` and, for a table, `EOS_TABLE_ENABLED` (`configure.py`). Each implementation supplies the three functions as `PresFromRhoEg`, `EgasFromRhoP` and `AsqFromRhoP` (`src/eos/general/`). `src/eos/general/ideal.cpp` implements the ideal gas within the framework, mainly for debugging, and `src/eos/general/noop.cpp` makes the three functions stop the code when no general EOS is configured. `src/eos/general/eos_table.cpp` interpolates in the table of the mesh, held by the class `EosTable` and read once by the mesh (`src/utils/eos_table_class.cpp`, `EosTable`; `src/mesh/mesh.cpp`, `Mesh::Mesh`). The table holds, in this order, $p/e$, $e/p$ and $\rho a^2/p$, each as a function of $\log \rho$ and of the logarithm of the energy or pressure plus `dens_pow` times $\log \rho$. With `dens_pow` = −1 by default, the second coordinate is the specific energy or pressure, the case $\eta = -1$ of eqs. 19 to 21. Ratios stored for each quantity scale the second coordinate, as $c_0$ does in eq. 25, and the parameters `eos_rho_unit` and `eos_egas_unit` convert between code units and those of the table. The table is read from a binary, HDF5 or text file named by `eos_file_name`, whose type follows from its extension unless `eos_file_type` sets it (`EosTable::EosTable`). The interpolation is bilinear, with the indices clamped to the edges of the table, so that values off the table are extrapolated linearly (`src/utils/interp_table.cpp`, `InterpTable2D::interpolate`). For a general EOS the conversion to primitive variables subtracts the kinetic energy, and in MHD the magnetic energy, from $E$, raises the internal energy to the floor `efloor` where it is lower, adjusting the total energy, and calls `PresFromRhoEg` (`src/eos/general/general_hydro.cpp`, `src/eos/general/general_mhd.cpp`, `EquationOfState::ConservedToPrimitive`). The energy floor defaults to `pfloor` divided by $\gamma - 1$, with $\gamma = 2$ unless it is given. The sound speed and the fast speed use `AsqFromRhoP`, and `GetGamma` stops the code, as $\gamma$ has no meaning for a general EOS. The code refuses a general EOS with reconstruction in characteristic variables, and warns that combined with reconstruction above second order it has documented bugs in some cases (`src/reconstruct/reconstruction.cpp`, `Reconstruction::Reconstruction`).

The simple EOS of hydrogen of the tests contains electrons, neutral hydrogen and protons in local thermal equilibrium, with the one reaction $\mathrm{H}^0 \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}^+ + e^-$ (App. B, eq. B18). The partition functions per volume factor into bound states, nuclear spin, the spin of electrons, translation and excitation (eq. B19). The bound states are set to one, a small error for neutral hydrogen in an EOS meant as a proof of concept (eq. B20). The translational factor is $(2 \pi m_i k T/h^2)^{3/2} = n_q (m_i/m_e)^{3/2} (T/T_{\mathrm{ion}})^{3/2}$, with $n_q = (2 \pi m_e k T_{\mathrm{ion}}/h^2)^{3/2} = 1.514892 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and the ionisation temperature of hydrogen $T_{\mathrm{ion}} = \alpha^2 m_e c^2/2k = 157\,888$ K (eqs. B21 to B23). The spin factors of electrons and neutral hydrogen and the nuclear spin are 2, and protons carry the excitation factor $\exp(-T_{\mathrm{ion}}/T)$ (eqs. B24 to B27). The Saha equation, with conservation of nuclei and charge neutrality (eqs. B28 to B30), $n_{\mathrm{nuc}} = \rho/m_p$ and the mass of the electrons neglected, gives the ionisation fraction $x = 2/[1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 \tilde\rho\, e^{1/\tilde T} \tilde T^{-3/2}}]$, with $\tilde\rho = \rho/(m_p n_q)$ and $\tilde T = T/T_{\mathrm{ion}}$ (eqs. B31, B32). The pressure is $p = n_q k T_{\mathrm{ion}} \tilde p$ with $\tilde p = \tilde\rho \tilde T (1 + x)$, and the specific internal energy $\epsilon = (k T_{\mathrm{ion}}/m_p)(x + \tfrac{3}{2} \tilde p/\tilde\rho)$ (eqs. B33, B34). Closed forms give $\Gamma_1$ and $\partial x/\partial \tilde T$, and with them $a^2 = \Gamma_1 p/\rho$ (eqs. B35 to B38). The code works in these dimensionless units, computing $x$, $p$, $e = \rho \epsilon$ and $a^2$ from $\tilde\rho$ and $\tilde T$, and inverts $p$ or $e$ for $\tilde T$ by the Brent–Dekker method to the precision `InversionPrecision`, $10^{-12}$ by default (`src/eos/general/hydrogen.cpp`). It writes $\Gamma_1$ in another closed form. **[GK]** The two forms of $\Gamma_1$, eq. B36 and that of the code, agree to eight digits across the transition and match the derivative $\partial \ln p/\partial \ln \rho$ at constant entropy, computed numerically from eqs. B33 and B34 with $\mathrm{d}\epsilon = (p/\rho^2)\, \mathrm{d}\rho$. Both tend to 5/3 for a fully neutral or fully ionised gas. The values of $n_q$ and $T_{\mathrm{ion}}$ in eqs. B22 and B23 follow from the physical constants, and eqs. B31 and B37 follow from eqs. B28 to B30.

### 2.3 Constrained transport

#### 2.3.1 Keeping the field free of divergence

Godunov methods for MHD need two extensions of the hydrodynamic algorithm: a Riemann solver for MHD, and a way to keep the evolved field free of divergence, the harder of the two ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) 2005, §1). [Brackbill and Barnes](#brackbill-barnes-1980) (1980) first noted that where $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} \ne 0$ the Lorentz force gains a component along $\mathbf{B}$, which can give wrong dynamics, and [Tóth](#toth-2000) (2000) showed that MHD shocks can then have wrong jump conditions. Three approaches were in use. A Hodge projection cleans the field of its divergence after each step ([Balsara and Kim](#balsara-kim-2003) 2003; [Crockett et al.](#crockett-et-al-2005) 2005; [Tóth](#toth-2000) 2000). An evolution equation for the divergence, added to the system, keeps its errors from accumulating in one place, as in the eight-wave scheme ([Powell et al.](#powell-et-al-1999) 1999) and the GLM-MHD scheme ([Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2002) 2002). Difference equations for the field can instead conserve the magnetic flux exactly, the approach that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) called constrained transport (CT). CT had served other MHD algorithms well ([Clarke](#clarke-1996) 1996; [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b), and the authors adopt it. Its most compact form uses area averages of the field on the faces of cells rather than volume averages at their centres, since a CT built on fields at cell centres ([Tóth](#toth-2000) 2000) averages over a wider stencil than the fluxes use. The staggered grid that CT needs is often seen as a drawback, but it reflects that the quantity MHD conserves is the magnetic flux, an area average. CT conserves that flux, and so keeps the divergence zero in an integral sense, on the scale of single cells.

The paper shows that finite volumes for the other variables and CT for the field can be made consistent if their fluxes obey simple relations (2.3.3; see also [Londrillo and Del Zanna](#londrillo-del-zanna-2004) 2004). These relations lead to algorithms for the electric fields at the corners of cells that reduce exactly to the solver in one dimension for plane-parallel flows aligned with the grid, unlike earlier Godunov schemes with CT ([Balsara and Spicer](#balsara-spicer-1999) 1999; [Dai and Woodward](#dai-woodward-1998) 1998; [Ryu et al.](#ryu-et-al-1998) 1998; 2.3.5). Combined with PPM ([Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) 1984) and the Roe solver for MHD ([Cargo and Gallice](#cargo-gallice-1997) 1997), the reconstruction must include multidimensional terms of the induction equation. Methods split by direction cannot keep the constraint between their updates in one dimension, so the unsplit CTU integrator of [Colella](#colella-1990) (1990) is adopted, with extensions that CT requires (2.6). The resulting algorithm takes a single step, is of second order and fully conservative, which suits refined meshes, and reduces to the algorithm in one dimension for planar flows aligned with the grid. The paper describes it in two dimensions.

#### 2.3.2 Finite areas and the update of the field

[Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, §2) write the equations of ideal MHD with the total pressure $P^* = P + B^2/2$ and the total energy density $E = \epsilon + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$, for an ideal gas with $\gamma = 5/3$ unless stated otherwise (eqs. 1 to 5), with $\epsilon$ the internal energy density. None of their main results depends on the EOS. The field must stay free of divergence, or else the equations for the conserved variables cease to be consistent with those for the primitive variables. On a Cartesian grid in two dimensions, the finite-volume update gives the volume-averaged field fluxes only in its transverse components (§2.1, eqs. 6 to 12). Updates split by direction therefore do not keep the divergence zero between their sweeps, which suggests that such algorithms do not suit MHD. CT instead integrates the induction equation $\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t + \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0$, with $\mathbf{E} = -\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}$, over the faces of cells and applies Stokes' theorem (§2.2, eq. 13). In two dimensions this gives $B^{n+1}_{x,i-1/2,j} = B^n_{x,i-1/2,j} - (\delta t/\delta y)(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j+1/2} - \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j-1/2})$ and $B^{n+1}_{y,i,j-1/2} = B^n_{y,i,j-1/2} + (\delta t/\delta x)(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i+1/2,j-1/2} - \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j-1/2})$ (eqs. 14, 15). The field components are averages over the faces (eqs. 16, 17), and the electric field is an average over the step at a corner of the cell (eq. 18). As with finite volumes, the update is exact, and a method lies in how it approximates $\mathcal{E}$. The magnetic flux is conserved exactly, so that a cell without net magnetic charge at $t^n$ has none at $t^{n+1}$. Averaging over faces, the smallest domains that turn the differential equation into an integral one, keeps as much pointwise information as possible and so minimises the dissipation of averaging.

In three dimensions the update of each face adds the electric fields along the other edges of that face ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §3.2, eqs. 16 to 18), for example $B^{n+1}_{x,i-1/2,j,k} = B^n_{x,i-1/2,j,k} - (\delta t/\delta y)(\mathcal{E}_{z,i-1/2,j+1/2,k} - \mathcal{E}_{z,i-1/2,j-1/2,k}) + (\delta t/\delta z)(\mathcal{E}_{y,i-1/2,j,k+1/2} - \mathcal{E}_{y,i-1/2,j,k-1/2})$. Each component of $\mathcal{E}$ is averaged along its edge and over the step (eqs. 22 to 24). The divergence formed from the face fields at $t^{n+1}$ (eq. 25) then stays zero if it was zero at $t^n$ ([Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) 1988), and the magnetic flux through each cell is conserved. The cost is a staggered grid, which complicates the algorithm and its interface to AMR. CT on faces keeps only this one of the many possible discretisations of the divergence, while [Tóth](#toth-2000) (2000) extended CT to keep several discretisations built on fields at cell centres. Which one matters most for a given integrator is hard to judge. The authors argue that the form on faces fits finite volumes best, since it conserves the magnetic flux, and with it the total of magnetic monopoles, within each cell. A field loop advected across the grid tests whether the discretisation kept is consistent with the update of the induction equation, as an inconsistent one lets net magnetic flux grow (2.16). [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) (2008, §2) use the same CT in three dimensions.

The momentum and energy fluxes need the field at cell centres, which Athena takes as the average of the two face values along each direction, $B_{x,i,j,k} = \tfrac{1}{2}(B_{x,i-1/2,j,k} + B_{x,i+1/2,j,k})$ and likewise for $B_y$ and $B_z$, accurate to second order ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, eqs. 26 to 28; [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) 2005, eqs. 19, 20; [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) 2008, eq. 6). The face fields are evolved, and the fields at cell centres are computed from them at the end of each step.

#### 2.3.3 Consistency of finite volumes and CT

The field and its fluxes must be coupled consistently in the two representations ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) 2005, §2.3). The condition is that the cell-centred field remain the average of the face fields at $t^{n+1}$ (eqs. 19, 20). Subtracting the finite-volume update of the cell-centred field from the CT update of the face fields then fixes the fluxes of the former in terms of the corner electric fields (eqs. 21 to 24). The $y$-flux of $B_x$ through a face must be the average of $\mathcal{E}_z$ at the two ends of that face, $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_x} \cdot \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{y,i,j\pm1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j\pm1/2} + \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i+1/2,j\pm1/2})$, and the $x$-flux of $B_y$ minus the corresponding average, $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_y} \cdot \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{x,i\pm1/2,j} = -\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i\pm1/2,j-1/2} + \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i\pm1/2,j+1/2})$, where $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_x}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_y}$ select the components of the flux. This agrees with the finite-volume fluxes being averages in space of the electric fields that CT uses. In effect, the Godunov fluxes of the transverse components of the volume-averaged field are replaced by averages of the corner values of $\mathcal{E}_z$, whatever algorithm computes those. The relations thus act as a correction that makes the fluxes of the Riemann solver consistent with CT, and the algorithm for the corner fields then decides how accurate and stable the whole scheme is (2.3.5).

#### 2.3.4 The field in the code

In Athena++ the class `Field` holds the face-centred field `b`, a `FaceField` with one more entry along each normal (`src/field/field.hpp`, `Field`; `src/field/field.cpp`, `Field::Field`). Further registers are `b1` and, for the integrator `ssprk5_4` or super time-stepping, `b2`, with `b0` and `ct_update` for super time-stepping of the type RKL2. The class also holds the cell-centred field `bcc`, the electric fields on the edges `e`, an `EdgeField`, and the weights `wght` with which the electric fields are carried to the corners (2.3.5). It holds as well the electric fields that the Riemann solvers return on the faces (`e2_x1f`, `e3_x1f` and so on), coarse copies for mesh refinement, its boundary variable and its diffusion (`FaceCenteredBoundaryVariable`, `FieldDiffusion`). The cell-centred field is the linear interpolation of the two face values to the volume centre of the cell (`Field::CalculateCellCenteredField`). With uniform spacing this is the average of eqs. 26 to 28, and otherwise $B_{1,i} = [(x_{1,i+1/2} - x^v_{1,i}) B_{1,i-1/2} + (x^v_{1,i} - x_{1,i-1/2}) B_{1,i+1/2}]/\Delta x_{1,i}$, with $x^v_{1,i}$ the volume centre, and likewise in the other directions. The EOS calls this function before it converts the conserved variables (2.2.1). The update of CT adds the curl of the edge electric fields in its integral form, with the areas of the faces and the lengths of the edges that the coordinates supply (`src/field/ct.cpp`, `Field::CT`). $B_1$ changes by $-(w/A_1)(L_{3,j+1/2} \mathcal{E}_{3,j+1/2} - L_{3,j-1/2} \mathcal{E}_{3,j-1/2}) + (w/A_1)(L_{2,k+1/2} \mathcal{E}_{2,k+1/2} - L_{2,k-1/2} \mathcal{E}_{2,k-1/2})$, with $A_1$ the area of its face, $L_2$ and $L_3$ the lengths of the edges and $w$ the weight that the integrator passes, and likewise $B_2$ and $B_3$. On a Cartesian grid this is eqs. 16 to 18 of [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008). $B_1$ changes only in two or three dimensions, while $B_2$ and $B_3$ change in one dimension as well, through the derivatives of $\mathcal{E}_3$ and $\mathcal{E}_2$ along $x_1$. The faces of $B_2$ that lie on a polar boundary are not updated (2.8).

## 3. Software architecture

Nine subjects group the software architecture. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020, §1) explain three decisions that shape the framework of Athena++. First, the mesh is abstract and independent of the physics. The discretisations of the variables, such as volume averages at cell centres, area averages on faces and point values at cell centres or vertices, are built and stored on it, and AMR, boundary conditions and parallelisation by decomposing the domain are implemented for these variables without reference to any physics. New coordinates and new physics therefore work with AMR in any geometry at once, and mesh and physics can be developed apart, as in a version of the framework on Kokkos ([Edwards, Trott and Sunderland](#edwards-et-al-2014) 2014) for heterogeneous machines with GPUs. Second, the mesh is refined by blocks ([Stout et al.](#stout-et-al-1997) 1997) rather than by overlapping patches after [Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) (1984), as in Athena, PLUTO and Enzo. With patches, a refined region is covered by several levels, on which quantities computed from the conserved variables, the temperature for instance, may differ, and source terms that depend on them, such as cooling or reaction networks, then drive the levels apart. The overlap between the domains of the processes of the Message Passing Interface (MPI) on different levels also grows complex. In Athena++ each point of the domain belongs to exactly one level, and the levels communicate only through their boundaries, which simplifies the code and improves its performance and scaling. FLASH ([Fryxell et al.](#fryxell-et-al-2000) 2000) also refines by blocks, with the library PARAMESH ([MacNeice et al.](#macneice-et-al-2000) 2000), but Athena++ uses a framework of its own, to support variables on faces, as its MHD requires, to depend on fewer libraries and to gain performance at the cost of generality. Third, the steps of an algorithm, including the sends and receives of MPI, are not executed in a fixed order but assembled into lists of tasks, each of which runs once the tasks it depends on are complete. Computation can then overlap communication, which improves scaling on very many processors, and a calculation chooses its physics by the composition of its task lists. Separate regions can even run separate lists, for example a particle-in-cell code for the collisionless corona above an accretion disc in MHD ([Miller and Stone](#miller-stone-2000) 2000). Libraries such as Legion and Charm++ offer such dynamic execution among much else, but Athena++ implements only the scheduling it needs, in a few thousand lines of its own.

### 3.2 Arrays, the mesh and its blocks

All data of the grid lie in arrays of the template class `AthenaArray`, of one to six dimensions (`src/athena_arrays.hpp`, `AthenaArray`). The data occupy one contiguous block of memory, and the operator of function call addresses them with the last index fastest, so that `A(n,k,j,i)` is the element `i + N1*(j + N2*(k + N3*n))` of an array of size $N_4 \times N_3 \times N_2 \times N_1$. Loops with `i` innermost thus run through memory in order. An array records whether it is empty, allocated, or a shallow slice of another array. Allocated memory is set to zero. Copying an array copies its data, assigning one requires the target to be allocated with the same size already, and moving one hands over its data. Arrays may be allocated after their construction (`NewAthenaArray`) and released (`DeleteAthenaArray`). `SwapAthenaArray` exchanges the data of two arrays of equal size without copying them, and `InitWithShallowSlice` and `ShallowSlice3DToPencil` make views into part of another array, such as a range of its variables or one line of cells along $x_1$, again without copying. The type `Real` of the data is `double` by default and `float` when the code is configured for single precision, and `SIMD_WIDTH` sets the width of the vector lanes from the instruction set that the compiler targets (`src/athena.hpp`). The same header declares the structures for the size of a region and the logical location of a block in the tree of refinement, the containers `FaceField` and `EdgeField` for fields on faces and edges, the enumerations of indices and directions, and the types of the functions that users may enrol (3.6).
