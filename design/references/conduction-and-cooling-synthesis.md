# Synthesis of conduction and cooling

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on thermal conduction and radiative cooling in astrophysical gas, kept in `reference-works/Conduction and Cooling/Docs`. The collection is gathered around [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, [arXiv:1608.06290](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.06290)), a study of how clouds of a galactic fountain make the hot corona of their galaxy cool and condense, with thermal conduction included (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §3, eq. 10), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI or ADS bibcode, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

Marinacci gives the Kavli Institute for Astrophysics and Space Research of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The authors thank the anonymous referee for suggestions and comments (acknowledgements). Armillotta and Fraternali thank Fabrizio Brighenti for suggestions on how to implement thermal conduction in Athena, the code of their simulations, and Armillotta thanks Enrico Di Teodoro and Gabriele Pezzulli for their help. An award of CINECA under the ISCRA initiative provided computing resources and support, and the Kapteyn Astronomical Institute let the authors test the new algorithms of Athena on its Gemini clusters. **[GK]** The local copy is the only version on arXiv, posted as accepted for publication in MNRAS. The paper appeared there in volume 462, on pages 4157–4170, online on 3 August 2016 and in print on 11 November 2016.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

In star-forming galaxies, stellar feedback can both suppress and stimulate the accretion of gas from the circumgalactic medium ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, abstract). It stimulates accretion through gas of the disc that it ejects into the halo as fountain clouds, which interact with the hot corona around the galaxy. Where the cold, metal-rich gas of the disc, at $T \lesssim 10^4$ K, mixes with the hot gas of the corona, at $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K, the cooling time of part of the corona can fall steeply, so that this part condenses and is accreted. Accretion driven by the fountain had been studied only under the conditions of the Milky Way (§1). The paper extends the two-dimensional simulations of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)), which followed a cold cloud through the corona of the Milky Way, to haloes whose coronae have different temperatures and so span a wide range of virial masses. Its simulations, at a resolution of parsecs, add thermal conduction, which the earlier ones lacked. Conduction may be a key process for the efficiency of the condensation, as it may slow the condensation down or, under some conditions, inhibit it ([Begelman and McKee](#begelman-mckee-1990) 1990; [Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a). 2.8.1 sets out the astrophysical background of the paper, and 2.8.2 the set-up of its simulations.

The simulations show that the condensation of coronal gas depends strongly on the galactic environment ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, abstract). It grows less efficient as the virial temperature and mass of the halo increase, and is fully ineffective for virial masses above $10^{13}\,M_\odot$. The corona thus cools quickly in haloes of low to intermediate virial mass, $M_\mathrm{vir} \lesssim 3 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$, while the ability to cool the corona declines from late-type to early-type disc galaxies. Accretion may then switch off, and star formation be quenched, in massive systems. These virial masses rest on a definition of the virial radius that 2.8.2 qualifies.

### 1.3 The collection

The paper of 2016 stands at the centre of the collection. Its local copy has 14 pages, with 13 numbered equations and eight more in App. A, seven figures and one more in App. A, two tables and 83 references. §2 treats thermal conduction and its handling in the code, and §3 introduces the simulations and the choice of their parameters ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). §4 presents their results, chiefly the efficiency of coronal cooling and the effect of conduction on it, §5 discusses their limitations and their implications for the evolution of galaxies, and §6 summarises. App. A describes the algorithm of conduction and a test of it. The further papers are of three kinds: works on conduction and cooling that the paper builds on, newer studies that build on the paper, and methods that it leaves out, chiefly anisotropic conduction and forms of radiative cooling beyond its tables. ## 2. Numerical methods

Eight subjects group the methods. Thermal conduction takes 2.1 to 2.4: isotropic and suppressed by magnetic fields, saturated, anisotropic, and its numerical integration. Radiative cooling takes 2.5 to 2.7: in collisional ionisation equilibrium, out of equilibrium and under radiation, and its integration in time. The fountain clouds and the condensation of coronae, where conduction and cooling act together, take 2.8. `plan.md` lists the subjects, with the steps that write them.

### 2.1 Isotropic conduction and its suppression by magnetic fields

#### 2.1.1 The Spitzer conductivity

Thermal conduction transfers energy down a gradient of temperature: at the interface between two phases of gas, hot electrons carry heat into the colder medium, and the gradient is smoothed ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.1). In a fully ionised plasma, the classical theory of [Spitzer](#spitzer-1962) (1962) gives the heat flux $\mathbf{q}_\mathrm{class} = -\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T$ (eq. 1), with the conductivity $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp} = 1.84 \times 10^{-5}\,T^{5/2}/\ln\Psi$ erg s$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$ cm$^{-1}$ (eq. 2). Its Coulomb logarithm is $\ln\Psi = 29.7 + \ln[(T_\mathrm{e}/10^6\,\mathrm{K})/(n_\mathrm{e}/\mathrm{cm}^{-3})^{1/2}]$, with $n_\mathrm{e}$ the density and $T_\mathrm{e}$ the temperature of the electrons (eq. 3). In many astrophysical settings the classical theory does not apply directly, and conduction is much less efficient than the Spitzer value.

#### 2.1.2 Suppression by tangled magnetic fields

In a magnetic field, the conducting electrons move along the field lines rather than isotropically, so that conduction across the field is strongly reduced ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.1). The effect of a tangled field is usually modelled by multiplying the Spitzer flux by a dimensionless factor $f \lesssim 1$, so that $\mathbf{q} = -f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T$ (eq. 4). For a tangled field, [Rechester and Rosenbluth](#rechester-rosenbluth-1978) (1978) and [Chandran and Cowley](#chandran-cowley-1998) (1998) estimated a coefficient 100 to 1000 times below the Spitzer value. [Narayan and Medvedev](#narayan-medvedev-2001) (2001) found that if the turbulence spans a wide range of length scales, as strong or intermediate MHD turbulence may, the efficiency depends on the ratio of the smallest scale of the turbulence to the scale of the structure. The factor $f$ then grows as the smallest scale shrinks, and converges to a maximum of about 0.2. **[GK]** Narayan and Medvedev (2001, [arXiv:astro-ph/0110567](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0110567), §2) derive the reduction by 100 to 1000 for a tangled field with a single coherence length, after Rechester and Rosenbluth (1978) and Chandran and Cowley (1998), and find a conductivity of about $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}/5$ once the turbulence spans at least two decades in scale.

The simulations contain no magnetic field, and $f$ stands in for its effect on conduction ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2.1). The maximum of 0.2 of [Narayan and Medvedev](#narayan-medvedev-2001) (2001) holds for a single phase of plasma, whereas the simulations concern the interface between two fluids, cloud and corona, with different magnetic fields. There, conduction can be reduced further if magnetic reconnection, which breaks and reconnects field lines of the two fluids, is inefficient ([Biskamp](#biskamp-2000) 2000; [Priest and Forbes](#priest-forbes-2000) 2000). Efficient reconnection lets the conducting electrons carry heat from the hot medium to the cold one, while inefficient reconnection keeps them in their own fluid and suppresses conduction strongly. The role of reconnection in a chaotic field is hard to trace and lies beyond the paper, which fixes $f = 0.1$ as a compromise between the upper limit of Narayan and Medvedev and a possible reduction by inefficient reconnection. Sim. 9 tests $f = 0.2$ (2.8.2).

### 2.2 Saturated conduction

#### 2.2.1 The saturated heat flux and the transition to it

The Spitzer formula also fails where the scale length of the temperature falls below the mean free path of the conducting electrons, since the classical description assumes a short mean free path ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.1). The heat flux is then replaced by a flux-limited form, the saturated heat flux $|\mathbf{q}_\mathrm{sat}| = 5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3$ of [Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977, [1977ApJ...211..135C](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJ...211..135C/abstract)), with $\rho$ the density and $c$ the sound speed (eq. 5). The efficiency $\Phi_\mathrm{s} \lesssim 1$ absorbs the uncertainties of the flux-limited treatment and the suppression of the flux by magnetic fields. [Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) (1993, [1993ApJ...404..625D](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...404..625D/abstract)) introduced a form that passes smoothly between the two regimes, $\mathbf{q}_\mathrm{eff} = -\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T/(1 + \sigma)$ (eq. 6). Here $\sigma = \kappa_\mathrm{Sp}|\nabla T|/(5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3)$ is the ratio of the classical to the saturated flux (eq. 7). The flux is thus strongly reduced for $\sigma \gtrsim 1$, and it never exceeds the saturated value.

For the simulations, the module of Athena for isotropic conduction computes the flux $\mathbf{q} = -f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T/(1 + \sigma)$, which combines eqs. 4 and 6 ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2, eq. 8). Magnetic fields may also reduce the saturated flux, through an efficiency $\Phi_\mathrm{s} \sim \sqrt{f}$ (§2.2.1; [Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977). If $\theta$ is the angle between the local field and the local gradient of temperature, the classical flux is reduced by $\cos^2\theta$: one factor $\cos\theta$ projects the gradient onto the field, and the other projects the resulting flux along the field onto the direction of the gradient. The saturated flux does not depend on the gradient, and so is reduced by $\cos\theta$ alone. The simulations take $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{f}$, neglecting any other reduction of the saturated flux. **[GK]** With $\sigma$ formed from $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$, as in eq. 7 and in the code (App. A, eq. A7), eq. 8 limits the flux for $\sigma \gg 1$ to $5f\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3$. The saturated flux is then reduced by $f^{3/2}$, about 0.03 for $f = 0.1$, rather than by $\sqrt{f}$, about 0.3, as the argument intends. A reduction by $\sqrt{f}$ needs $\sigma$ formed from $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$, as the ratio of the reduced classical flux to the saturated one.

### 2.5 Radiative cooling in collisional ionisation equilibrium

#### 2.5.1 Cooling in the energy equation

Radiative cooling and conduction enter the equation of the energy density $e$, $\partial e/\partial t + \nabla\cdot[(e + P)\mathbf{v}] = -\rho^2\Lambda - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}$ ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2, eq. 9). Here $\mathbf{v}$ is the velocity, $\rho$ the density and $P = (\gamma - 1)U$ the pressure, with $U$ the internal energy density and $\gamma = 5/3$. The cooling rate $\Lambda$ depends on temperature and metallicity, and the simulations take it from the tables of [Sutherland and Dopita](#sutherland-dopita-1993) (1993, [1993ApJS...88..253S](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJS...88..253S/abstract)) for collisional ionisation equilibrium. **[GK]** With $\Lambda$ in erg cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, as Sutherland and Dopita tabulate it and as §4.2 of the paper quotes it, the loss term is a power per unit volume only if $\rho$ there stands for number densities. The paper does not say which number densities multiply $\Lambda$.

### 2.7 The time integration of cooling

#### 2.7.1 Operator splitting and the limit on the time step

In Athena, as the paper describes it, the cooling term is added to the energy equation at first order, by operator splitting and with an explicit update ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2). At high resolution, the cooling time in the simulations mostly exceeds the time scale of the hydrodynamic processes. To avoid instabilities where cooling is very effective, the hydrodynamic time step is nevertheless limited to 10 per cent of the cooling time. 3.1 compares this update with the public version of the code.

### 2.8 Fountain clouds and coronal condensation

#### 2.8.1 Galactic coronae and fountain clouds

[Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §1) start from the prediction of current cosmological models that star-forming galaxies like the Milky Way are embedded in atmospheres of hot gas at the virial temperature, $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K, which reach out to hundreds of kiloparsecs from the centre of the galaxy ([Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) 2006). On the grounds of big-bang nucleosynthesis and of the cosmic microwave background, these cosmological coronae are widely held to contain a significant part of the warm-hot baryons that are missing from the census of the Universe ([Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) 2006; [Komatsu et al.](#komatsu-et-al-2009) 2009; [Shull, Smith and Danforth](#shull-et-al-2012) 2012). Their surface brightness in X-rays is thought too faint for the current instruments ([Bregman](#bregman-2007) 2007), yet hot haloes have been found around some massive spiral galaxies. Around the giant spirals NGC 1961, UGC 12591 and NGC 266, X-ray emission reaches more than 50 kpc from the centre ([Dai et al.](#dai-et-al-2012) 2012; [Bogdán et al.](#bogdan-et-al-2013a) 2013a, [b](#bogdan-et-al-2013b); [Anderson, Churazov and Bregman](#anderson-et-al-2016) 2016). These coronae have masses comparable with the baryonic masses of their discs, $\lesssim 10^{11}\,M_\odot$, and account for 10 to 50 per cent of the baryons missing from these galaxies. **[GK]** The paper cites only the first of the two papers of [Bogdán et al.](#bogdan-et-al-2013a) (2013a, [arXiv:1212.0541](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.0541)), which treats NGC 1961 and NGC 6753; the corona of NGC 266 is the subject of the second ([2013b](#bogdan-et-al-2013b)).

For the Milky Way, [Spitzer](#spitzer-1956) (1956) first proposed a hot corona, as a medium whose pressure confines the high-velocity clouds (HVCs; [Wakker and van Woerden](#wakker-van-woerden-1997) 1997). Most of the evidence for it is indirect. It comes from absorption spectroscopy in the ultraviolet ([Sembach et al.](#sembach-et-al-2003) 2003), the head-tail structure of several HVCs ([Putman, Saul and Mets](#putman-et-al-2011) 2011), the dispersion measures of pulsars in the Large Magellanic Cloud ([Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) 2010) and the stripping of dwarf galaxies of the Local Group by ram pressure ([Grcevich and Putman](#grcevich-putman-2009) 2009; [Gatto et al.](#gatto-et-al-2013) 2013). More recently, hot gas around the Galactic disc was detected through the emission lines of O VII and O VIII in the soft X-ray background of the Milky Way ([Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015). **[GK]** The paper names rotation measures of pulsars in the Magellanic Stream, but [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) (2010, [arXiv:1003.3273](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3273)) constrain the corona with the dispersion measures of pulsars in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Spitzer's proposal, which explains interstellar clouds far from the Galactic plane by their balance of pressure with a rarefied hot gas, is the article cited here; the paper's list of references names instead his book Physics of Fully Ionized Gases of the same year.

Coronae thus seem a significant reservoir of gas, which star-forming galaxies could accrete to sustain star formation at the observed rates ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). How galaxies like the Milky Way keep accreting gas to feed their star formation is a long-standing problem. Their rate of star formation has declined only mildly over their lifetime of about 10 Gyr ([Aumer and Binney](#aumer-binney-2009) 2009; [Fraternali and Tomassetti](#fraternali-tomassetti-2012) 2012). Their content of gas appears to have stayed roughly constant over the Hubble time ([Bauermeister, Blitz and Ma](#bauermeister-et-al-2010) 2010; [Zafar et al.](#zafar-et-al-2013) 2013). The gas of the thin disc typically sustains star formation for a few gigayears only, so that spiral galaxies need, at every epoch, a supply of external gas of about $1\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ to make up for the gas turned into stars ([Sancisi et al.](#sancisi-et-al-2008) 2008). This gas is thought to be poor in metals, to satisfy models of the chemical evolution of spiral galaxies ([Pagel](#pagel-2009) 2009; [Matteucci](#matteucci-2012) 2012) and to explain the observed gradients of metallicity ([Cavichia et al.](#cavichia-et-al-2014) 2014; [Pezzulli and Fraternali](#pezzulli-fraternali-2016) 2016).

For decades, HVCs were held to be the main source of cold gas poor in metals for the Milky Way. The most recent estimates, however, put their accretion at only about $0.08\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ ([Putman, Peek and Joung](#putman-et-al-2012) 2012), more than an order of magnitude below the Galactic rate of star formation ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). In nearby galaxies, too, the accretion of cold extragalactic gas seems to account for only about 10 per cent of the rate of star formation ([Sancisi et al.](#sancisi-et-al-2008) 2008; [Di Teodoro and Fraternali](#di-teodoro-fraternali-2014) 2014). Accretion from the coronae therefore seems the only viable possibility, but the coronal gas is hot and rarefied, and it must cool before it can form stars ([Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015).

How the gas cools and is accreted is debated ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). The first hypothesis held that thermal instabilities in the corona form cold clouds ([Maller and Bullock](#maller-bullock-2004) 2004; [Kaufmann et al.](#kaufmann-et-al-2006) 2006). Later studies showed, however, that in galaxies like the Milky Way thermal conduction and buoyancy together damp the thermal instability of the corona, so that cold clouds smaller than 10 kpc can form only beyond 100 kpc from the disc ([Binney, Nipoti and Fraternali](#binney-et-al-2009) 2009; [Nipoti and Posti](#nipoti-posti-2013) 2013). This contradicts the distances of the HVCs, about 5 to 20 kpc ([Wakker](#wakker-2001) 2001; [Wakker et al.](#wakker-et-al-2007) 2007). Cosmological simulations of galaxies like the Milky Way with adaptive mesh refinement, and simulations by smoothed particle hydrodynamics that treat the mixing of phases appropriately, show no spontaneous cooling of the corona either ([Joung et al.](#joung-et-al-2012) 2012; [Hobbs et al.](#hobbs-et-al-2013) 2013). [Joung et al.](#joung-et-al-2012) (2012) found that cold clouds form in the hot corona only through non-linear perturbations, such as a cosmological filament.

The alternative is a mechanism of galactic origin that triggers the cooling of coronal gas, and much observational evidence points to a constant interaction between the cold gas of star-forming galaxies and their coronae ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). Sensitive observations of H I in star-forming disc galaxies find 5 to 10 per cent of their H I a few kiloparsecs above the disc, in the so-called extraplanar layer of H I ([Swaters, Sancisi and van der Hulst](#swaters-et-al-1997) 1997; [Oosterloo, Fraternali and Sancisi](#oosterloo-et-al-2007) 2007). In the Milky Way, most of this emission comes from the intermediate-velocity clouds (IVCs), complexes of cold gas with the metallicity of the disc at about 2 kpc from the Sun ([Marasco and Fraternali](#marasco-fraternali-2011) 2011). The kinematics of the H I halo is regular, but the halo rotates more slowly than the neutral gas of the disc, and its rotation slows with height above the plane. A vertical gradient of $-15$ km s$^{-1}$ kpc$^{-1}$ has been measured in several spiral galaxies, the Milky Way among them, both in neutral and in ionised gas ([Heald et al.](#heald-et-al-2006) 2006; [Oosterloo, Fraternali and Sancisi](#oosterloo-et-al-2007) 2007; [Marasco and Fraternali](#marasco-fraternali-2011) 2011). Most of this gas is thought to consist of fountain clouds, gas that stellar feedback ejects from the disc ([Bregman](#bregman-1980) 1980; [Houck and Bregman](#houck-bregman-1990) 1990), which travels through the coronal gas and falls back onto the disc within about 80 to 100 Myr.

[Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2006) (2006) built a dynamical model of fountain clouds on ballistic orbits through the halo and applied it to the extraplanar H I of the nearby galaxies NGC 891 and NGC 2403 ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). The model reproduces the vertical distribution of the H I halo but underestimates its vertical gradient of rotation, as the observed rotation is slower than the model predicts. [Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2008) (2008) found that the kinematics follows if the fountain clouds lose angular momentum by accreting ambient gas of lower angular momentum. To find the physical process behind this interaction, [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)) simulated in two dimensions a cold cloud, at $10^4$ K and with the metallicity of the disc, travelling through the corona of the Milky Way. The cold gas of the fountain and the hot gas of the corona mix efficiently in a turbulent wake behind the cloud. The mixing shortens the cooling time of the hot gas dramatically, so that part of the corona condenses and is accreted onto the disc. With the model of [Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2008) (2008) and this condensation, [Marasco, Fraternali and Binney](#marasco-et-al-2012) (2012) reproduced the H I halo of the Milky Way, with a present accretion of coronal gas onto the disc of about $2\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$. This agrees with the accretion that the Milky Way requires, about 1 to 3 $M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ ([Chomiuk and Povich](#chomiuk-povich-2011) 2011; [Putman, Peek and Joung](#putman-et-al-2012) 2012). [Fraternali et al.](#fraternali-et-al-2013) (2013) extended the model with warm gas in the turbulent wake, and so reproduced the positions and velocities of most of the warm absorbers observed in the Galactic halo. Accretion driven by the galactic fountain thus seems a viable way for star-forming galaxies to gain the gas that sustains their star formation, and the paper extends its study to other galactic environments (1.2).

#### 2.8.2 The set-up of the simulations

The simulations of [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §3) ran with the code Athena ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008), digested in `athena-synthesis.md`. They use two-dimensional Cartesian geometry, except Sim. 11 of Table 2, which runs in three dimensions to estimate the differences between the geometries. The boundaries on all four sides, six in three dimensions, are semi-permeable, so that gas can flow out of the domain. As in [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)), a cold cloud, rich in metals, travels through a hot and static corona with an initial velocity, its velocity of ejection from the disc. Real fountain clouds orbit above the disc in the gravitational potential of the galaxy and fall back onto it, but the simulations neglect both the gravitational acceleration and the variation of the coronal density along the orbit. The clouds stay at roughly the same distance from the disc: they rise at most a few kiloparsecs above the plane, and their distance from the centre of the galaxy varies by less than 30 per cent ([Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2006) 2006; [Marasco, Fraternali and Binney](#marasco-et-al-2012) 2012). If the corona is in hydrostatic equilibrium in the potential of the galaxy, its density along the orbit is then not expected to vary much. §3 gives neither the size of the domain nor its number of cells. **[GK]** [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2011) (2011, [arXiv:1103.5358](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.5358), §2, Table 1), whose set-up the paper adopts, used 1,024 cells across the direction of motion and about 6,100 along it, of about 2 pc, with the code ECHO rather than Athena.

Table 1 gives the parameters that all simulations share ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). The cloud starts at a temperature $T_\mathrm{cl} = 10^4$ K and a velocity $v_\mathrm{ej} = 75$ km s$^{-1}$, the relative velocity between cloud and ambient medium below which the corona stops absorbing momentum ([Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2011) 2011). The corona has a particle density $n_\mathrm{cor} = 10^{-3}$ cm$^{-3}$. This is lower than the electron density of $2.6 \times 10^{-3}$ cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 10$ kpc that [Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) (2006) found. It compares with the total particle density of $10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$ at 10 kpc above the plane that [Heitsch and Putman](#heitsch-putman-2009) (2009) adopted, and with the mean electron density of $5 \times 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$ within 50 kpc that [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) (2010) inferred from indirect evidence. The metallicity is $Z_\mathrm{cl} = 1.0\,Z_\odot$ in the cloud and $Z_\mathrm{cor} = 0.1\,Z_\odot$ in the corona, in agreement with the values estimated for the galaxies whose hot haloes are observed in X-rays ([Bogdán et al.](#bogdan-et-al-2013a) 2013a; [Hodges-Kluck and Bregman](#hodges-kluck-bregman-2013) 2013; [Anderson, Churazov and Bregman](#anderson-et-al-2016) 2016). For the Milky Way the value is not well defined, but absorption spectra in the far ultraviolet and the emission lines of O VII and O VIII give 0.1 to 0.3 $Z_\odot$ ([Sembach et al.](#sembach-et-al-2003) 2003; [Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015). The density of the cloud follows from pressure equilibrium with the corona. **[GK]** The paper attributes the mean electron density to [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2011) (2011), a study of the hot halo of NGC 1961. The value comes from [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) (2010), who derive it from the dispersion measures of pulsars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, as the mean density between the Sun and that galaxy, 50 kpc away.

Table 2 lists eleven simulations, which differ in the coronal temperature $T_\mathrm{cor}$, the resolution, the conduction and its efficiency, and the radius $R_\mathrm{cl}$ of the cloud ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). Sim. 2 serves as reference, in two dimensions, with $T_\mathrm{cor} = 2 \times 10^6$ K, $R_\mathrm{cl} = 100$ pc, cells of 2 pc and conduction of efficiency $f = 0.1$, the factor on the Spitzer conductivity (2.1); its cloud has a mass of $2.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$. Sim. 1 is the same without conduction. Sim. 5, 6 and 7 change the coronal temperature to 1, 4 and $8 \times 10^6$ K, with cloud masses of 1.2, 4.8 and $9.6 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$, and Sim. 3 and 4 change the cells to 4 and 1 pc. Sim. 8 places a cloud of radius 60 pc and mass $2.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ in the corona of $8 \times 10^6$ K, and Sim. 9 doubles the efficiency of conduction to $f = 0.2$. Sim. 10 and 11 repeat Sim. 2 with cells of 10 pc, in two and in three dimensions. **[GK]** At the density of Sim. 7, a radius of 60 pc gives a mass of $2.1 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$, while a quarter of the mass of Sim. 7, which §4.4 names for Sim. 8 together with that radius, needs a radius of 63 pc.

The main aim is the interaction between cloud and corona, and the efficiency of the condensation of coronal gas, in environments of different temperature ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). The coronal gas has about the virial temperature of the halo of dark matter in which the galaxy resides, $T_\mathrm{vir} = G\mu m_\mathrm{p} M_\mathrm{vir}/(2k_\mathrm{B} r_\mathrm{vir})$ (eq. 10), with $\mu$ the mean molecular weight of the coronal gas, $m_\mathrm{p}$ the mass of the proton, $k_\mathrm{B}$ the Boltzmann constant and $G$ the gravitational constant. Within the virial radius $r_\mathrm{vir} = [3M_\mathrm{vir}/(4\pi\Delta_\mathrm{vir}\rho_\mathrm{cr})]^{1/3}$, the mean density of the halo is $\Delta_\mathrm{vir}$ times the critical density $\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ of the Universe (eq. 11). The virial overdensity $\Delta_\mathrm{vir}$ follows from the dissipationless collapse of a spherical top hat, and depends on the cosmological model and on time. For flat cosmologies, with $\Omega_\mathrm{m} + \Omega_\Lambda = 1$, the paper takes the approximation $(18\pi^2 + 82x - 39x^2)/\Omega_\mathrm{m}(z)$ of [Bryan and Norman](#bryan-norman-1998) (1998). Here $x = \Omega_\mathrm{m}(z) - 1$, and $\Omega_\mathrm{m}(z)$ is the ratio of the mean density of matter to the critical density at redshift $z$. In the $\Lambda$CDM model with $\Omega_\mathrm{m} = 0.3$, $\Delta_\mathrm{vir}$ is then about 350 at $z = 0$. The coronal temperature thus measures the virial mass, $T_\mathrm{cor} \propto M_\mathrm{vir}^{2/3}$, and varying it explores a wide range of virial masses. **[GK]** The fit of [Bryan and Norman](#bryan-norman-1998) (1998, [arXiv:astro-ph/9710107](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9710107), §2.1, eq. 6), $18\pi^2 + 82x - 39x^2$, is the overdensity relative to the critical density, about 101 at $z = 0$ for $\Omega_\mathrm{m} = 0.3$. Only its division by $\Omega_\mathrm{m}(z)$ yields a value near 350, about 337, which is the overdensity relative to the mean density of matter and so multiplies $\Omega_\mathrm{m}\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ rather than $\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ in eq. 11. The paper prints the division under the last term alone, which would give about 57. With $\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ in eq. 11, an overdensity of 350 puts the mean density within $r_\mathrm{vir}$ at about $1/\Omega_\mathrm{m} \approx 3.3$ times that of the definition of Bryan and Norman, so that a given coronal temperature corresponds to a virial mass $\Omega_\mathrm{m}^{1/2} \approx 0.55$ times as large.

Each simulation starts with the cloud in pressure equilibrium with the corona ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). Experiments out of equilibrium showed that the cloud readjusts and reaches pressure equilibrium within about 5 Myr. The density of the cloud is thus fixed by the environment, and rises with the coronal temperature. The initial radius of 100 pc, that of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010), is the same in all simulations but Sim. 8, so that the mass of the cloud follows its density, from $1.2 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ to $9.6 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ in Sim. 5, 2, 6 and 7. These masses agree with those estimated for the Galactic IVCs with good constraints on their distances ([Wakker](#wakker-2001) 2001). The Jeans mass of a standard cloud, with $T_\mathrm{cl} = 10^4$ K and $R_\mathrm{cl} = 100$ pc, is $1.6 \times 10^8\,M_\odot$, so that self-gravity can be neglected. **[GK]** With $P = nk_\mathrm{B}T$ in both phases, the cloud holds $n_\mathrm{cl} = n_\mathrm{cor}T_\mathrm{cor}/T_\mathrm{cl}$ particles per unit volume, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4 and 0.8 cm$^{-3}$ at coronal temperatures of 1, 2, 4 and $8 \times 10^6$ K. The masses of Table 2 then correspond to about 1.16 proton masses per particle of the cloud, a mean molecular weight between those of ionised and of neutral gas, nearer the latter. §3 states no mean molecular weight. [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2011) (2011, §2), whose cloud of the same radius and temperature in the same corona has the same mass, let it vary with temperature.

The simulations of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)) started from clouds that were unrealistically spherical ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). Hydrodynamic simulations of supernova explosions show that the gas ejected from the disc is strongly irregular in shape ([Melioli, de Gouveia Dal Pino and Geraissate](#melioli-et-al-2013) 2013). A cloud that is not spherical offers the corona a wider surface of contact, and so a faster and more efficient interaction. To let the spherical cloud deform quickly, and to make the simulations more realistic, the cloud is given turbulent motions. In every cell of the cloud, the two components of the velocity are drawn at random from a Gaussian distribution with a dispersion of 10 km s$^{-1}$. This agrees with the velocity dispersion, mainly due to turbulence, observed in the H I of the Milky Way and of nearby disc galaxies ([Tamburro et al.](#tamburro-et-al-2009) 2009). A bulk velocity of 75 km s$^{-1}$ along $x$, the direction of motion of the cloud, is added to this turbulent field.

In two dimensions, one of the directions perpendicular to the motion of the cloud is suppressed, so that the simulations follow the flow around an infinite cylinder that moves perpendicular to its axis, with an initially circular cross-section of radius $R_\mathrm{cl}$ ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). The quantities that they yield per unit length of the cylinder are converted to those of an initially spherical cloud of the same radius by multiplying them by the length $4R_\mathrm{cl}/3$, within which the cylinder holds the mass of the sphere. The masses of Table 2 include this correction.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture records how the papers change their codes, chiefly the modules of conduction and cooling of Athena.

### 3.1 Athena in the simulations of 2016

The simulations of [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §2.2) ran with Athena ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008), a parallel, multidimensional code for MHD on grids, digested in `athena-synthesis.md`. It uses Godunov methods of higher order, with a conservative finite-volume discretisation that evolves the volume averages of mass, momentum and total energy. The authors modified its module for isotropic conduction to compute the flux of eq. 8 (2.2.1), which they add directly to the flux of energy. As the explicit update of that module restricts the hydrodynamic time step severely, they replaced it by an implicit update of the temperature (App. A; 2.4). Cooling uses the tables of [Sutherland and Dopita](#sutherland-dopita-1993) (1993), added by operator splitting at first order with an explicit update, and limits the time step to a tenth of the cooling time (2.5.1, 2.7.1). The acknowledgements thank Fabrizio Brighenti for suggestions on implementing conduction in Athena (1.1). **[GK]** The public release of the C version of Athena, version 4.2 at [github.com/PrincetonUniversity/Athena-Cversion](https://github.com/PrincetonUniversity/Athena-Cversion) (commit bb328f4 of 9 June 2017), holds both modules in `src/microphysics/`. There, `conduction.c` adds an explicit heat flux to the energy equation, with an isotropic part from a constant diffusivity times the density and an anisotropic part along the magnetic field, and `integrate_diffusion.c` applies it by operator splitting. A cooling function enrolled as `CoolingFunc`, such as that of [Koyama and Inutsuka](#koyama-inutsuka-2002) (2002) in `cool.c`, enters the unsplit integrators as an explicit source term. `src/integrators/integrate_2d_ctu.c` applies half a step of it to the interface states and a full step with the density and pressure at the half step. An implicit solver for the function of Koyama and Inutsuka, applied by operator splitting, exists as well (`cool_solver.c`). The explicit, first-order and operator-split update of cooling that the paper describes is thus neither of the public options, and the paper does not say how it changed them.
