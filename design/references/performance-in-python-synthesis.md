# Synthesis of performance in Python

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on how finite-volume codes for hyperbolic problems reach high performance in Python, kept in `reference-works/Performance in Python/Docs`. The collection is gathered around the paper of [Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) (2026, [arXiv:2503.20899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20899)) on AGATE, a code for magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) written in Python (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) (2026, §4.7, Table 2), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Works that the papers cite are cited as the papers cite them. Identifiers are checked against the records of [arXiv](#web-arxiv-api) and [Crossref](#web-crossref). Measured rates are restated with their problem, hardware, precision and number of cores, and are converted into cell updates per second where a source gives what the conversion needs.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

### 1.2 Aims and scope

Simulations in physics demand ever more computing, and the codes that run on the largest machines are written in languages of low level, C++ or Fortran in computational physics and heliophysics alike ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §1). These languages give performance but bar the average scientist, while Python, among the most popular languages, makes development and debugging easier. Some frameworks for high-performance computing (HPC) offer interfaces in Python, as AMReX does through pyAMReX ([Myers et al.](#myers-et-al-2024) 2024), but the code beneath such an interface stays hard to modify or extend, and the slowness of Python has kept it out of HPC. Three libraries change this: NumPy vectorises operations on arrays ([Harris et al.](#harris-et-al-2020) 2020), Numba generates machine code that runs on several threads ([Lam, Pitrou and Seibert](#lam-et-al-2015) 2015), and CuPy reaches graphics processing units (GPUs) through the CUDA libraries of NVIDIA ([Okuta et al.](#okuta-et-al-2017) 2017). On them AGATE builds a code written entirely in Python. It solves the ideal, Hall and Chew–Goldberger–Low (CGL) equations of MHD by finite volumes, and is meant for production runs, the development of algorithms, prototyping and teaching alike.

The paper concludes that pure Python is impractical for most uses, that NumPy and Numba give useful speed-ups for small simulations on central processing units (CPUs), and that the modes on the GPU run more than 40 times faster than Numba on large problems (§5). It sees AGATE as a viable platform for MHD in research, applications and teaching, whose modular design prepares further physics, methods and geometries.

Whether Python suits demanding computation at all is debated in astrophysics. [Portegies Zwart](#portegies-zwart-2020) (2020) urges astronomers to abandon it for compiled languages on ecological grounds, and [Augier et al.](#augier-et-al-2021) (2021) answer that Python compiled by its tools can match or beat C++ and Fortran (2.3.4).

### 2.1 The interpreter and vectorisation in NumPy

#### 2.1.1 The array as a view on memory

Lists and dictionaries, the data structures of Python, do not suit fast numerical computation, and the NumPy array was developed for it from the mid-1990s ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011, [arXiv:1102.1523](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.1523)). An array is a uniform collection of elements in any number of dimensions, characterised by the type of its elements and by its shape, and underneath it describes one or more blocks of memory. Five attributes do so: the address of its first byte, the type of its elements, its shape, its strides, which give the number of bytes to skip in memory to reach the next element along each axis, and flags, which say whether the array may be modified and whether its memory is contiguous in the row-major order of C or the column-major order of Fortran. Strides let the same memory be seen in different ways without copying it. A slice that takes every second element along both axes is a view with doubled strides, which shares the memory of its parent, so that writing into the view changes the parent. A transpose or a reshape changes only the strides and the shape, and so costs nothing, and even the type of the elements may be reinterpreted in place. The paper names three techniques that make computation with arrays efficient: vectorising calculations, avoiding copies of data in memory, and minimising the number of operations (abstract).

#### 2.1.2 Vectorisation and broadcasting

Loops of Python that apply a simple computation to each element of a large set of data are slow. Grouping the element-wise operations into operations on whole arrays, called vectorisation, lets NumPy perform them in C ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011). A polynomial of second degree evaluated on $10^5$ numbers takes about 500 ms in a loop of Python and 1 ms vectorised. A forward or central divided difference is written with slices offset by one element, as `(y[1:] - y[:-1])/(x[1:] - x[:-1])`, and runs 100 times faster than a loop on 1000 elements. Projecting $10^5$ points through a camera matrix, by the matrix product of NumPy and a division through broadcasting, takes 9 ms, 70 times faster than a loop.

Arrays of different shapes combine by broadcasting. Their dimensions, aligned from the last, match where they are equal or where one of them is 1 or missing, and the result takes the larger size along each. The broadcast arrays are never built, since NumPy reads their elements through strides of zero. The distances $R_{ijk} = (i^2 + j^2 + k^2)^{1/2}$ on a grid of $200^3$ points, computed from three full arrays of coordinates, allocate four named and five temporary arrays of about 64 MB each, some 576 MB in all, and need 48 million operations. Three one-dimensional vectors of coordinates, broadcast against one another, need about 128 MB and 16 million operations, and take 182 ms instead of 410 ms.

#### 2.1.3 Temporaries and the memory bus

As arrays grow, vectorised expressions slow down through the large temporary arrays that they create ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011). The polynomial $x^2 - 3x + 4$ becomes four operations, each of which allocates an array for its result. Most systems of arrays offer no way around these temporaries, but NumPy lets operations run in place, storing their results in an existing array without allocating new memory. Computing $x^2$ into the output array and then subtracting $3x$ and adding 4 in place takes 600 μs instead of 1 ms, almost twice as fast. $3x$ itself is not computed in place, as that would change the input. Cython, which the same issue of the journal describes ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011), Theano ([Bergstra](#bergstra-2010) 2010) and numexpr ([Cooke et al.](#cooke-et-al-numexpr)) lessen the load on the memory bus further, and Cython serves where code cannot easily be vectorised. Vectorisation and broadcasting are no panacea: where repeated operations run over very large blocks of memory, an outer loop over chunks with vectorised inner operations can use the cache better. The paper concludes that, applied skilfully, NumPy spends most of the time of a computation in vectorised operations rather than in loops of Python, and that optimising compilers such as Cython exploit the cache better still.

**[GK]** Since release 1.13, NumPy avoids some temporaries itself on platforms that provide the C function `backtrace`: it turns `d = a + b + c` into `d = a + b; d += c`, which saves memory bandwidth on large arrays ([NumPy 1.13.0 release notes](#web-numpy-113)). The same release defines the results of operations whose input and output overlap in memory, such as shifted views of one array, as if the inputs were copied first, and makes the copies where a heuristic finds them needed. Universal functions also write into a given array through their argument `out`, which avoids even the temporary $3x$ of the example above ([NumPy documentation](#web-numpy-ufuncs)).

#### 2.1.4 Processors starved for data

The speed of processors has grown faster than that of memory, to the point that processors now wait for data most of the time ([Alted](#alted-2010) 2010, [doi:10.1109/MCSE.2010.51](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCSE.2010.51)). In the early 1980s memory was accessed at about 1 MHz, and memory and processors kept pace up to 16 MHz at the end of the decade. In the early 1990s memory levelled off while processors passed 100 MHz, at which a word is consumed every 10 ns, a rate that no memory could sustain, and the first caches on the chip appeared. By 2000 processors reached 1 GHz and waited up to 50 clock ticks for each read or write of memory, which second-level caches of up to 12 MB came to mitigate. When the frequency could no longer rise, processors gained cores, four in the common processors of 2010, and a third level of cache. The latency of memory is now about 150 times the clock step of a processor or more, and its throughput, although it improves faster than its latency, lags about 25 times behind. Memory does not keep up because faster memory needs more layers of wiring, more complex logic and higher frequencies, whose voltage drives up the consumption of energy and the heat, at a cost that few will pay. The caches nearer the processor are faster and smaller, since speed costs energy, and the hierarchy from the first-level cache to the disk now has at least six levels (Fig. 1).

The organisation of memory has therefore become the key factor in optimisation, and the data structures must be adapted to the hierarchy, which programmers must still learn to do at a fairly low level ([Alted](#alted-2010) 2010; [Drepper](#drepper-2007) 2007). Most remedies exploit two principles. Temporal locality reuses data several times over a short period, so that only the first access fetches it from slow memory. Spatial locality reads data sequentially, which the circuits fetch much faster than scattered data and which prefetching brings into the cache before the processor asks for it. The blocking technique exploits both: it moves a contiguous block that fits into the cache, operates on it and reuses it as much as possible, and writes it back to memory (Fig. 2). Simple in principle, it is less so in practice, since the block may be sized for the first, second or third level of cache, or for main memory, and only experiment can tell which is best. Libraries that already block are therefore to be preferred, such as LAPACK for matrices and numexpr ([Cooke et al.](#cooke-et-al-numexpr)), a virtual machine in Python and C that evaluates complex arithmetic expressions on arrays of any size, blocking them and compiling them just in time for a good balance between the cache and the prediction of branches. Compression can relieve the memory bus as well: the library Blosc, a fast lossless compressor that blocks and shuffles the bytes, moves highly compressible data from memory to the processor faster than uncompressed data, though not data that compress poorly (sidebar).

Several cores on one chip share a single memory bus, which starves them further, and keeping their caches coherent lengthens the latency of memory again ([Alted](#alted-2010) 2010). Non-uniform memory access (NUMA) gives each processor, typically each socket, banks of its own, which it reads quickly while it reads those of the others slowly. This raises the throughput where the data stay local to their processes, but makes moving data between processors more costly, so that software that improves the access to memory matters still more on several cores than on one. Graphics processors reach their memory with up to ten times the bandwidth over independent parallel paths, at the price of smaller memories, and programming models such as OpenCL let them work together with CPUs. The paper concludes that the gap will keep widening and more applications will be limited by the access to memory, so that the arrangement of the data, not the code itself, becomes central to the design of programs. **[GK]** The halving of the rate per core of Athena++ on a full node, which its authors ascribe to the bandwidth of memory rather than to arithmetic (`athena-synthesis.md`, 3.9), is this effect.

### 2.2 Compiled kernels

#### 2.2.1 Five modes of one code

AGATE offers five modes of acceleration, None, Numpy, Numba, Cupy and Kernel, which decide which library carries out the numerical work at the lowest level ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §2.3). The first three run on CPUs and the last two on GPUs, through CuPy. The code selects the matching functions when it starts, and all code above them, the algorithms and the organisation of the program (3.1), is the same in every mode. Each class of implementation defines several versions of each of its functions, each serving one or more modes (App. A). The appendix shows them on a loop over one-dimensional arrays, which computes an intermediate value from a sine, sets it to $-1$ where it is negative, and multiplies it by the mean of two input arrays.

1. None runs the loop in pure Python. It iterates over `numba.prange` instead of `range`, which behaves as `range` outside Numba and so keeps the function valid for the next mode.
2. Numba passes the same function to its just-in-time compiler with `nopython=True` and `parallel=True`, so that the iterations of `prange` are shared among the cores.
3. Numpy needs a function of its own without loops, in operations on whole arrays: the sine of an array, the negative values replaced through a boolean mask, and the result assigned into the output array in place.
4. Cupy compiles a loop body written in Python just in time for the GPU, as Numba does for the CPU, but needs a separate function, since the index of each thread on the GPU is computed from the indices of its block and thread, which have no meaning on a CPU.
5. Kernel writes the loop body in CUDA C as a string, which CuPy compiles and calls as a raw kernel.

Each kernel thus exists in four versions: a loop shared by None and Numba, a vectorised function, a loop for CuPy and a kernel in CUDA C. CuPy also replaces the functions of NumPy on the GPU directly, but preliminary tests found this inferior to the two GPU modes, so AGATE does not offer it. Numba compiles for GPUs as well, which AGATE supports, but CuPy gave consistently faster kernels for effectively the same code, so CuPy is preferred on GPUs.

#### 2.2.2 Cython

Python owes its place in science to its readable and concise code and to its interface to C, through which existing code in C, C++ and Fortran is reused and critical sections are written in C ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011, [doi:10.1109/MCSE.2010.118](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCSE.2010.118)). Computational loops written in Python are slow, largely because the language is so dynamic, and while NumPy removes many of them, some computations are expressed well only as loops. Cython, which grew out of Pyrex by Greg Ewing, extends Python with declarations of C types and compiles the result to C, which is compiled and linked against Python and called from the interpreter. With the declarations, attributes and calls are resolved at compile time, the generated code avoids most of the costs of the run time, and the C compiler applies all its optimisations. Whatever cannot be determined statically keeps the semantics of Python, so that only the parts that dominate the run time need annotations, since 80 per cent of the run time is spent in 20 per cent of the code. The compiler turns idioms of Python into specialised C: a loop over `range` into a loop of C where the range and the type of its variable allow it, `enumerate` into a counter, a chain of comparisons of an integer into a `switch`, and classes declared with `cdef` into structures of C with tables of virtual functions, which serve data that do not fit arrays, such as trees and graphs.

A numerical integral of the Gamma function of the GNU Scientific Library, a loop with typed variables, runs 30 times faster than the same loop in Python, and as fast as the equivalent NumPy expression but with much less memory ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011, Figs. 1 and 2). For expressions on whole arrays, such as $v = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{1/2}$, NumPy is usually fast enough, with two exceptions. On very small arrays evaluated many times, the overhead of Python in each call of NumPy dominates. On very large arrays, NumPy needs large temporaries and moves the intermediate results repeatedly over the memory bus, which in most scientific settings is the bottleneck rather than the processor. A loop in Cython over typed NumPy arrays computes the expression without temporaries and runs about ten times faster on large arrays (Fig. 5). Typed callbacks for solvers of equations and for optimisers cut the overhead of their calls, by a factor of 40 for an ordinary differential equation in 12 variables. Data that do not form rectangular arrays, such as the coefficients $a_{lm}$ of a spherical harmonic transform, stored in one dimension at position $l^2 + l + m$, are handled by plain loops. Their variance per $l$, up to $l_\mathrm{max} = 1500$, runs more than 1000 times faster in Cython than in Python, and 27 times faster than a loop of Python over calls of NumPy.

Cython calls C and C++ natively, and Fortran through Fwrap, a separate tool that wraps Fortran 77 to 2003 through the interoperability of Fortran 2003 with C ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011). Arrays passed to Fortran are laid out in column-major order, by `np.asfortranarray` or `order='F'`, so that no copy is needed, and arguments of intent out are modified in place. Compared with pure Python, the disadvantages of Cython are the time of compilation and a separate build phase, so that projects mix modules of Python and of Cython. An experimental pure mode declares the types through decorators that the interpreter ignores. Compared with C and Fortran, its main limit is parallelism in shared memory. Sections declared `nogil` contain only C and may run in parallel, which is tedious, and OpenMP is not yet supported, while parallelism by message passing between processes, as with MPI, is well supported. Compared with C++, it lacks templates, so that code must be repeated for each type of data or generated by a template engine. Cython pays where the time goes into low-level arithmetic in the code itself rather than in libraries, and where the computation does not express well in operations of NumPy, as in sequential algorithms.

**[GK]** The present documentation of Cython describes parallel loops through `cython.parallel.prange`, run by OpenMP, which flags of the compiler such as `-fopenmp` enable, and whose body has had to release the GIL. From Cython 3.3 the body may hold the GIL, which runs in parallel only on free-threaded builds of Python ([Cython documentation, parallelism](#web-cython-parallelism)). From Cython 3.1, a module declares with the directive `freethreading_compatible = True` that it may run without the GIL. A module without it re-enables the GIL when a free-threaded interpreter imports it, and the support is called experimental ([Cython documentation, free threading](#web-cython-freethreading)).

#### 2.2.3 Numba

Python is a dynamically typed and interpreted language, productive through its syntax, its flexible semantics and its libraries, among which NumPy provides the foundation of efficient numerical computation, the ndarray: a buffer of one type of element, contiguous in the order of C or of Fortran or with arbitrary strides ([Lam, Pitrou and Seibert](#lam-et-al-2015) 2015, [doi:10.1145/2833157.2833162](https://doi.org/10.1145/2833157.2833162), §1). Operations on whole arrays run in C, while loops of Python over the elements pass through several layers of indirection. Numba was first developed to make such uses of NumPy efficient, by translating the indexing of an ndarray into direct loads and stores through pointers, without the extensions in C or the hybrid languages such as Cython that were needed before. It is a just-in-time compiler for CPython that works one function at a time, loaded as a library rather than replacing the interpreter, and it targets a subset of Python rich in ndarrays and numerical scalars in loops (§2). From the pointer to the data, the shape and the strides of an ndarray, it generates loops specialised for arrays: a naive multiplication of matrices runs 463, 454, 280 and 276 times faster than in CPython 3.4 for matrices of $64^2$ to $512^2$ elements with Numba 0.20, against 453, 407, 263 and 268 times in C (Table 1). Numba neither traces nor replaces the interpreter. A decorator replaces a function by an object that compiles it when it is first called with a new combination of types, which local inference derives from the arguments, so that no annotations are needed. The compiled code departs from the semantics of Python in a few respects: integers have fixed widths of up to 64 bits instead of arbitrary size, all polymorphism must be resolved at compile time, and assignments coerce types implicitly. The ndarray already has similar limits, and they allow aggressive optimisation. Back ends for CUDA, through the library NVVM, and for the HSA architecture of AMD expose the execution model of each GPU directly to Python (§2, §4.7).

The decorator `@jit` compiles a specialised version for each new set of types of the arguments and reuses it afterwards (§4). The bytecode is disassembled into basic blocks and its stack machine into virtual registers, a step tailored to each version of CPython, and translated into the intermediate representation of Numba, in which an assignment visible to all later blocks defines a new variable, so that a name may change its type (§4.1 to §4.3). Local type inference propagates the types through a graph of dependencies until a fixed point, unifying types that differ by coercion (§4.4). Where every type is inferred, the representation is lowered to LLVM in the mode without Python, whose functions may run without the GIL and in parallel threads. Otherwise all values become Python objects, in the object mode, which calls the C API of Python and only removes the overhead of the loop of the interpreter. Two optimisations at a high level follow (§4.5). Loops in functions compiled in object mode are extracted into functions of their own, which get a second chance to compile without Python. Array expressions such as `a * x + y` are rewritten into a single loop over the arrays, which allocates only the output and keeps intermediate values in registers. Lowering emits for each function the compiled function and a wrapper that unboxes the Python objects into native arguments and boxes the results (§4.6). For the GPU, arrays passed as arguments are copied to the device and back automatically, sometimes needlessly, or by explicit calls of the user (§4.7).

**[GK]** The present release of Numba, 0.68.1 of 26 October 2026, supports Python 3.10 to 3.15 and publishes wheels on the Python Package Index for Linux, Windows and macOS on ARM, since release 0.65.0 also for the free-threaded Python 3.14 ([Numba documentation](#web-numba-install); [PyPI](#web-pypi-json)). Its wheels for macOS on Intel processors ended with release 0.62.1 of September 2025, which supports Python only up to 3.13, so that the Package Index holds no Numba for Python 3.14 on an Intel Mac, such as the development machine of mhdpy.

#### 2.2.4 Pythran

A scientific application in Python costs from twice the time of its equivalent in C, where the Python only calls routines of C, to a hundred times or more, where it accesses arrays element by element ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, [doi:10.1088/1749-4680/8/1/014001](https://doi.org/10.1088/1749-4680/8/1/014001), §1). The cost comes from interpretation, from the dynamic binding of names, and from the absence of any optimisation of the byte code. As scientific applications are said to spend 90 per cent of their time in 10 per cent of the code, a compiler need optimise only a part of an application. Two families of tools close the gap. Static translators turn Python into C or C++: Cython through the Python/C API, Nuitka likewise into C++, and ShedSkin into C++ without that API for implicitly typed programs, failing on dynamic ones. Just-in-time compilers turn the costly parts into native code while the program runs: numexpr for expressions of NumPy given as strings, Numba and Parakeet for code centred on NumPy, and PyPy for the whole language. Apart from PyPy, none applies the static optimisations that compilers of C have used for decades, and those that translate to C leave them to the C compiler, which can do little with the generated code: GCC hardly vectorises the output of Cython, although the semantics of the arrays are known at the level of Python.

Pythran compiles a module written in a subset of Python 2.7 into parametric C++ ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §1, §2). It supports comprehensions, generator expressions, lambda functions, nested and polymorphic functions and global variables, but not classes of the user or dynamic features such as introspection and variables of changing type. Unlike Cython, its input stays valid Python, which runs unchanged in the interpreter. Comments such as `# pythran export foo(int list, float)` give the signatures of the functions that leave the module, and the types of all other functions are inferred from their calls. A front end turns the module into the abstract syntax tree of Python and then into a simpler intermediate representation, a subset of that tree. A middle end analyses and optimises it, and a back end turns it into C++ templates, instantiates them for the exported signatures and compiles them. The generated code needs a compiler of C++11, as g++ 4.8.2 or clang++ 3.3, Boost.Python for the bindings, and the template library pythonic, which emulates the standard library of Python and NumPy on top of the Standard Template Library of C++, GMP for long integers, and the Numerical Template Toolbox (NT2) for vectors.

The analyses are type-agnostic (§3). An alias analysis finds which locations a name may denote, so that `sum` is known to be the built-in. Purity analyses find functions without global effects and without changes to their arguments, from which follow maps that may run in parallel and expressions that are constant. Chains of use and definition remove dead code and rename variables whose type changes, which C++ could not hold. The optimisations at the level of Python turn generator expressions and `map`, `zip` and `filter` into lazy iterators where this is valid, which removes temporary lists, fold constant expressions at compile time, and unroll loops over lists of known length, while inlining is left to the C++ compiler (§4). Expressions of NumPy receive three optimisations at the level of the library (§5). Expression templates fuse their universal functions into a single loop without intermediate arrays, which also raises the arithmetic per element. The fused loop is vectorised with the instructions for vectors of the processor through boost.simd of NT2. And it is parallelised through OpenMP, as such loops carry no dependence. Loops written by the user are parallelised explicitly by directives of OpenMP placed in comments, such as `#omp parallel for` (§6). Parallelising outer loops is often better than relying on the inner expressions of NumPy, as it avoids a barrier at the end of each expression and gives each thread more work. A computation of a Julia set parallelised so on its outer loop reaches the perfect speed-up of 4 on four cores.

**[GK]** The present documentation of Pythran, version 0.19.0, gives Python 3.7 and later as supported, the support of Python 2.7 having ended with version 0.9.5, and gives instructions for Linux and macOS, with Windows in progress ([Pythran documentation](#web-pythran-docs)).

#### 2.2.5 Designing a code with Numba

Libraries such as NumPy and SciPy escape the GIL, which restricts the interpreter of CPython to one thread, by running their work in compiled extensions, so that the GIL limits performance only where a program passes control back and forth between the interpreter and compiled code ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, [arXiv:2303.08394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08394), pp. 1–2). This happens where no compiled implementation of the algorithm exists, or where the interpreter must organise much data to feed one. Numba promises to remove the need for a compiled language there. It compiles a subset of Python that works on NumPy arrays through the compiler infrastructure LLVM, which generates machine code for CPUs and GPUs, vectorises it automatically where the hardware allows, and may run it on several threads outside the GIL, while the metadata of the arrays on their dimensions, type and layout let the code use the caches well (p. 2). A function marked by a decorator is compiled when it is first called, for the types of its arguments, and the result is cached for later calls with the same types (Fig. 1). The bytecode is analysed into an intermediate representation, the types are inferred from those of the inputs, and LLVM generates the machine code. Each call unboxes its Python objects into native types and boxes its results again, without copying memory, but at a cost per call.

The authors test the promise of Numba on PyExaFMM, a multithreaded implementation of the kernel-independent fast multipole method in three dimensions, an algorithm built on an octree that needs much organisation of data and careful allocation of memory ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, pp. 2, 4–6). Its pitfalls are several (p. 3). Where Numba finds no type of its own for a Python object, or meets an unsupported feature, it compiles in object mode, which is no faster than Python and happens silently unless the mode without Python is demanded. Three ways of writing the same steps, two products of random matrices whose rows are stored in a dictionary typed for Numba, run at different speeds, 29.1 ms against 25.7 ms for matrices of $1000 \times 1000$, apparently depending on whether the dictionary is created inside or outside the compiled functions (Table 1, Listing 2). The optimisations that cause such differences are opaque to the user, and a typed dictionary used from the interpreter is always slower than a dictionary of Python because of the boxing. Numba is therefore closer to a framework of programming than to a mere compiler, with a learning curve of its own: performance demands familiarity with its internals and may change the design of algorithms and data structures substantially. The measurements were made on an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3970X of 32 cores, with Python 3.8.5 and Numba 0.53.0.

The authors conclude that optimal multithreaded performance with Numba needs careful consideration of the algorithm, of the implementation of Numba and of a data-oriented design, and that the expertise required may exceed that of the audience at which Numba aims ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, pp. 8–9). Numba nevertheless transforms projects that value the expressiveness of Python, simple builds on all platforms and its ecosystem, and that have a few isolated bottlenecks. PyExaFMM stays concise, at 4901 lines, deploys on all platforms through Conda and the usual channels of Python, and needs no separate interface to a compiled library.

#### 2.2.6 Data-centric Python

The speed of NumPy rests on calls of optimised methods and on vectorised operations on arrays, both of which tend to vanish in larger scientific codes, which leaves a gap between code written in the style of NumPy and the code that domain scientists actually write ([Ziogas et al.](#ziogas-et-al-2021) 2021, [arXiv:2107.00555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00555), §1). Data-centric Python, built on the framework DaCe, compiles each function marked with the decorator `@dace.program` from a subset of Python centred on NumPy arrays into a stateful dataflow multigraph (SDFG), an intermediate representation that makes every movement of data explicit ([Ben-Nun et al.](#ben-nun-et-al-2019) 2019) (§2). Arguments annotated with types and symbolic shapes allow compilation ahead of time, and loops may be declared parallel through `dace.map` or found by a transformation. Not supported are containers of Python other than arrays as arguments, dynamic typing, variables whose existence depends on the flow of control, and recursion (§2.5). A first pass of 14 transformations coarsens the dataflow of the direct translation, much as a C compiler at `-O1`, for instance by fusing consecutive states and removing redundant copies (§2.4). An automatic pass, comparable to `-O3`, then cleans up and collapses the parallel maps, greedily fuses the largest subgraphs that share an iteration space, tiles maps whose writes conflict so as to cut atomic operations, moves small arrays to the stack, and allocates temporaries once for the whole program (§3.1). On CPUs it raises parallelism through the clause `collapse` of OpenMP. Operations of libraries, such as matrix products, expand into calls of MKL or cuBLAS, or into dataflow of their own (§3.2). DaCe generates C++ for CPUs and C++ with CUDA for GPUs, and 90 per cent of the CPU and GPU codes of the evaluation compile in less than 15 s (§3.3, Fig. 6).

#### 2.2.7 Wrapped Fortran kernels in PyClaw

[Ketcheson et al.](#ketcheson-et-al-2012) (2012, [arXiv:1111.6583](https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.6583), §1) build PyClaw by an approach in three steps: Python gives a convenient interface to serial legacy code, then couples that code to tools for parallelisation with minimal changes to it, and finally to other packages that extend its function. The legacy code is Clawpack, a package of Fortran for hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, freely available since 1994 and used by more than 7000 registered users, with the wave-propagation methods of second order of [LeVeque](#leveque-2002) (2002) and their extension to several dimensions by transverse Riemann solvers, and SharpClaw, its sibling of high order with WENO reconstruction of fifth order and a strong-stability-preserving Runge–Kutta method of fourth order (§2.1). Only the Riemann solver is specific to a hyperbolic system, and the kernels of Fortran that perform the steps are wrapped automatically by f2py, as are routines of a user for initial conditions or source terms (§3.1). NumPy joins the pieces, and the package PyWENO generates further kernels of WENO in Fortran, from fifth to seventeenth order, without temporary work arrays (§3.3). An earlier study of stencil updates in Python ([Langtangen and Cai](#langtangen-cai-2008) 2008) had found vectorised NumPy fast enough for some operations and loops of Fortran wrapped for Python as fast as pure Fortran, which PyClaw extends from kernels to a whole object-oriented framework (§5.1).

#### 2.2.8 Stencils generated from Python in Pace

Pace writes the nonhydrostatic dynamical core FV3, a finite-volume solver on the cubed sphere ([Putman and Lin](#putman-lin-2007) 2007), and the cloud microphysics of the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory entirely in Python, through the domain-specific language GT4Py ([Dahm et al.](#dahm-et-al-2023) 2023, [doi:10.5194/gmd-16-2719-2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-16-2719-2023), abstract, §3). GT4Py analyses functions written as stencils and compiles them into C++ or CUDA, bound back to Python, while a back end that generates NumPy needs no compilation and serves debugging and quick development (§2.3, Fig. 1). A stencil updates every point of a three-dimensional field from a fixed pattern of neighbours. It always runs in parallel over the horizontal plane, while in the vertical it runs in parallel, forwards or backwards, as declared, and indices are relative to the point updated, as `Array[0,0,0] - Array[1,0,0]`, whatever the layout of the field in memory. Helper functions are inlined at no cost, and the extent over which intermediate quantities must be computed, ghost points included, is deduced automatically. To express FV3, the authors extended GT4Py with fields of two and four dimensions, loops in the vertical, and computations restricted to horizontal regions near the edges and corners of the faces of the cube (§4.1, Fig. 5). Compiled for one configuration at a time, the stencils let the compiler of the language reduce accesses to main memory by replacing temporary fields with small buffers, inline functions and conditionals, prune branches that the constants of the configuration rule out, and fuse operators into single kernels (§5.1). The calls of many small compiled stencils from Python still cost time, so a back end through DaCe compiles the whole loop over the time steps, Python between the stencils included, into one executable called once from Python, which removes that overhead and lets optimisations act across stencils (§5.2).

### 2.3 Measured comparisons of the routes on the same kernels

#### 2.3.1 The modes of AGATE on the Orszag–Tang vortex

AGATE times its modes on the Orszag–Tang vortex in ideal MHD with divergence cleaning, run to $t = 0.5$ with the default algorithm on grids of $32^2$ to $2048^2$ cells ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §4.7, Table 2). The vortex fills $[0, 1]^2$ with periodic boundaries, starting from $\rho = 25/(36\pi)$, $p = 5/(12\pi)$, $v_x = -\sin(2\pi y)$, $v_y = \sin(2\pi x)$, $B_x = -B_0 \sin(2\pi y)$ and $B_y = B_0 \sin(4\pi x)$, with $B_0 = 1/\sqrt{4\pi}$ (§4.2). The machine holds two AMD EPYC processors of 64 cores each, with two threads per core, and an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Numba runs on 16 threads, the number that performed best in preliminary tests on $256^2$ cells. Pure Python is impractical, and NumPy is about 100 times faster on $128^2$ cells, where pure Python takes 1802 s and NumPy 17.6 s. Numba compiles for about 13 s at the start of each run, and is 2 to 5 times faster than NumPy on large grids: on $1024^2$ cells, NumPy takes 7477 s and Numba 1320 s. The modes on the GPU run more than 40 times faster than Numba and more than 300 times faster than NumPy on the larger grids. The Kernel mode takes 12.0 s on $1024^2$ cells and 87.1 s on $2048^2$, where NumPy and Numba were not run, as they would have taken too long.

Those of Numba grow by only 5.3 and those of the Kernel mode by 3.9, so that both parallel modes use their hardware better on larger grids. #### 2.3.2 Pythran against CPython, Cython, PyPy and numexpr

The expression $d = (b^2 + c^2)^{1/2}$ of NumPy, timed on a quad-core i7 with hyper-threading, shows the three optimisations of Pythran in turn ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §5). NumPy takes 1.23 ms. Expression templates, which replace four temporary arrays and four loops by one allocation and one loop, bring it to 621 μs, vectorisation to 418 μs, and OpenMP on the four cores to 105 μs, about eleven times faster than NumPy. Vectorisation falls short of the factor of four that four numbers of double precision per AVX register promise, because of the unaligned transfers between memory and the registers. numexpr takes 395 μs, almost four times as long as Pythran. The paper does not give the length of the arrays.

Four benchmarks compare CPython 2.7.3, Cython 0.20, PyPy 2.0.2 and Pythran on the same machine, without any annotation of parallelism, each the best of repeated runs of `timeit` and compiled by g++ 4.8.2, mostly with `-O3` ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §7, Tables 1 to 4). The Cython versions needed manual conversion, with checks of bounds and negative indices off, C types for all variables and the mathematical functions of libm.

- NQueens, from the project Unladen Swallow, uses generator expressions and sets of integers heavily: 181 ms in CPython, 42.3 ms in Cython, 51.3 ms in PyPy and 22.5 ms in Pythran, eight times faster than CPython. Its lazy evaluation avoids many allocations, but more than half of its time still goes into allocating and freeing a set in the inner loop.
- rosen_der, a complex expression of NumPy on one-dimensional slices: 22.3 ms in CPython, 3.39 ms in Cython, 3.81 ms in Pythran and 51.3 ms in PyPy, whose support of NumPy was not yet mature. Cython is slightly faster, but only after its code was rewritten by hand as an explicit loop, and the automatic parallelisation of Pythran gains a further factor of four without any change to the input.
- growcut, image processing with explicit indexing of every element: 398 ms in CPython, 991 μs in Cython, 526 μs in Pythran and 5.54 ms in PyPy. All compilers gain hugely, as single elements are extremely slow to access in NumPy, and Pythran gains most, probably through cleaner code.
- blackscholes, with a list instead of an array as input, measures the cost of converting Python objects into dense native data: 490 μs in CPython, 74.7 μs in Cython, 34.8 μs in Pythran and 37.3 μs in PyPy, which needs no such conversion.

#### 2.3.4 Compiled Python on the N-body problem

[Portegies Zwart](#portegies-zwart-2020) (2020, [arXiv:2009.11295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.11295), §3, Fig. 3) estimates the carbon that a direct N-body calculation of $2^{14}$ particles in a Plummer sphere emits in several languages, on an Intel Xeon E-2176M at 2.7 GHz and an NVIDIA P100. Python takes far longer and emits far more than C++ or Fortran, and among 27 languages tested elsewhere only Perl and Lua are slower. NumPy and Numba could speed Python up by large factors, but are rarely used so, and the comment urges astronomers to abandon Python for compiled languages, or to run on many cores or GPUs, and educators to reconsider teaching it.

[Augier et al.](#augier-et-al-2021) (2021, [doi:10.1038/s41550-021-01342-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-021-01342-y)) agree that scientists should mind their carbon footprint, but reply that avoiding Python would be counterproductive. The comment had timed an inefficient implementation in Python, 50 times slower than one in C++. Interpreted and compiled are properties of implementations, not of languages: the standard interpreter CPython is slow for lack of a compiler at run time, but most programs spend their time in optimised libraries such as NumPy, and by the rule of 80 to 20 only a few lines need low-level code. In the N-body problem these are the loop over pairs of particles, executed $N^2/2$ times per step, $1.3 \times 10^{12}$ times for the $N = 16384$ particles and $10^4$ steps of the comment, which three tools compile: Pythran ahead of time to C++, Numba just in time through LLVM, and the alternative interpreter PyPy with its own just-in-time compiler.

They time ten implementations for 10 N-body time units on nodes of Grid'5000 with Intel Xeon E5-2630 at 2.3 GHz, measuring the energy with wattmeters and converting it at 283 g of CO₂ per kWh ([Augier et al.](#augier-et-al-2021) 2021, Fig. 1). C++ and Fortran come from the website [nbabel.org](http://www.nbabel.org/), as in the comment, five versions are in Python and three in Julia, all compiled with flags such as `-Ofast` and `-march=native`, with GCC 8.3.0, Python 3.8.5, Pythran 0.9.8 and Numba 0.52. The versions with Pythran and Numba use NumPy only for its arrays, not its functions. Four versions in Python run faster than those in C++ and Fortran, and a naive version, plain NumPy code accelerated only by decorating one function with `@transonic.jit` of the project FluidDyn ([Augier, Mohanan and Bonamy](#augier-et-al-2019) 2019), is only three times slower than Fortran. Versions with Pythran and OpenMP and in Julia also run on 6 and 12 cores. Counting only the energy of the cores used, the version with Pythran on 12 threads runs ten times faster than on one but emits only half as much, as the power drawn grows with the cores. The authors conclude that performance depends less on the language than on the time spent on optimisation and the skill to use the right tools, and that education and tooling matter most for the ecological footprint of computing, whatever the language.

### 2.4 Threads, the global interpreter lock and free threading

#### 2.4.1 Threads in Numba

Numba runs loops on several threads through `prange` in functions compiled with `parallel=True`, a syntax reminiscent of OpenMP ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, p. 7, Listing 3). Its threads come from a threading layer, OpenMP or Intel's Threading Building Blocks (TBB), chosen through the environment variable `NUMBA_THREADING_LAYER`. PyExaFMM takes OpenMP, which suits loops whose iterations carry similar work. Operations of NumPy inside compiled functions keep the threads of the libraries that implement them, such as OpenBLAS or the Math Kernel Library of Intel, so that a parallel region of Numba that calls them nests parallelism within parallelism. The threads then outnumber the cores, which leaves threads idle, breaks the locality of the caches and may make threads hang while they wait for one another. PyExaFMM therefore sets `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1` before it starts, so that only the threads of Numba run.

Each operator of PyExaFMM is parallelised over the nodes of the tree that it updates ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, pp. 7–8). For one operator, copying the data of each target into vectors that lie together in memory before the parallel loop makes it 13 per cent faster, on 32,768 leaves of at most 150 particles each with an expansion of order 10, about $10^6$ particles on the surface of a sphere. For the operators with long lists of interactions, such copies would take about 17 GB and cost more time to allocate than they save, so these loop over the targets and look up their data in place. On the same tree with an expansion of order 6, the whole algorithm takes $5.95 \pm 0.02$ s, after $9.00 \pm 0.01$ s of precomputation. The time spent outside the compiled code, in boxing, in organising inputs and in passing control between Numba and Python, stays below 5 per cent of each operator except one, whose organisation of data must run in the interpreter under the GIL, so that the code runs on all cores nearly all the time (Fig. 3).

#### 2.4.2 Making the global interpreter lock optional

The GIL of CPython lets only one thread execute Python code at a time, which makes processors of several cores hard to use from Python ([Gross](#gross-2023) 2023, PEP 703, Abstract). For scientific computing this lack of concurrency often matters more than the speed of Python itself, since most cycles go into optimised kernels, but a call of any Python code by one thread can hold up all others (Motivation). Developers of machine learning and bioinformatics report that threads in Python stop scaling at fewer than ten, and that processes do not help where their communication costs too much. The maintainers of scikit-learn, NumPy and SciPy describe the workarounds that the GIL forces: pools of processes with memory mapping and serialisation by pickle, and the burden of keeping the threads of BLAS from oversubscribing the cores. NumPy releases the GIL in its inner loops, but offers no way to use all cores of a machine, which it leaves to Dask and other solutions built on processes, less efficient and clumsier to use.

PEP 703 adds a build option, `--disable-gil`, which compiles CPython to run Python code without the GIL. The GIL stays the default, and the tag of the ABI of the new build carries the letter t (Specification). Four groups of changes make the interpreter safe for threads without it.

1. Reference counting becomes biased. Each object belongs to the thread that created it, which updates a local count without atomic instructions, while other threads update a shared count atomically, since most objects are used by one thread only. Objects that live as long as the program, such as small integers, `None` and interned strings, become immortal and skip counting, and objects that many threads share, such as functions, code objects and modules, defer it.
2. The allocator pymalloc, unsafe without the GIL, gives way to mimalloc, whose structures also let the collector of cycles find all objects and let most reads of dictionaries and lists avoid locks.
3. The collector of cycles stops all threads while it runs, and it gives up its generations.
4. Containers such as lists and dictionaries carry a lock of one byte each, taken through Python critical sections, which release locks implicitly so as to avoid deadlocks, while reads optimistically avoid locking.

The specialising interpreter specialises each bytecode only once in programs with several threads. An extension module declares through a slot `Py_mod_gil` that it runs without the GIL. Importing one without that slot pauses all threads, enables the GIL and warns, and the environment variable `PYTHONGIL` forces the GIL off or on. The changes cost time: on pyperformance 1.0.6, 6 per cent with one thread and 8 with several on Intel Skylake, and 5 and 7 per cent on AMD Zen 3, mostly through biased reference counting and then through the locks per object (Performance). The default build is unaffected. Extensions in C must be rebuilt for the new ABI, guard their own global state with locks, and give up borrowed references where other threads might free the objects (Backwards Compatibility). The author foresees a path on which the GIL becomes an option at run time after two or three releases and is disabled by default after another two or three (Open Issues).

**[GK]** PEP 779 set the criteria for the second of three phases, in which the free-threaded build is officially supported but optional, for Python 3.14 ([Wouters, Page and Gross](#web-pep-779) 2025). It gives the penalty in single-threaded performance as about 10 per cent at the time, 3 per cent on macOS, and the extra memory as 15 to 20 per cent. The guide of CPython 3.14 to free threading gives the average overhead on pyperformance as about 1 per cent on macOS on ARM to 8 per cent on Linux on x86-64, and adds that a running interpreter reports through `sys._is_gil_enabled()` whether the GIL is active ([Python documentation](#web-python-free-threading)).

#### 2.4.3 The free-threaded build on six cores

[Montoya Salazar](#montoya-salazar-2026) (2026, [arXiv:2603.04782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04782)) compares the time, the use of the processor, the memory and the energy of Python 3.14.2 in its default and its free-threaded builds, on a laptop with an Intel Core i7-8750H of six cores and twelve hardware threads at up to 4.1 GHz and 16 GiB of memory, under Ubuntu 24.04.3 (§4.1). A profiler written for the study samples every 50 ms, each point runs ten times in each build with pauses of 60 s, and the ratios of matched runs, free-threaded over default, are averaged geometrically with confidence intervals of 95 per cent (§4.2, §4.4, §4.5). The workloads fall into four groups (§4.3): operations of NumPy, namely vectorised arithmetic, matrix products and fast Fourier transforms; sequential kernels in pure Python, a Mandelbrot set, a bubble sort and a sieve of primes; numerical work in pure Python shared among threads by `ThreadPoolExecutor`, factorials, matrix products over blocks of rows and an N-body problem of 2000 particles over 10 steps; and threaded work on objects of Python.

The results fall into four patterns (§5).

- NumPy: the builds do not differ, with ratios of time between 0.997 and 1.015 over 21 points, as the work runs inside the library. Only the virtual memory grows, by about 1 GB, which mimalloc reserves without using it.
- Sequential Python: the free-threaded build runs 13 to 17 per cent slower on the sieve, 33 to 35 per cent on the bubble sort and 40 to 43 per cent on the Mandelbrot set, more than the 8 per cent that the guide of CPython states, and uses 12 to 24 per cent more resident memory.
- Threaded numerical Python: one thread is 17 to 21 per cent slower than in the default build, but six threads run the factorials and the N-body problem about four times faster than the default build (ratios of 0.23 to 0.25), with little further gain beyond the six physical cores, while the default build gains nothing from threads (Tables 4 and 5).
- Threaded objects: work on data of its own per thread scales almost as well, while threads that modify one shared list run 5.2 times slower than in the default build with one worker and 12.2 times slower with twelve, through the contention of the locks per object.

Energy follows time to within 1 per cent over all 84 points, so that a faster run is a more economical one, even where it occupies more cores (§5.6). The author concludes that the free-threaded build is no universal improvement: it pays where a substantial part of the run is parallel work on independent data, and costs time and memory everywhere else (§5.7, §7). **[GK]** The processor is a close relative of that of the development machine of mhdpy, an i7-9750H of the same family with six cores, but the operating system differs, and the guide of CPython 3.14 states a smaller overhead on macOS than on Linux (2.4.2).

#### 2.4.4 OpenMP in pure Python

[Piñeiro and Pichel](#pineiro-pichel-2026) (2026, [arXiv:2411.14887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14887)) bring the directives of OpenMP 3.0 to Python on the free-threaded build of Python 3.13, written in pure Python. A function that holds directives is decorated with `@omp`, and the directives take the form of blocks such as `with omp("parallel for reduction(+:count)")`, or of plain calls for directives without a block (§3). When the module is loaded, the decorator retrieves the source of the function through the module `inspect`, parses it into a syntax tree through `ast`, replaces each directive by calls of its runtime, which starts threads through the module `threading` and shares out the iterations, compiles the new tree and returns it in place of the function (§3, §3.4). The runtime also offers the functions of OpenMP, such as those for the number of threads and the schedule.

On two Intel Xeon Gold 5220R of 24 cores each, with Python 3.13, NumPy 2.1.1 and mpi4py 4.0.0, six numerical kernels in pure Python scale poorly ([Piñeiro and Pichel](#pineiro-pichel-2026) 2026, §4.1, Fig. 8): a fast Fourier transform of $4 \times 10^6$ complex numbers, a Jacobi iteration on a matrix of $1000 \times 1000$, an LU decomposition, molecular dynamics of 2000 particles, and two integrals computed by sums over $10^9$ and $2 \times 10^9$ points. Only four of the six gain from four threads, more threads slow them down, and the best speed-up is 3.18, for the molecular dynamics on four threads. The cores are busy for about 54 per cent of the time on 8 threads and 22 per cent on 16 (Fig. 9). OMP4Py itself adds less than 0.2 per cent on one thread, and dividing the loop by hand changes nothing, but compiling the body of the loop for $\pi$ with Cython, through the decorator `@cython.compile` and declared types, cuts its time on one thread from about 785 s to about 10 s and scales it by a factor of 21.7 on 24 threads (Figs. 10 and 11). The authors conclude that the interpreter of Python 3.13 does not yet support threads maturely, with more than 100 open issues on free threading in February 2025, 15 of them crashes, rather than that OMP4Py is at fault. PyOMP, a fork of Numba with directives of OpenMP ([Mattson, Anderson and Georgakoudis](#mattson-et-al-2021) 2021), runs the same kernels much faster but scales little, by factors of 2.3 at most on 24 threads, and not at all for some kernels. Work on objects of Python, such as the clustering of a graph with NetworkX and the counting of words, which PyOMP cannot compile, scales well, by up to 25.5 on 48 threads (§4.2).

**[GK]** The poor scaling of numerical work in pure Python on Python 3.13 contrasts with the fourfold gain on six cores that [Montoya Salazar](#montoya-salazar-2026) (2026) measures with Python 3.14.2 (2.4.3), which suggests that the free-threaded interpreter improved between the two releases, although the workloads and machines differ.

#### 2.4.5 Threads over segments in ten languages

[Diehl et al.](#diehl-et-al-2024) (2024, [arXiv:2307.01117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01117)) implement one model of the stencil codes on block-structured meshes in ten languages and libraries, Chapel, Charm++, C++, HPX, Go, Julia, Python, Rust, Swift and Java, and measure it in shared memory (§1, §2). The heat equation in one dimension on a periodic loop is advanced by the Euler method in time with second-order differences in space (eq. 2), each thread updating a segment of the grid, while the ghost values pass between neighbouring segments through queues (2.6.2). The version in Python, run with Python 3.10, keeps each segment in a NumPy array, which brings vector instructions, starts a thread of the module `threading` per segment, and exchanges the ghost values through `queue.Queue`, which is synchronised and may cost more than needed, while a queue written by hand did no better (§3.7, Table 2). On $10^6$ points over 1000 steps, on an Intel Xeon Gold 6148, an AMD EPYC 7H12 and a Fujitsu A64FX with up to 40 cores, Python is the slowest on every architecture (§6, Fig. 2). Its time falls to a minimum of about 1 s on about eight cores of the Intel and AMD processors and rises again to about 4 s on 40, while C++, Rust, Chapel, Charm++ and HPX keep falling, to a few hundredths of a second. By lines of code, Python is the second shortest after Chapel, and with Julia and Chapel it forms the group that a model of the cost of software, COCOMO, rates as the easiest to write (§5, Fig. 1). The authors ascribe its slowness to its being interpreted, and see it as a good choice for prototyping and where performance is not critical (§3.7, §7).

**[GK]** The rise of the time of Python beyond about eight threads fits a GIL that the threads must take between their calls of NumPy and around their queues: as the segments shrink, the overhead per call stays, and the threads spend ever more of their time waiting for the lock. The paper tests neither this nor a free-threaded build. Its best time, about 1 s for $10^9$ updates of a point, corresponds to about $10^9$ updates per second for this simplest of stencils. Eq. 2 divides the second difference by $2h$ where $h^2$ is meant, a slip without effect on the cost. The text of §6 names an Intel Xeon Gold 6140 and an AMD EPYC 7543, where its subsections, Table 3 and Fig. 2 give a Gold 6148 and an EPYC 7H12.

### 2.5 Processes, shared memory and MPI

#### 2.5.1 Distributed Python against MPI

DaCe distributes programs written for one node by transformations of their dataflow ([Ziogas et al.](#ziogas-et-al-2021) 2021, §4.1, §4.2). Element-wise operations on whole arrays are scattered from a root process and gathered again, matrix products are expanded into calls of the parallel BLAS, such as `pdgemm`, and a further transformation removes the gathers and scatters that fall between two distributed operations. On the multi-core partition of Piz Daint, with two Intel Xeon E5-2698v3 of 18 cores per node joined by the network Aries of Cray, the distributed kernels scale weakly from 1 to 1,296 processes, two per node with 18 threads each (§4.4, Fig. 12). Without communication, as in `doitgen`, the efficiency is close to perfect. The products of matrices and vectors keep more than 60 per cent beyond 64 processes, the products of matrices fall lower, as expected of ScaLAPACK in MKL, and the stencils `jacobi_1d` and `jacobi_2d` lie in between. The abstract and conclusion state efficiencies above 90 per cent on hundreds of nodes, up to 93.16 per cent on 512. Two distributed frameworks of Python fare worse. Dask ([Dask Development Team](#dask-2016) 2016) runs out of memory or performs unstably even at half the size of problem, and is more than 30 times slower than DaCe on `jacobi_1d`, which lets it overlap more communication with computation and so show a higher efficiency up to 16 processes. Legate ([Bauer and Garland](#bauer-garland-2019) 2019) matches DaCe on one CPU where BLAS dominates, runs 1.7 to 15 times slower elsewhere, and could be run only on part of the machine. DaCe communicates through the optimised MPI of Cray, Legate through the Legion runtime over GASNet, and Dask through TCP, which the authors take as the most significant, if not the only, cause of the differences.

#### 2.5.2 Processes against threads

The alternatives to threads that PEP 703 weighs all fall short ([Gross](#gross-2023) 2023, Alternatives). The module `multiprocessing` runs one interpreter, and so one GIL, per process, but objects must be serialised or copied into shared memory to pass between processes, which costs time and complicates interfaces. Starting a thread takes about 100 μs, while spawning a process takes about 50 ms, as Python must initialise anew, and many libraries in C and C++ support several threads but not several processes. Extensions that release the GIL around long functions, as many scientific libraries do, run concurrently, but acquiring and releasing the GIL usually stops them scaling beyond a few threads. Functions that parallelise internally, as in the NumPy of Intel, PyTorch and TensorFlow, work only where each operation is large enough, not for many small operations or operations that call Python. A GIL per interpreter, which PEP 684 introduced, allows parallelism between interpreters in one process, but restricts the sharing of data between them (Related Work).

#### 2.5.3 Threads within processes

OMP4Py combines with mpi4py into a hybrid of threads within each node and MPI between nodes, which PyOMP cannot do, as Numba cannot compile the calls of mpi4py ([Piñeiro and Pichel](#pineiro-pichel-2026) 2026, §4.3, Fig. 14). In a Jacobi iteration distributed by rows of the matrix, each process updates its part of the solution on 16 threads, `MPI_Allgather` shares the updated vector among the processes, and `MPI_Allreduce` gathers the global error for the test of convergence. On up to eight nodes of two Xeon Gold 5220R each, the time falls by factors of 1.84, 3.13 and 4.85 on 2, 4 and 8 nodes against one.

#### 2.5.4 MPI through mpi4py

The message passing interface (MPI), standardised since 1994 and implemented by open projects such as MPICH and Open MPI and by the vendors of HPC systems, remains the uncontested paradigm of large-scale computing ([Dalcin and Fang](#dalcin-fang-2021) 2021, [doi:10.1109/MCSE.2021.3083216](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCSE.2021.3083216)). MPI for Python, mpi4py, aims to expose as much of MPI as possible while staying close to its syntax and semantics, in the idioms of Python. Its module `MPI` offers the classes `Comm`, `Win` and `File`, for point-to-point and collective communication, one-sided operations and parallel input and output, in an object-oriented interface modelled on the former bindings of MPI for C++, and it targets complete implementations of MPI-3.1 while still working with those of MPI-1 and MPI-2. Generic Python objects travel through the protocol pickle, serialised before sending and restored after receiving, at a cost in processor time and memory that small objects can bear for the convenience. Objects like arrays travel through the buffer protocol of Python with negligible overhead, as their memory is handed to MPI directly, so that codes perform on a par with equivalents in C, C++ or Fortran; one-sided operations and parallel input and output rest on the same protocol. Release 1.0 of 2009 rewrote mpi4py in Cython, whose development its author joined, which made the code far easier to extend and maintain than the earlier mix of hand-written C and Python.

Release 2.0, of 2015, brought the features of MPI-3 ([Dalcin and Fang](#dalcin-fang-2021) 2021). Matched probes make the receipt of messages safe when several threads share a communicator, which mpi4py uses to receive pickled objects in a single message. Non-blocking collectives, such as `Ibcast` and `Iallgather`, overlap communication with computation. Neighbourhood collectives, `Neighbor_alltoall` and `Neighbor_allgather` with their non-blocking and variable-count variants, exchange data along the neighbours of a Cartesian or graph topology, the pattern of the stencils of solvers of partial differential equations. New one-sided operations include atomic ones, operations that return requests, and windows of shared memory created by `Win.Allocate_shared`. With an MPI implementation aware of CUDA, mpi4py passes arrays on GPUs through the CUDA Array Interface without copies to the host, up to 15 times faster for large transfers than staging them through the host on a workstation with two GPUs joined by NVLink (Fig. 1). The package `mpi4py.futures`, of 2017, runs tasks on a pool of processes through the interface of `concurrent.futures`, which sidesteps the GIL, either by spawning workers at run time or, where the implementation or the batch system forbids that, by splitting an existing communicator into a master and workers. For large objects, version 5 of pickle with buffers out of band, in `mpi4py.util.pkl5`, raises the throughput of arrays of 1 GiB by about 3.5 times and passes the limit of $2^{31} - 1$ elements per message (Fig. 2). Libraries built on mpi4py include h5py for parallel HDF5, VTK, yt, Dask through dask_mpi, and mpi4py-fft for distributed fast Fourier transforms. The authors plan to drop Python 2, to keep providing prebuilt binary packages for easier installation, and to support MPI-4.

**[GK]** The present documentation of mpi4py lists binary wheels on the Python Package Index for Linux, macOS and Windows, and the installation of an implementation of MPI from the same index in one command, as `python -m pip install mpi4py mpich` or `mpi4py openmpi` on Linux and macOS ([mpi4py documentation](#web-mpi4py-install)).

#### 2.5.5 Neighbourhood collectives against all-to-all

Manapy exchanges the values of its halo cells at every iteration of a three-dimensional model of a streamer discharge on 29.9 million cells, a set of thirteen quantities per cell, among them the densities of electrons and ions, the potential, the electric field and the gradients ([Kissami and Ratnani](#kissami-ratnani-2022) 2022, [arXiv:2203.00925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00925), §4.1, Table 10). With the collective `MPI_Alltoallv`, which involves every process, the time of one exchange falls from 0.67 s on 128 cores to 0.11 s on 512 and 1024 and then rises again, to 3.0 s on 16,384. With `MPI_Neighbor_alltoallv`, which sends data only to the neighbouring subdomains, it keeps falling, to 2.5 ms on 16,384 cores, and its non-blocking variant performs alike.

### 2.6 The decomposition into blocks and the exchange of ghost cells

#### 2.6.1 An explicit exchange of halos in data-centric Python

A stencil written on global arrays, such as the Jacobi iteration in two dimensions, would make DaCe scatter and gather the arrays at every step, so that the user can instead write the communication explicitly on local views ([Ziogas et al.](#ziogas-et-al-2021) 2021, §4.3).

1. Allocate on each process local arrays of $(l_x + 2) \times (l_y + 2)$ cells, a block of the global array with one layer of ghost cells on every side.
2. Scatter the global arrays into the interiors of the local blocks once, in blocks of a two-dimensional grid of processes.
3. In each half step, post non-blocking sends of the edges of the interior and receives into the ghost layers for the four neighbours, wait for all of them, and update the interior, shifting the limits by one cell where a block lies on the boundary of the domain.
4. Gather the interiors into the global arrays at the end.

The pattern resembles one written with mpi4py, but has two advantages: the calls of MPI enter the dataflow, so that transformations such as the overlap of communication with computation apply to them, and the strided columns of a block are sent through derived vector types of MPI without copies. Their types are created once, which is valid because the sizes are symbols that do not change during the run, and DaCe warns where they might.

#### 2.6.2 Ghost zones exchanged through queues

In the model of [Diehl et al.](#diehl-et-al-2024) (2024, §2), each thread applies the update sequentially to its own segment of the grid, and the ghost values pass between neighbouring segments through queues. Each queue has a single producer and a single consumer and so needs no synchronisation in principle, although synchronising the suspension and resumption of threads helped in some cases. The pattern is shared by many codes on block-structured meshes, and each language realises it with its own means (§3). The version in C++17 forks and joins threads of `std::thread` around a queue of its own, built on `std::mutex` and `std::condition_variable`. HPX replaces these by its light-weight threads and channels, Go by goroutines and buffered channels, and Rust by scoped threads and channels, while Charm++ decomposes the grid into an array of chares that pass their ghost values as messages.

#### 2.6.3 Ghost cells through PETSc in PetClaw

Clawpack and SharpClaw impose boundary conditions through layers of ghost cells, filled at the start of each step, after which the scheme updates the interior cells, which suits parallelisation ([Ketcheson et al.](#ketcheson-et-al-2012) 2012, §4). The serial kernels can run unchanged on each process once a routine has filled its ghost cells from the boundary conditions or from its neighbours, and only that routine needs to know the global topology. This separation lets numerical schemes and patterns of communication be developed independently. PETSc decomposes the same way, through its object DMDA for distributed structured grids, which allocates the storage and communicates the ghost values, and petsc4py returns the storage to PyClaw as NumPy arrays (Fig. 4.1). Each process extracts a local vector with an overlap of ghost unknowns, applies the serial kernels, and returns the result to a global vector, while PETSc distinguishes a natural ordering of the unknowns for output, a global ordering contiguous on each process for its solvers, and a local ordering that includes the ghost unknowns. A further single call of PETSc reduces the maximum wave speed over all processes for the condition on the time step. The parallel extension, PetClaw, takes about 300 lines of Python, and any script runs in parallel when `from clawpack import pyclaw` is replaced by `from clawpack import petclaw as pyclaw` and the script is started with `mpirun`. The user is shielded from PETSc as a user of PETSc is shielded from MPI, and PETSc could later supply implicit solvers or unstructured meshes without changes to PyClaw.

The weak scaling of PetClaw on the IBM BlueGene/P Shaheen, of 65,536 cores, holds 160,000 cells per core, $400 \times 400$, for a fixed number of steps of the interaction of a shock with a bubble (2.7.2), from 1 core to the whole machine ([Ketcheson et al.](#ketcheson-et-al-2012) 2012, §5.2, Fig. 5.1). The serial computations take 88 to 93 s per core in every run, more than 90 per cent of the time, the reduction for the time step about 4 s from 16 cores on, and the exchange of ghost values about 6 s in all. The efficiency is 0.97 from 16 to 4096 cores and 0.92 on 65,536, where the parallel initialisation, a cost paid once, grows to 6.6 s. Loading the libraries of Python at start-up, which every process does from the same files at once, scales poorly on the parallel file system and is excluded from these times, but takes less than 5 per cent of the runs of the paper (§5.3). As most parallel debuggers support only C or Fortran, debugging parallel Python is hard, a further reason to rely on a tested library such as PETSc (§4).

#### 2.6.4 Partitions with halo cells in Manapy

Manapy solves partial differential equations by finite volumes on unstructured grids of two and three dimensions, in Python, with all its functions accelerated either by Numba or by Pyccel, which generates C or Fortran from Python ([Kissami and Ratnani](#kissami-ratnani-2022) 2022, §1). Its parallelism rests on MPI, with the mesh partitioned by METIS or ParMETIS. For a convection–diffusion equation, the convective fluxes are upwind with a MUSCL reconstruction of van Leer's type and the limiter of Barth and Jespersen, the gradients in the cells come from least squares over all cells that share a node, the diffusive fluxes from a diamond scheme, and the solution advances by a Runge–Kutta method of three stages (§2.1). Every subdomain therefore needs, besides its own cells, ghost cells at the physical boundary, halo cells from the neighbouring subdomains, and halo-ghost cells, the ghost cells of its neighbours, and its operators loop over all four kinds in turn (Fig. 1, Algorithms 1 and 2). At each iteration the halo cells receive the unknowns, the velocity and the gradients.

Three grids, of 1.06, 15.5 and 29.9 million cells, test the strong scaling of the operators for the gradients in cells and on faces and the interpolation by least squares on the cluster Toubkal, whose nodes hold two Intel Xeon Platinum 8276 of 28 cores at 2.2 GHz joined by HDR InfiniBand (§3.1 to §3.3, Tables 1 to 5). On 32,768 cores the partition with the most cells of the largest grid holds 825 cells of its own and 2,218 halo cells from 23 neighbours (Table 2). The scaling owes its quality to two effects: smaller partitions fit better into the caches, and the computation per core shrinks, while the halo cells, the surface of the partitions, eventually dominate. The smallest grid therefore stops scaling at 4096 cores, with about 500 cells per core on 2048 cores and more halo cells than own cells, while on the largest grid the speed-up of the gradient in cells exceeds the ideal about threefold through the caches.

#### 2.6.5 The faces of a cubed sphere

Pace runs in parallel through MPI, one rank per node in its measurements, or with a serial communicator that runs each rank in turn and saves data files in place of the communication, which serves testing ([Dahm et al.](#dahm-et-al-2023) 2023, §3, §5). The cubed sphere is decomposed by its six faces, each further divided among ranks. Near the edges and corners of the faces FV3 applies special stencils for the geometry of the grid, which on six ranks, one per face, every rank must compute, while on more ranks only some do (§5, §5.3). Tests on 54 ranks cover every case, from a rank with no edge or corner to one with all eight (§4.2). FV3 parallelises over the vertical, which suits CPUs with prediction of branches and large caches, while GPUs need the horizontal plane, where the special stencils at edges and corners cannot run with the rest and break larger kernels into separate launches. A grid duplicated at the edges of the faces could avoid this, which shows how a choice of algorithm made for one architecture can hold back another.

### 2.7 Whole codes for fluids against compiled codes

#### 2.7.1 AGATE against Athena++ and Cholla

The same benchmark runs in Athena++ ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020) on the CPUs and in Cholla ([Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2015) 2015; [Caddy and Schneider](#caddy-schneider-2024) 2024) on the GPU, each built as its repository instructs ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §4.7, Table 2). Athena++ runs on one node, once without OpenMP and once with 16 threads, for which the mesh blocks are sized so that each thread has one. Cholla requires three dimensions, so it runs on $N \times N \times 3$ cells, and the Kernel mode of AGATE is timed on the same grid. Athena++ is considerably better optimised for CPUs than AGATE, which the authors expect, since they focus on GPUs. On $1024^2$ cells, Athena++ takes 1396 s without threads, about as long as Numba on 16 threads, and 214 s with them, against 7477 s for NumPy. On the GPU, Cholla runs 10 to 15 per cent faster than AGATE on the largest grids and about as fast on smaller ones, a difference that the authors accept for the advantages of Python. AGATE needs no compilation beyond the 13 s of Numba and well under a second on the GPU, where Athena++ and Cholla took 81 s and 119 s to compile. The authors find AGATE suited above all to rapid deployment, to students and to prototyping on smaller problems, and to HPC through its GPU modes.

**[GK]** The times of Athena++ without threads grow by a factor of 7.9 from $512^2$ to $1024^2$ cells, as the work does, and exceed those with 16 threads by a factor of 6.5, which suggests that it ran on one core. On $1024^2$ cells, NumPy is then 5.4 times slower than Athena++ on one core, and Numba on 16 threads 6.2 times slower than Athena++ on 16 threads.

#### 2.7.2 PyClaw against Clawpack

PyClaw and Clawpack run nearly the same kernels of Fortran, which differ only in the layout of their arrays, so that the cost of the framework in Python shows in their difference on one core ([Ketcheson et al.](#ketcheson-et-al-2012) 2012, §5.1, Table 5.1). Linear acoustics in two dimensions, whose Riemann solver is a product of a $3 \times 3$ matrix with a vector, bounds the overhead from above: PyClaw takes 41 s against 28 s for Clawpack on an Intel Xeon, a ratio of 1.5, and 316 s against 192 s on a PowerPC 450 of the BlueGene/P, a ratio of 1.6. The shallow-water equations, with a Roe solver and an entropy fix, represent realistic nonlinear problems: 99 s against 79 s on the Xeon, a ratio of 1.3, and 800 s against 714 s on the PowerPC, 1.1. The authors judge an increase of 10 to 30 per cent in time for realistic applications well worth the advantages of Python, above all its easy parallelism, and expect it to shrink for more complex systems and in three dimensions. They conclude that the small serial loss is more than compensated, even on a laptop, by running in parallel without further effort (§8). The applications include the interaction of a planar shock with a bubble of light gas in an axisymmetric domain of $[0, 2] \times [0, 0.5]$, with $\gamma = 1.4$, a shock of pressure ratio 5 and a bubble of a tenth of the ambient density, on $1280 \times 320$ cells with the unsplit algorithm of Clawpack and with SharpClaw at fifth and seventh order, which resolves more fine structure in the ring of vorticity that the bubble becomes (§7.2, Figs. 7.2 and 7.3). The paper gives the sizes of the problems of Table 5.1 neither here nor for the steps of the weak scaling, so that its times cannot be converted into cell updates per second.

#### 2.7.3 Pace against Fortran on CPU and GPU

On the Cray XC50 Piz Daint, whose nodes hold a 12-core Intel Xeon E5-2690 v3 and an NVIDIA Tesla P100, Pace runs one rank of MPI per node on either the CPU or the GPU, while the Fortran reference runs in its best configuration of six ranks per node with four threads each ([Dahm et al.](#dahm-et-al-2023) 2023, §5, Table 1). On six nodes, one per face, with faces of $108 \times 108$, $128 \times 128$ and $192 \times 192$ columns of 80 levels, global grids of 96, 72 and 48 km, one call of the dynamical core takes 3.58, 4.66 and 12.74 s in Fortran, 16.00, 22.25 and 48.07 s for Pace on the CPU, and 1.98, 2.34 and 3.98 s for Pace on the GPU. On the CPU, Pace thus runs at 0.21 to 0.27 times the speed of Fortran, which the authors ascribe to the high optimisation of the Fortran for CPUs and to their own focus on GPUs, and they report that work on the CPU has begun. On the GPU its advantage grows with the work per node, to 3.2 on the largest faces, as GPUs need enough work to pay for their start-up (Fig. 9). In weak scaling with $192 \times 192 \times 79$ points per GPU, up to 864 nodes and a grid of about 4 km, Pace keeps a speed-up of about 3.6 over Fortran at all scales, and the conclusions state a speed-up of about 3.3 on the GPU (Fig. 8, §8). The microphysics takes about 1 per cent of the time. **[GK]** One call of the dynamical core takes many sub-steps of acoustics, tracer advection and vertical remapping (Fig. 3), whose number the paper does not give, so that its times cannot be converted into cell updates per second.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture records how the codes divide the work between Python and their kernels, lay out their data, choose their physics and are installed. 3.1 digests AGATE, 3.2 the NumPy array, 3.3 Cython, 3.4 Numba, 3.5 Pythran, 3.6 PyExaFMM, 3.7 PyClaw, 3.8 Manapy and 3.9 Pace.

### 3.1 AGATE: interfaces and implementations

AGATE builds its solver from Python classes, each step of the solution an interface paired with implementations ([Bard and Dorelli](#bard-dorelli-2026) 2026, §2.1, Fig. 1). The interfaces manage the operations of high level and shield the main program from the numerical detail, which the implementations carry out. A user may select or write any implementation that meets its interface. The code solves $\partial U/\partial t = \Delta(U, t)$, where $\Delta$ is the instantaneous rate of change of the state vector $U$, through the following classes.

- `Grid` holds the locations, sizes and connections of the cells. Only a uniform Cartesian grid exists so far.
- `Vector` stores $U$ on the grid, and `State` extends it for MHD with references to the single variables and storage for quantities outside the state, such as the ratio of specific heats.
- `Evolver` advances the state in time, by Runge–Kutta schemes from RK11, of one stage and first order, and RK22 to the strong-stability-preserving RK33, RK43 and RK53. It computes $\Delta$ through the interface `Scheme`, whose implementations are ideal, Hall and CGL MHD.
- `Scenario` sets the initial conditions and boundaries of a problem and creates the matching `State` on a `Grid`.
- `Hacker` runs tasks of the user when conditions are met during a run, from analysis on the fly to changes of the run. The test of reconnection (§4.6) uses one to compute the rate of reconnection without output files.
- `Roller` drives the loop in time from $t_0$ to $t_1$. Its functions of automatic initialisation build all classes of the algorithm from defaults and options, while every component can also be set by hand.

The default scheme is a Godunov-type finite-volume method in the form of eq. 1, with a global time step $\Delta t = C\,\Delta x_\mathrm{min}/v_\mathrm{max}$, whose Courant number defaults to 0.8, 0.45 and 0.325 in one, two and three dimensions (§2.2). Its steps are interfaces of their own: `Reconstructor`, `Speeder` for the wave speeds, `Fluxor` for the physical fluxes, `Riemann`, and `Sourceror` for the source terms. By default the conserved variables are reconstructed linearly with the monotonised central limiter, $\Delta U_i = \mathrm{minmod}[\beta(U_{i+1} - U_i), \beta(U_i - U_{i-1}), (U_{i+1} - U_{i-1})/2]$ with $\beta = 1.3$, and the HLL solver uses the signal speeds $s_\mathrm{L} = \min(v_\mathrm{L} - c_\mathrm{L}, v_\mathrm{R} - c_\mathrm{R})$ and $s_\mathrm{R} = \max(v_\mathrm{L} + c_\mathrm{L}, v_\mathrm{R} + c_\mathrm{R})$ (eqs. 2 to 6). Errors in the divergence of the magnetic field are cleaned by the generalised Lagrange multiplier of [Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2002) (2002), with $c_h$ at 95 per cent of the largest local wave speed and $c_p^2/c_h = 0.18$ (§3.1). The modes of acceleration enter below the interfaces (2.2.1), so that physics and algorithm, chosen by composing objects at run time, are chosen independently of the hardware. The tests reproduce the shock tube of Brio and Wu, the Orszag–Tang vortex, whistler waves with second-order convergence, magnetosonic waves and the firehose instability in CGL MHD, and the reconnection challenge of the Geospace Environmental Modeling (GEM) project (§4.1 to §4.6). The paper names NumPy, Numba, CuPy and Matplotlib as the software that it uses (acknowledgements), but does not describe how AGATE is installed.

### 3.2 NumPy: memory shared with other code

The NumPy array shares memory with other code without copying it ([van der Walt, Colbert and Varoquaux](#van-der-walt-et-al-2011) 2011). A memory-mapped array addresses an array stored on disk through the interface of any other array, so that a small part of a very large file can be read or changed, and the changes are written back by flushing. Memory allocated by foreign code, such as a library in C++ or Fortran, becomes an array through the array interface, an attribute `__array_interface__` that holds a dictionary with the address of the data, the shape and the type of the elements. An example exposes a buffer allocated through ctypes and modifies it through NumPy. Structured data types combine fields of different types in one element, such as a time stamp of 64 bits and a position of two floating-point numbers, whose fields are then addressed by name, and they read the records of a binary file in one line. **[GK]** A structured array keeps the fields of each element together in memory, the layout in which topic 32 has the variables of a cell lie together, whereas an array per variable, or the variable on the first axis, keeps each field contiguous.

### 3.3 Cython: building and distributing compiled modules

Cython generates C, which a C compiler builds into an extension module ([Behnel et al.](#behnel-et-al-2011) 2011). Projects mostly mix modules of Python and of Cython, since the modules of Cython need not be recompiled when the Python changes. To avoid requiring Cython as a dependency, many projects ship the generated C files, which compile unchanged against Python 2.3 to 3.2 when the package is set up by distutils. Fwrap can likewise generate a deployable package for machines without Fwrap or Cython. Wrappers written directly in the Python/C API, with its steep learning curve, have drawn a sharp line between users in Python and developers in C, which Cython lowers. The extent of such coupling shows in SciPy, whose more than 200,000 lines of C++, 60,000 of C and 75,000 of Fortran faced about 70,000 lines of Python.

### 3.4 Numba: a compiler built on LLVM

LLVM gives Numba a back end of high quality with a ready compiler at run time, so that its developers could concentrate on the front end without deep knowledge of the instruction sets, and once the back end for CPUs worked, those for CUDA and HSA followed easily, sharing most of its logic ([Lam, Pitrou and Seibert](#lam-et-al-2015) 2015, §5). The interface of LLVM in C++ changes often, so Numba replaced its binding llvmpy, which mirrored that interface, by llvmlite, which uses the more stable interface of LLVM in C, with wrappers in C where needed, and builds the intermediate representation of LLVM as text in pure Python (§5.1). Errors inside LLVM are handled inconsistently, some ignored until a crash, others reported as codes or as failed assertions that stop the process, none of them as Python exceptions (§5.2). As LLVM leaves the binary interface to the front end, Numba passes only scalars and pointers between compiled functions, flattening every aggregate, and returns an error code, with the actual result passed through the first argument, in the style of the C API of Python (§5.3). The compiler MCJIT of LLVM forces each function into a module of its own, finalised at once, which complicates mutual recursion and inlining across modules and duplicates functions in memory (§5.4). Caching compiled code between runs needs metadata, such as a time stamp and a version, and Python objects akin to a closure beside the object code, which the callbacks of MCJIT do not carry, so that Numba keeps the object code itself and saves or loads it where convenient (§5.5).

**[GK]** By contrast with Numba (2.2.3), NumPy 2.5.4 and mpi4py 4.1.2 publish wheels for macOS on Intel processors for Python 3.14 in both builds, and Cython 3.3.1 and Pythran 0.19.0 publish wheels in pure Python, which need only a compiler of C or C++ on the machine to build modules ([PyPI](#web-pypi-json)).

### 3.5 Pythran: modules that stay Python

Pythran turns a module, not a whole program, into a native module, so that the costly parts of a program are moved into modules of their own and compiled ([Guelton et al.](#guelton-et-al-2015) 2015, §2). The generated C++ can also be used from applications in C++. Pythran extends distutils with a class `PythranExtension`, used like any extension, and since its input is Python, a `setup.py` can fall back to the module in pure Python where Pythran is not available (§8). Pythran ran on Linux and Mac OS X and was distributed through its repository and the Python Package Index. **[GK]** SciPy builds some of its modules with Pythran, which brings along the library xsimd for vector instructions ([build file of SciPy 1.16.3](#web-scipy-meson)).

### 3.6 PyExaFMM: a data-oriented design around Numba

Data-oriented design favours data structures with simple layouts in memory, such as arrays, which make locality of the caches and parallelisation easier to achieve than objects that bundle attributes of several types ([Kailasa et al.](#kailasa-et-al-2022) 2022, p. 6). Numba centres on arrays, and PyExaFMM follows it. Its octree is linear rather than built of pointers: each node is a unique identifier in a one-dimensional vector, and all other data, the coefficients of the expansions, the particles and the potentials, lie in further one-dimensional vectors, reached through indices that tie each identifier to its data. The authors note that Numba thus shaped their design and made the software more complex, although its data structures became simpler. A single Python object, `Fmm`, forms the interface (Fig. 2). It allocates the arrays of expansions and potentials, and its methods call the compiled functions of the operators and of their data, while trees and precomputed quantities are kept in a database in HDF5. The operators stay separate functions, so that each can be tested on its own, at the price of at least one passage between Numba and Python per call of an operator. A single compiled routine that returned to Python only once would be faster, but would give up modularity and unit tests. This structure parallels the classic design of a Python interface over a compiled language, with the difference that all of it is written in Python, although performant code for Numba may be Pythonic only in its syntax.

### 3.7 PyClaw: solvers, solutions and states

PyClaw is a framework of objects in which the user supplies callbacks, such as a Riemann solver, that describe the problem, while PyClaw manages the main routine, the steps in time and the output, without strictly formatted data files ([Ketcheson et al.](#ketcheson-et-al-2012) 2012, §3). Its two main abstractions are the `Solver` and the `Solution` (§3.2, Fig. 3.1). A solver advances a state in three parts: it sets the ghost cells from the boundary conditions, advances the hyperbolic terms, and advances any source terms by fractional steps. Subclasses implement these for each method, abstract enough for both the classic algorithm of Clawpack and the method of lines of SharpClaw, and a new method enters by implementing a single step in time, which the paper names `homogenous_step`, a routine that legacy codes often provide already. A solution describes the domain, a tensor product of uniform one-dimensional grids that may be mapped to a physical domain, and holds the state: one or more grids, each with states that keep the conserved quantities `q` and optional auxiliary fields `aux` in NumPy arrays. This hierarchy lets data and algorithms change without changing the interface, as the distributed state of PetClaw shows (2.6.3). A short script sets up, runs and plots a problem, for instance a two-dimensional Riemann problem of the Euler equations in about twenty lines (Listing 2), and the same scripts run interactively, in serial and in parallel, and in batch queues (§3.1).

PyClaw installs with `pip install numpy` and `pip install clawpack`, and its parallel extension is enabled whenever petsc4py is present, which is not an explicit dependency (§1, Listing 1). Every major application has a regression test, whose script, parameters and verified output lie in the repository and, where possible, are checked against another solver or an analysis, and which give the same output for any solver, kernel or architecture (§6.1). The authors find these tests the greatest single factor in its adoption, refactoring and extension. Development uses git hosted on GitHub, with a mailing list, and the documentation is generated by Sphinx from text and from the docstrings of the code (§6.2, §6.3). PyClaw did not yet include adaptive refinement, which other extensions of Clawpack provide, nor three dimensions or implicit stepping, which were in testing (§3, §8).

### 3.8 Manapy: a framework of finite volumes for students and production

Manapy aims to be a code that is easy to understand and to modify, for students, researchers and industrial users, with the discretisations of the popular solvers of computational fluid dynamics ([Kissami and Ratnani](#kissami-ratnani-2022) 2022, §1, §5). Its module `ddm` reads a mesh from Gmsh, partitions it and builds the local domains, and its module `ast` offers variables with their boundary conditions and linear systems, so that the Poisson equation in three dimensions is set up, assembled, solved and compared with its exact solution in a short script (§6). The linear systems are solved directly by MUMPS, through pymumps, or iteratively by PETSc, through petsc4py, with several preconditioners, and the output goes to Matplotlib in two dimensions and to ParaView in three (§1). Each process assembles its own part of the matrix of the Poisson equation, up to 2.2 billion non-zero elements for the largest grid, in times that fall faster than the number of cores up to 16,384 (§3.4.1, Tables 6 and 7). The direct solver is faster on few cores and the iterative solvers scale better on many, PETSc with flexible GMRES and algebraic multigrid best of all, while MUMPS fails on the larger matrices through what the authors call an overflow of 64-bit doubles (§3.4.2, Tables 8 and 9). In the model of a streamer discharge, which couples the convection and diffusion of electrons to the Poisson equation for the electric potential, the solution of the Poisson equation takes most of the time, and all parts of the simulation scale to 8192 cores (§4, Table 11, Fig. 11). Models of shallow water, shallow-water MHD, streamers and incompressible flow by the PISO algorithm have been built on Manapy with students (§1), and its authors plan parallel adaptive refinement and the automatic generation of code from abstract forms (§5).

### 3.9 Pace: components compiled at initialisation

Pace consists of separate packages for the dynamics, the microphysics, the utilities and the integration of the language, and its dynamics form a hierarchy of components with clear boundaries ([Dahm et al.](#dahm-et-al-2023) 2023, §3.1, Fig. 3). The horizontal transport of FV3, for instance, extends any one-dimensional reconstruction that meets certain numerical conditions to two dimensions, so that a scientist can change the dynamics by replacing only the modules of the one-dimensional piecewise parabolic method by another reconstruction. Nearly every computational component is a class with an initialisation method, which allocates its memory, compiles or loads its stencils and initialises its subcomponents, and a call method, after which the component is called like any function, much as Fortran pairs computation with routines to initialise and finalise (Listing 2). A factory of stencils keeps the logic of compilation out of the components and passes the constants of the configuration and of the decomposition to the compiler as externals, which also eases debugging, since a check or an output can be inserted for every stencil by changing the factory alone. The introspection of Python serves testing (§3.2). One test replaces the allocation routines of GT4Py by routines that raise an error after initialisation, so that no array is allocated during a call, and another calls the dynamical core once and twice on the same state and compares all temporary arrays, which finds any component that depends on stale data. The port proceeded by small units, each fed with the inputs and checked against the outputs of the corresponding Fortran, saved by the library Serialbox, to a relative difference of $10^{-14}$ by default, before larger components were assembled and validated (§4, §4.2, Fig. 4). The authors find that Python made Pace modular and productive and gave it powerful tools for testing, and that the approach can serve to port other models of weather and climate (§8).
