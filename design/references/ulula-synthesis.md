# Synthesis of Ulula

This synthesis digests Ulula, a hydrodynamics code for two dimensions written in Python, in its release 1.0.2, kept with its documentation as `reference-works/Ulula/Code`. The code paper completes the sources, kept as `reference-works/Ulula/Docs/2505.20399v1.pdf`: the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025) ([Diemer](#diemer-2025) 2025, [arXiv:2505.20399v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20399v1)). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in these sources or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. Code is cited by its path in the repository and the name of a function or class, as in (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `Simulation.riemannSolverHLLC`), and a page of the documentation by its path. A section sign without a named source refers to the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025).

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin, version and licence

Ulula is a hydrodynamics code meant for teaching and for numerical experiments, by Benedikt Diemer of the University of Maryland (`README.md`). It was developed alongside ASTR 670, a graduate course on hydrodynamics taught there, whose students tried it out in class (acknowledgements of the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025)). The name plays on that of Euler, which contains Eule, the German word for owl. Ulula is Latin for owl, and Latin is the language of most of Euler's papers (`docs/source/index.rst`).

Version 0.1.0 of 24 March 2021 held the hydrodynamic solver and its examples (`docs/source/versions.rst`). Version 0.1.1 reorganised the code and added the Sedov–Taylor explosion and plots of radial averages in one dimension. Version 0.2.0 saved simulations to files in HDF5, the Hierarchical Data Format, which serve both as snapshots and for restarts, and kept the primitive and conserved fluid quantities in dictionaries. Version 0.2.1 reworked the runtime interface, so that files, plots and movies can follow evenly spaced times, all at once and without altering the run, and 0.2.2 checks that a restart runs to a later time than that of its file. Nearly four years later, 0.3.0 of January 2025 brought gravity, by a fixed acceleration or a fixed potential that the user sets, reflecting walls as boundaries, code units with plots in any system of units, and the handling of violations of the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition. It also added automatic checks of conservation and setups for cloud crushing, free fall, the Rayleigh–Taylor instability and tidal disruption. Version 0.4.0 made simulations in one dimension proper ones and added the isothermal equation of state, updates and boundary conditions that the user defines for the run, and setups for an atmosphere and a sound wave. It replaced global index variables by indexing that adapts to the variables in use. Version 0.4.1 added the HLLC Riemann solver, the Gresho vortex, advection in one dimension and options for plots, among them temperatures in kelvin and radial and azimuthal velocities. Version 0.5.0 of 7 May 2025 brought self-gravity from the Poisson equation for periodic boundaries, setups for the Jeans instability and a Keplerian disk, an atmosphere of varying temperature, and much of the documentation. Version 1.0.0 of 13 May 2025 made its u/v notation consistent across code and documentation. Version 1.0.1 of 26 May added the merger setup and plots in one dimension with panels stacked vertically, and 1.0.2 of 19 November 2025 moved the build to `pyproject.toml`, a change by Miha Cernetic.

The code is kept at [bitbucket.org/bdiemer/ulula](https://bitbucket.org/bdiemer/ulula), and its documentation is published at [bdiemer.bitbucket.io/ulula](https://bdiemer.bitbucket.io/ulula) (`README.md`; §1). **[GK]** PyPI, the Python Package Index, holds every release from 0.1.0 to 1.0.2, among them 0.3.1 and 0.3.2 of 8 January 2025, which the history does not list, and its dates fall on the day that the history gives or the day after. The repository has no tags. The head of its branch master is commit 946dfb1 of 19 November 2025, which carries the version 1.0.2. Between 1.0.1 and 1.0.2 its commits also dropped the support of Python 2, with Python 3.6 as the lowest version, and updated the instructions for a developer installation. The local copy agrees in content with the source distribution of 1.0.2 on PyPI in its 30 Python files, `pyproject.toml`, `README.rst` and `LICENSE.txt`, and with commit 946dfb1 in the eleven pages of `docs/source/`. Its other files, `README.md`, the makefile of the documentation and the images, agree with that commit in size, and it lacks only the file `.gitignore` of the repository. The published documentation states no version.

Ulula is distributed under the MIT licence, with the copyright for 2020 to 2025 held by Diemer (`LICENSE.txt`). The README presents the code as being tested and offers no warranty. The version history credits Calvin Osinga with the code for movies in the GIF format and Philip Mansfield with the computation of overlaps between annuli and squares in radial plots. It names Gandalf, the code of [Hubber et al.](#hubber-et-al-2018) (2018, [doi:10.1093/mnras/stx2405](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stx2405)), as the origin of the exact solution of the Sedov–Taylor explosion. The [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025), by Diemer alone, adds that the exact solution of the shock tube follows the lecture notes of Frank [van den Bosch](#van-den-bosch) and Susanne [Höfner](#hofner), that Philip Mocz commented on a draft, and that the National Science Foundation, under grant 2338388, and the Sloan Foundation supported the work (acknowledgements). **[GK]** arXiv holds only the first version of the paper, of 26 May 2025, without a reference to a journal.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

The [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025) explains the need for such a code (§1). Gravity and hydrodynamics are arguably the dominant physical processes in the Universe, and hydrodynamics governs systems from gas planets and stars to the interstellar and intergalactic media, clusters of galaxies and the early Universe, yet physics curricula for undergraduates often leave it out. Few of its problems have analytical solutions, sound waves and hydrostatic atmospheres among them, so that research depends on numerical solutions, which teaching on paper does not convey. Simple methods for partial differential equations fail. Finite differences are quickly taught, but they neither keep the solution positive nor conserve mass, and the discontinuities of the non-linear Euler equations resisted a long search for solvers, until finite-volume schemes brought the first real success ([Godunov](#godunov-1959) 1959). How such solvers work is hard to convey without experiments. Writing one from the start takes too long for a course, while production codes bury the equations in code for adaptive grids, parallelisation, input and output, and further physics. The need to compile them from C or Fortran can also rule them out for homework.

Ulula is the paper's answer: a small code for hydrodynamics and gravity in two dimensions, written to be brief and legible, which still offers fast algorithms of the kind used in research (§1). The whole solver fits into one Python file of fewer than 800 active lines. It holds a MUSCL–Hancock scheme whose Riemann solvers, reconstruction schemes, boundary conditions and equations of state can be exchanged (abstract). Two dimensions balance simplicity against performance. One dimension soon becomes dull, since many problems of hydrodynamics are multi-dimensional by nature, while problems in three dimensions take too long without parallelisation. For simplicity Ulula uses only Python and NumPy, with no further library to install or compile, JAX and Numba for instance. Extensive online documentation and heavy comments serve its teaching. Two lines of code suffice to run a prepared setup, to try other solvers and to make plots and movies. The documentation names the components that the brevity makes easy to find, exchange and modify: spatial interpolation, slope limiter, Riemann solver and time stepping (`docs/source/index.rst`). **[GK]** Counted without blank lines, comments and docstrings, `ulula/core/simulation.py` has 783 lines at release 1.0.2.

Ulula is not meant for scientific production runs, as it runs on one processor and on a grid of fixed size (`docs/source/index.rst`). Since NumPy carries out all its operations on arrays, it is still fairly fast on one processor. Many of its test problems take under a minute on a current laptop, and the abstract speaks of a few minutes.

Ulula is not the first code in Python for teaching hydrodynamics (§1). pyro ([Zingale](#zingale-2014) 2014; [Harpole et al.](#harpole-et-al-2019) 2019), digested in `pyro-synthesis.md`, has a similar aim and comes with an open-source text on numerical hydrodynamics ([Zingale](#zingale-2021) 2021), digested in `computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md`. The two codes are built differently. Ulula is one framework of interchangeable components, while pyro holds many solvers for particular problems, such as advection, relaxation by multigrid, diffusion and incompressible flow, so that its code is many times larger. pyro also speeds up some routines by compiling them just in time, where Ulula relies on NumPy alone. Shorter and simpler still are single scripts for one problem, such as the "Create your own" series of [Mocz](#mocz), which solves problems like the Rayleigh–Taylor instability in as few lines as possible. At the other end of the range, compiled libraries have made Python fast enough for production codes such as PySPH ([Ramachandran et al.](#ramachandran-et-al-2019) 2019) and Dedalus ([Burns et al.](#burns-et-al-2020) 2020), which serve research and far exceed Ulula in size and complexity. **[GK]** The synthesis of Pyro bears out both differences. Since version 3.0, pyro compiles its kernels with Numba at run time, and its Python without the tests comes to about 20,000 lines in the sizes that `plan.md` gives for its steps, three times the 6,700 lines of Ulula.

The paper names the limits of the code (§5). Ulula is of second order, knows only Cartesian grids in one and two dimensions, runs on one processor, and has neither viscosity nor radiative cooling. Physics that behaves differently in three dimensions is left out on purpose. Most interesting problems of magnetohydrodynamics are three-dimensional, and turbulence differs in kind in three dimensions, so that no setup of turbulence is included. The paper concludes that a framework of fewer than 800 active lines need not give up the fidelity of its algorithms or their speed, which the operations of NumPy on one processor limit. It sees Ulula as a platform for quick experiments with a low threshold, rather than as a monolithic solver.

## 2. Numerical methods

Eight subjects group the numerical methods by component. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

Ulula solves its equations with a Godunov scheme whose components can be exchanged (§3; Table 1). Two Riemann solvers compute the fluxes between cells: HLL, which ignores contact discontinuities, and HLLC, which restores them (2.4). The states on either side of an interface come either from constant data in each cell, which gives first order in space, or from a linear reconstruction of second order (2.5). Its slope is limited by the minmod limiter, the most cautious, by the limiter of van Leer, or by the monotonised central (MC) limiter, the most aggressive. In time, the states are either taken as they are, which the paper calls the Euler integrator, of first order and unstable, or advanced by half a time step in the Hancock step, of second order (2.6). The Hancock step works in conserved or in primitive variables, and the latter is faster. The class `HydroScheme` names these choices by identifiers: `const` or `linear` for the reconstruction, `none`, `minmod`, `vanleer` or `mc` for the limiter, `hll` or `hllc` for the Riemann solver, and `euler`, `hancock` or `hancock_cons` for the time integration (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `HydroScheme`; `docs/source/simulation.rst`). Its defaults, linear reconstruction with the MC limiter, the HLLC solver and the Hancock step in primitive variables, together form the MUSCL–Hancock scheme. The limiter `none` takes the central slope without limiting and gives a highly unstable scheme, while constant reconstruction is highly diffusive, and both serve only as demonstrations. HLLC cannot be combined with an isothermal gas, which has no contact discontinuities. Otherwise the code accepts any combination, unstable ones such as constant states with the Hancock step included, since experiments with failing algorithms are part of its purpose.

### 2.1 The equations and equations of state

#### 2.1.1 The Euler equations with gravity

Ulula solves the Euler equations of a compressible, inviscid fluid under gravity, written as conservation laws of mass, momentum and total energy (§2, eq. 1). At a fixed point, the density of each conserved quantity falls where its flux diverges and rises where it converges, and sources add to it (`docs/source/simulation.rst`). With the density $\rho$, the velocity $\boldsymbol{v} = (v_x, v_y)$, the pressure $P$, the internal energy per unit mass $\varepsilon$ and the gravitational potential $\Phi$, the equations read $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{v}) = 0$, $\partial(\rho\boldsymbol{v})/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v} + \mathbf{I}P) = -\rho\nabla\Phi$ and $\partial E/\partial t + \nabla\cdot[(E + P)\boldsymbol{v}] = \rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t$. The total energy per unit volume, $E = \rho(v^2/2 + \varepsilon + \Phi)$, includes the potential energy. As one vector equation, $\partial\boldsymbol{U}/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{U}) = \boldsymbol{S}$ (eq. 2), the system has the conserved variables $\boldsymbol{U} = (\rho, \rho v_x, \rho v_y, E)$ and the sources $\boldsymbol{S} = (0, -\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial x, -\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial y, \rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t)$. The fluxes of mass and energy are $\rho\boldsymbol{v}$ and $(E + P)\boldsymbol{v}$, and those of the two momenta $(\rho v_x^2 + P, \rho v_x v_y)$ and $(\rho v_x v_y, \rho v_y^2 + P)$ (eq. 3). The documentation writes the momentum fluxes with a Kronecker delta, which adds $P$ only to the flux of $\rho v_x$ in $x$ and to that of $\rho v_y$ in $y$. Fluxes and sources need the pressure and velocity, so that the primitive variables $\boldsymbol{V} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, P)$ complete the description, and the code converts often between the two sets (2.1.3). The potential is either imposed or follows from the density by the Poisson equation $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, with the gravitational constant $G$ (eq. 5). Fixed potentials of the user can stand for a constant acceleration or for masses that are not simulated hydrodynamically (2.7).

**[GK]** The source of the energy equation follows from the potential energy in $E$. Without it, the kinetic and internal energy change by the work $-\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Phi$, while the continuity equation gives $\partial(\rho\Phi)/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\Phi\boldsymbol{v}) = \rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t + \rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Phi$ for the potential energy. Their sum is the energy equation of eq. 1. In a potential that is constant in time, the total energy therefore has no source and is conserved, while the momentum keeps its source.

#### 2.1.2 Equations of state

An equation of state closes the system by relating pressure and internal energy (§2). For an ideal gas, $P = nk_\mathrm{B}T = \rho k_\mathrm{B}T/(\mu m_\mathrm{p}) = \rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, with the number density $n$, Boltzmann's constant $k_\mathrm{B}$, the temperature $T$ in kelvin, the mean particle mass $\mu$ in units of the proton mass $m_\mathrm{p}$ and the adiabatic index $\gamma$ (eq. 4). Its sound speed is $c_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$. An isothermal gas keeps $T$, and so $\varepsilon$, constant. It is barotropic, as its pressure depends on the density alone. The energy equation can then be dropped, since density and the fixed internal energy determine the energy, and the sound speed is $c_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{P/\rho}$.

`Simulation.setEquationOfState` chooses the mode `ideal`, the default with $\gamma = 5/3$, or `isothermal` (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). Its docstring advises $\gamma = 5/3$ for atomic and $7/5$ for diatomic molecular gases. The mean particle mass is needed, in either mode, only to plot temperatures. The isothermal mode requires the fixed internal energy per unit mass in code units. `Setup.internalEnergyFromTemperature` computes it from a temperature as $\varepsilon = k_\mathrm{B}T/[(\gamma - 1)\mu m_\mathrm{p}]$, converted with the square of the ratio of the code units of time and length (`ulula/core/setup_base.py`). The code takes the isothermal pressure as $P = \rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, which equals $\rho k_\mathrm{B}T/(\mu m_\mathrm{p})$, so that $\gamma$ cancels, and the sound speed as $\sqrt{\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)}$, the same in every cell (`Simulation.soundSpeed`). A flag, `track_pressure`, is true for the ideal gas and false for the isothermal one, and decides whether pressure and total energy are carried as variables at all (3.2). `Simulation.soundSpeed` stops with an error where the sound speed is not a number, and advises a smaller CFL number. The background of the documentation still says that Ulula always assumes an ideal gas (`docs/source/simulation.rst`), although version 0.4.0 added the isothermal mode.

A pressure floor can be set as well. The docstring describes it as a minimum that is enforced, which can help where numerical errors make pressures negative, but which breaks the conservation of energy, as it may add thermal energy. `Simulation.checkPressure` runs wherever pressures follow from the conserved variables or are evolved otherwise. Without a floor, it stops with an error at any zero or negative pressure, and advises a smaller CFL number or a floor. With a floor, it raises every pressure below the floor to it, but only when some pressure in the array is zero or negative, so that pressures between zero and the floor otherwise remain.

#### 2.1.3 Primitive and conserved variables

`Simulation.primitiveToConserved` sets the mass density and the momenta $\rho v_x$ and $\rho v_y$, and for an ideal gas the total energy $E = \rho(v_x^2 + v_y^2)/2 + P/(\gamma - 1)$, to which it adds $\rho\Phi$ when gravity is on. `Simulation.conservedToPrimitive` divides the momenta by the density and subtracts the kinetic energy and, with gravity, the potential energy from $E$. It multiplies the remaining internal energy per unit volume by $\gamma - 1$ to obtain the pressure, which it then checks (2.1.2). The potential is kept only among the primitive variables, so that the primitive array must hold it before the conversion. Both functions take the arrays of input and output as arguments, so that they also convert states other than the main ones, such as those at the cell edges, and `primitiveToConservedRet` returns the conserved variables in a new array.

#### 2.1.4 The flux vector

`Simulation.fluxVector` computes the flux of mass, momentum and total energy from a primitive state, for one direction at a time, since the scheme sweeps the directions in turn (2.3). With $v_1$ the velocity along the sweep and $v_2$ the one across it, the flux has the components $\rho v_1$, $\rho v_1^2 + P$ for the momentum along the sweep, $\rho v_1 v_2$ for the momentum across it, and $(E + P)v_1$, where $E$ is computed from the primitive state with $\rho\Phi$ included. The momenta keep their places in the array, so that one function serves both sweeps. For an isothermal gas the pressure is $\rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, and there is no flux of energy, as the energy is no variable.

### 2.2 The grid and boundary conditions

#### 2.2.1 The domain and its cells

`Simulation.setDomain` sets up a domain of $n_x$ by $n_y$ square cells (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). The cell width is $\Delta x = (x_\mathrm{max} - x_\mathrm{min})/n_x$ in both directions, so that the upper edge in $y$ follows as $y_\mathrm{min} + n_y\Delta x$, and the defaults are $x_\mathrm{min} = 0$, $x_\mathrm{max} = 1$ and $y_\mathrm{min} = 0$. The numbers of cells must be integers, with $n_x \geq 2$ and $n_y \geq 1$, and $n_y = 1$ makes the simulation one-dimensional. Before it builds anything, the function rejects two combinations: the HLLC solver with an isothermal gas, and self-gravity from the Poisson equation with boundaries that are not periodic (2.7). Each edge carries $n_\mathrm{g}$ ghost cells, one for constant and two for linear reconstruction (3.2). In $x$, the physical cells have the indices $n_\mathrm{g}$ to $n_\mathrm{g} + n_x - 1$, and the centres of all cells, ghost cells included, lie at $x_\mathrm{min} + (i - n_\mathrm{g} + 1/2)\Delta x$ for the index $i$. The same holds in $y$ for a two-dimensional domain. A one-dimensional domain has a single row in $y$, without ghost cells, at the height $y_\mathrm{min} + \Delta x/2$. Its first index in $y$ is 0, but the code sets the last one to 1, which works because the slices of the physical domain, which run to one past the last index, are cut at the edge of the array. The time and the number of steps start at zero, and the direction of the last sweep at $-1$, before a flag records that the domain is set. `Simulation.xyGrid` returns the coordinates of all cell centres, ghost cells included, as two two-dimensional arrays.

#### 2.2.2 Boundary conditions

Ulula offers three kinds of boundary, chosen for the whole domain: periodic, outflow and wall (§3.6; `Simulation.setDomain`). `Simulation.enforceBoundaryConditions` fills the ghost cells from the physical domain, at every time step (2.3). With periodic boundaries the domain wraps around. The ghost cells at each edge take the values of the physical cells at the opposite edge, and in two dimensions the corners take those of the diagonally opposite corner. With outflow boundaries, the ghost cells copy the adjacent physical cell and the corners the corner cell, so that flows continue across the edge. With walls, the ghost cells mirror the adjacent physical cells, the corners in both directions, and the velocity normal to the wall changes sign: $v_x$ in the ghost cells on the left and right, and $v_y$ in those at the bottom and top. The mirrored counter-flow balances any motion onto the wall, so that no mass or energy crosses it.

The docstring of `setDomain` states what each kind conserves. Periodic boundaries conserve mass, momentum and energy, walls mass and energy but not momentum, and outflow boundaries none of them. Outflow boundaries can also mislead where the flow at an edge points into the domain, which then keeps gaining mass unless pressures from within stop the flow. By default, `enforceBoundaryConditions` treats the hydrodynamic variables of both arrays, `V` and `U`. Called with a list of arrays and a slice of variables, it fills only those, as the solver of gravity does for the potential and its gradients (2.7).

**[GK]** The conservation at walls holds while the flow meets them subsonically. A wall pairs each state with its mirror image, of equal density and pressure and opposite normal velocity, so that the Riemann solvers of 2.4 see $S_\mathrm{R} = -S_\mathrm{L}$. With $v_n$ the velocity towards the wall and $|v_n| < c_\mathrm{s}$, the HLL and HLLC fluxes of mass, of the tangential momentum and of energy then vanish, and the flux of the normal momentum is $P + \rho v_n c_\mathrm{s}$. Where the flow meets the wall faster than sound, the two speeds cross, and the code keeps the flux of the mirrored state, which carries mass and energy into the domain (2.4.1). A scalar copy of the two solvers confirms both cases.

#### 2.2.3 Updates and boundaries of the user

Two functions of the user, each called with the simulation object, extend the boundaries (§3.6). An update function, set with `Simulation.setUserUpdateFunction`, runs before the boundary conditions are filled. It lets a setup act on the simulation at run time, while the boundaries still follow automatically. A boundary function, set with `Simulation.setUserBoundaryConditions`, runs after them, for boundaries of its own, such as a flow that enters the domain through one side. Neither runs when only given arrays are filled. The code does not check what these functions do, and they must leave the primitive and conserved variables consistent with each other. The base class of the problem setups provides both, and each becomes active when a setup overrides it.

### 2.3 The time step and source terms

#### 2.3.1 The Godunov update

Ulula is at heart a Godunov scheme, which solves eq. 2 by moving the conserved quantities between cells (§3). The fluxes across the cell interfaces come from a Riemann solver (2.4), and the states that enter it can be interpolated in space (2.5) and in time (2.6) to raise the accuracy. The paper calls its algorithms not new, since similar solvers are described in many papers and textbooks, such as [Toro](#toro-2009) (2009) and [Zingale](#zingale-2021) (2021). It describes them for teaching, and close to the expressions of the code. With $i$ and $j$ the indices of cells in $x$ and $y$, a Godunov scheme advances a cell over the time $\Delta t$ from step $n$ to step $n + 1$ as $\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1}_{ij} = \boldsymbol{U}^n_{ij} + (\Delta t/\Delta x)(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2} - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2}) + (\Delta t/\Delta x)(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}^{n+1/2}_{j-1/2} - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}^{n+1/2}_{j+1/2}) + \Delta t\,\boldsymbol{S}^{n+1/2}_{ij}$ (§3.1, eq. 6). The flux at $i - 1/2$ crosses the interface between the cells $i - 1$ and $i$, and that at $i + 1/2$ the interface between $i$ and $i + 1$, and likewise in $j$, so that the differences of the fluxes discretise the divergence of eq. 2. The superscript $n + 1/2$ marks that fluxes and sources should approximate their averages over the step, and an accurate scheme needs accurate values of these averages. Ulula aims at second order, at which all errors proportional to $\Delta x$ or $\Delta t$ cancel, but it also offers schemes of first order for experiments.

#### 2.3.2 Dimensional splitting

Taken literally, eq. 6 computes the fluxes of both directions from the same state $\boldsymbol{U}^n$, and each flux must then account for the transverse fluxes, such as the component in $y$ of the flux at $i - 1/2$ (§3.1). Such an unsplit scheme treats the directions symmetrically. Ulula chooses the simpler split scheme instead, which computes and applies the fluxes of each direction in a sweep of its own and ignores the transverse fluxes during a sweep. The state is updated with the differences of the fluxes of the first sweep before the second begins. The flux vector of eq. 3 thus splits into $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_x = (\rho v_x, \rho v_x^2 + P, \rho v_x v_y, (E + P)v_x)$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_y = (\rho v_y, \rho v_y v_x, \rho v_y^2 + P, (E + P)v_y)$ (eq. 7). These are the fluxes of the state within a cell, distinct from the fluxes across interfaces that eq. 6 needs. The code for a one-dimensional problem then serves both directions, run once in each, with $v_x$ and $v_y$ exchanged together with the second and third components (2.1.4). Split schemes stay of second order when the order of the sweeps alternates, as in x-y-y-x-x-y ([Strang](#strang-1968) 1968).

`Simulation.timestep` alternates the order from step to step, each step beginning with the direction in which the previous one ended (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). The direction of the last sweep starts at $-1$, so that the first step sweeps in $y$ first, and the sequence runs y-x-x-y-y-x. The docstring and a comment describe the same alternation as beginning with x-y, as does the example of the paper. A one-dimensional simulation sweeps only in $x$.

Within a sweep, `timestep` first reconstructs the states at the cell edges (2.5, 2.6). For every interface of the domain, the Riemann solver receives the state at the right edge of the cell on its left and the state at the left edge of the cell on its right, and returns the flux (2.4). The conserved variables of the physical cells change by $\Delta t/\Delta x$ times the difference between the fluxes at their left and right interfaces, and the primitive variables follow by conversion. The boundary conditions are filled after each sweep rather than after each step, since the second sweep would otherwise meet cells near the edges that lag behind.

#### 2.3.3 The time step

The time step is limited by the condition of [Courant, Friedrichs and Lewy](#courant-et-al-1928) (1928), CFL for short, by which information must not travel more than one cell in a step (§3.1). To be safe, $\Delta t = \alpha_\mathrm{cfl}\Delta x/c_\mathrm{max}$, with the CFL number $\alpha_\mathrm{cfl}$ between 0 and 1 and the largest signal speed anywhere in the domain, $c_\mathrm{max} = \max(|v_x| + c_\mathrm{s}, |v_y| + c_\mathrm{s})$ (eqs. 8 and 9). The paper notes that such speeds follow as eigenvalues of matrices like the Jacobian of eq. 26 (2.6). `Simulation.cflCondition` computes this step with the CFL number of the hydro scheme, by default 0.8, which the code requires to stay below 1 (3.2). `Simulation.maxSpeedInDomain` takes the speed over all cells, ghost cells included, and over both velocities, also in one dimension. Its docstring explains why the two velocities are not added in quadrature: the sweeps treat the directions separately, so that the larger speed along either direction sets the step.

The first sweep changes the state, and the fastest speed may grow, while the second sweep must use the same $\Delta t$ for the errors of first order to cancel (§3.1). Ulula therefore tolerates a larger CFL number in the second sweep, up to $\alpha_\mathrm{max}$, the parameter `cfl_max` of the hydro scheme. Before the second sweep, `timestep` computes the CFL number that the step reaches with the current speeds. If it exceeds $\alpha_\mathrm{max}$, the state is reset to the copy taken at the start of the step, and the step is divided by the ratio of the reached CFL number to $\alpha_\mathrm{max}$, times the factor `cfl_reduce_factor`, by default 1.2. The whole step is then repeated, with a warning. The step is attempted at most `cfl_max_attempts` times, by default three, after which the run stops with an error. The function returns the step it took, which can be smaller than a step given to it. A step can be given explicitly, for instance to reach the time of an output, and the user must then ensure that it obeys the condition.

#### 2.3.4 Source terms

The source term of eq. 6 is integrated in time to second order by two half-steps, $\Delta t\,\boldsymbol{S}^{n+1/2}_{ij} \approx (\Delta t/2)\boldsymbol{S}^n_{ij} + (\Delta t/2)\boldsymbol{S}^{n+1}_{ij}$, each evaluated with the state of its moment, one ahead of the sweeps and one after them (§3.1, eq. 10). In `timestep`, the first half-step changes the conserved variables before the first sweep, after which they are converted and the boundaries filled. The second half-step follows the update of the second sweep, or of the only sweep in one dimension, before the conversion to primitive variables. Source terms are active only with gravity, the one source in Ulula (2.7). When a step is repeated, its first half-step is repeated as well, from the restored state.

### 2.4 Riemann solvers

#### 2.4.1 The HLL solver

Deriving the fluxes at the interfaces from the states in the cells is not straightforward (§3.2). An average over the neighbouring cells would make a poor solver, as it ignores that signals such as shocks cross an interface in a definite direction. Riemann solvers account for this, by computing the flux between two discontinuous states that meet at an interface, $\boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{L}$ on its left and $\boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{R}$ on its right. Many have been proposed ([Toro](#toro-2009) 2009). For the Euler equations in one dimension, the Riemann problem has numerical solutions of any desired accuracy ([Colella and Glaz](#colella-glaz-1985) 1985), but approximate solvers are usually faster. The simpler of the two solvers of Ulula is that of [Harten, Lax and van Leer](#harten-et-al-1983) (1983), HLL. Its flux is $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{L}$ for $S_\mathrm{L} \geq 0$, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R}$ for $S_\mathrm{R} \leq 0$, and otherwise $[S_\mathrm{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{L} - S_\mathrm{L}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R} + S_\mathrm{L}S_\mathrm{R}(\boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{R} - \boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{L})]/(S_\mathrm{R} - S_\mathrm{L})$, with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{L} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{L})$, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{R})$ and the wave speeds $S_\mathrm{L} = v_\mathrm{L} - c_\mathrm{s}$ and $S_\mathrm{R} = v_\mathrm{R} + c_\mathrm{s}$ (eq. 11). If even the fastest wave to the left moves right, $S_\mathrm{L} \geq 0$, every wave travels to the right and the interface takes the left state, as in a flow that is supersonic towards the right, and conversely. Between these cases, the flux is an average over the single state between the fastest waves, which takes no account of a contact discontinuity.

`Simulation.riemannSolverHLL` takes the primitive states on both sides of all interfaces of a sweep (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). It computes the sound speed on each side, so that $S_\mathrm{L} = v_\mathrm{L} - c_\mathrm{s,L}$ and $S_\mathrm{R} = v_\mathrm{R} + c_\mathrm{s,R}$ with the velocity along the sweep, and the conserved states and fluxes from the primitive ones (2.1.3, 2.1.4). It evaluates the average of eq. 11 at every interface and then overwrites it with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{L}$ where $S_\mathrm{L} \geq 0$, and afterwards with $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R}$ where $S_\mathrm{R} \leq 0$. A comment explains that this costs less than evaluating the average only where it is needed.

**[GK]** With these estimates the wave speeds can cross. $S_\mathrm{L} > S_\mathrm{R}$ when $v_\mathrm{L} - v_\mathrm{R} > c_\mathrm{s,L} + c_\mathrm{s,R}$, that is, where two states collide faster than the sum of their sound speeds. Where then $S_\mathrm{L} \geq 0 \geq S_\mathrm{R}$, both conditions of eq. 11 hold, and the code keeps $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R}$, which it sets last. For two states that collide symmetrically, this flux carries mass across the interface, although the exact solution carries none.

#### 2.4.2 The HLLC solver

The HLL flux does not distinguish the states on either side of the contact discontinuity that the Riemann problem of the Euler equations generally contains (§3.2). The HLLC solver, HLL with the contact, adds this distinction ([Toro, Spruce and Speares](#toro-et-al-1994) 1994; [Li](#li-2005) 2005). Ulula follows the formulation of [Toro](#toro-2009) (2009), one of several that have been proposed. With one intermediate state on each side of a contact moving at the speed $S^*$, the flux is $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{L}$ for $S_\mathrm{L} \geq 0$, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{L} + S_\mathrm{L}(\boldsymbol{U}^*_\mathrm{L} - \boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{L})$ for $S_\mathrm{L} < 0 \leq S^*$, $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R} + S_\mathrm{R}(\boldsymbol{U}^*_\mathrm{R} - \boldsymbol{U}_\mathrm{R})$ for $S^* \leq 0 < S_\mathrm{R}$, and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R}$ for $S_\mathrm{R} \leq 0$ (eq. 12). Exact wave speeds are complicated to compute ([Batten et al.](#batten-et-al-1997) 1997). Following [Toro](#toro-2009) (2009), Ulula takes the outer speeds from HLL and estimates the speed of the contact as $S^* = [P_\mathrm{R} - P_\mathrm{L} + \rho_\mathrm{L}v_\mathrm{L}(S_\mathrm{L} - v_\mathrm{L}) - \rho_\mathrm{R}v_\mathrm{R}(S_\mathrm{R} - v_\mathrm{R})]/[\rho_\mathrm{L}(S_\mathrm{L} - v_\mathrm{L}) - \rho_\mathrm{R}(S_\mathrm{R} - v_\mathrm{R})]$, with $v$ the velocity along the sweep (eq. 13). With $X$ for L or R, the intermediate density is $\rho^*_X = \rho_X(S_X - v_X)/(S_X - S^*)$ (eq. 14), and the intermediate state is $\boldsymbol{U}^*_X = (\rho^*_X, \rho^*_X S^*, \rho^*_X v_{X,\perp}, \rho^*_X\{E_X/\rho_X + (S^* - v_X)[S^* + P_X/(\rho_X(S_X - v_X))]\})$, where $v_\perp$ is the velocity across the sweep (eq. 15). HLLC costs more than HLL but does markedly better where contact discontinuities matter (Fig. 1; 2.6.4). Since eqs. 13 and 15 contain pressure and energy, an isothermal gas would need them reconstructed, and there HLLC gains nothing by construction, as an isothermal gas has no contact discontinuities. Ulula therefore allows HLLC only for an ideal gas (2.2.1).

`Simulation.riemannSolverHLLC` follows chapter 10.4 of [Toro](#toro-2009) (2009), as its docstring says. It shares its first steps with HLL and computes $S^*$ at every interface. It then evaluates each of the four cases of eq. 12 only where it applies, and builds the star states only there, since they are more costly to compute. Where $S^* = 0$ exactly, it takes the star state on the left, and, as in HLL, it sets $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_\mathrm{R}$ last where $S_\mathrm{R} \leq 0$. **[GK]** The star states of the code agree with eqs. 14 and 15, and the crossing of the wave speeds affects HLLC in the same way as HLL.

### 2.5 Reconstruction and slope limiting

#### 2.5.1 Constant and linear states

The Riemann solver needs the states on both sides of each interface (§3.3). The state at the left edge of cell $i$, on the right side of the interface $i - 1/2$, is $\boldsymbol{U}_{i-1/2}$, and the state at its right edge, on the left side of $i + 1/2$, is $\boldsymbol{U}_{i+1/2}$. The simplest choice holds the state constant within each cell, $\boldsymbol{U}_{i-1/2} = \boldsymbol{U}_{i+1/2} = \boldsymbol{U}_i$ (eq. 16), which is of first order, since its error scales with $\Delta x$, and highly diffusive in practice (Fig. 1). For second order in space, Ulula reconstructs linearly within each cell, after [van Leer](#van-leer-1979) (1979). Each variable has a limited slope $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{lim}$ of its own, so that the edge states are $\boldsymbol{V}^n_{i\pm1/2} = \boldsymbol{V}^n_i \pm (\Delta x/2)\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{lim}$ (eq. 17). The reconstruction works in the primitive variables. Limiting them guards density and pressure directly against negative values and spurious jumps, and it prepares the Hancock step in primitive variables (2.6.2). The slopes are not interpolated between the centres of neighbouring cells, which would remove any difference at the edges and leave the Riemann solver nothing to do. They come instead from the differences to the neighbouring cells: the left and right derivatives $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{L} = (\boldsymbol{V}_i - \boldsymbol{V}_{i-1})/\Delta x$ and $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{R} = (\boldsymbol{V}_{i+1} - \boldsymbol{V}_i)/\Delta x$, and their mean, the central derivative $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{C} = (\boldsymbol{V}_{i+1} - \boldsymbol{V}_{i-1})/(2\Delta x)$ (eqs. 18 and 19). The central derivative alone can give unphysical edge values, such as negative densities or pressures (Fig. 2). A slope limiter prevents this by reducing it where $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{L}$ and $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{R}$ disagree.

`Simulation.reconstructionConst` does nothing, as the arrays of the edge states are then the array of the centres itself (3.2), and it only stands in where the code calls a reconstruction (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). `Simulation.reconstructionLinear` computes the undivided differences $\boldsymbol{V}_i - \boldsymbol{V}_{i-1}$ and $\boldsymbol{V}_{i+1} - \boldsymbol{V}_i$ of the hydrodynamic variables along the sweep, in every cell but the outermost layer of ghost cells, and passes them to the limiter. It copies all state variables into the arrays of the edge states, then adds half the limited difference at the right edge and subtracts it at the left. The potential, a state variable without a slope, thus stays constant within each cell. With undivided differences, half the limited difference equals the $(\Delta x/2)\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{lim}$ of eq. 17.

#### 2.5.2 Slope limiters

All limiters return a zero slope, and so a constant state, where the two slopes differ in sign, and they tend to zero as the smaller slope does (§3.3). The most cautious, the minimum modulus or minmod limiter ([Roe](#roe-1986) 1986), takes the smaller of the two slopes, $s_\mathrm{MinMod} = \mathrm{sign}(s_\mathrm{L})\min(|s_\mathrm{L}|, |s_\mathrm{R}|)$, where either sign serves, as both agree wherever the limiter acts (eq. 20). Reasonable limiters lie between minmod and the central slope. The limiter of [van Leer](#van-leer-1974) (1974), an intermediate choice, is the harmonic mean $s_\mathrm{vanLeer} = s_\mathrm{L}s_\mathrm{R}/s_\mathrm{C} = 2s_\mathrm{L}s_\mathrm{R}/(s_\mathrm{L} + s_\mathrm{R})$ (eq. 21). The most aggressive limiter in common use, the monotonised central limiter of [van Leer](#van-leer-1977) (1977), keeps the central slope until the smaller slope drops below a third of the larger, and below that takes twice the smaller slope, $s_\mathrm{MC} = \mathrm{sign}(s_\mathrm{L})\min(2\min(|s_\mathrm{L}|, |s_\mathrm{R}|), |s_\mathrm{C}|)$ (eq. 22). Further limiters that lie between minmod and MC, for instance that of [van Albada, van Leer and Roberts](#van-albada-et-al-1982) (1982) or UMIST ([Lien and Leschziner](#lien-leschziner-1994) 1994), give results like those of van Leer and are left out.

Fig. 2 plots the limited slope, in units of the left slope, against the ratio of the right to the left slope, for a right slope no steeper than the left, which suffices as all limiters are symmetric. Minmod is the diagonal, since it takes the right slope. MC rises at twice that rate up to a ratio of one third and then follows the central slope, and van Leer runs between the two, with van Albada and UMIST close to it. The limiters vanish at a ratio of zero and reach the left slope at a ratio of one, while the unlimited central slope starts at half the left slope. Four further panels apply no limiter, minmod, van Leer and MC to a row of cell values with a sharp drop. Without a limiter, the central slopes overshoot the neighbouring values at the drop, minmod is the flattest, and MC keeps the slopes closest to the mean of the two differences, as long as these are similar.

`Simulation.limiterNone`, `limiterMinMod`, `limiterVanLeer` and `limiterMC` implement these with masks over the arrays of slopes. The limiter `none` returns the unlimited central slope, which its docstring calls generally unstable and meant for tests and demonstrations. Minmod sets the left slope where both slopes share their sign and the left one is no steeper, and the right slope where the right one is shallower. The limiter of van Leer applies eq. 21 where the signs agree. MC first takes twice the shallower slope where the signs agree, and then the central slope wherever that is smaller in magnitude. Where the signs differ, every limiter leaves the slope at zero.

The choice of limiter matters (§3.3; Fig. 1). With the Euler integrator, the blob of the advection test of Fig. 1 diffuses without reconstruction, keeps a sharp but slightly distorted edge with minmod, and turns unstable with MC. With the Hancock step similar differences remain, and MC gives the sharpest result. The best limiter depends on the problem, and MC can, for instance, cause effects at the edges in a shock tube.

### 2.6 Time interpolation

#### 2.6.1 The Euler integrator

The linear reconstruction is of second order in space, but the fluxes of eq. 6 should also average over the step in time (§3.4). The simplest scheme takes the current edge states as the input of the Riemann solver, $\boldsymbol{V}^{n+1/2}_{i\pm1/2} \approx \boldsymbol{V}^n_{i\pm1/2}$ (eq. 23). This is wrong already at first order in time, as its error scales with $\Delta t$, and Ulula offers it, as the Euler integrator, to show its instability (Fig. 1). In the code, the time integration `euler` leaves the reconstructed states as they are.

For constant states, the reconstruction does nothing, and the code never reaches the Hancock step of 2.6.2, which would leave states without slopes unchanged in any case. Constant states with the Hancock step are therefore the same scheme as constant states with the Euler integrator, the diffusive first-order Godunov scheme of Fig. 1. The documentation instead calls this combination unstable (`docs/source/simulation.rst`), and §3.3 calls constant reconstruction both highly diffusive and unstable. **[GK]** The two panels of Fig. 1 for constant states and HLLC, one with the Euler integrator and one with the Hancock step, agree to the pixel. A run of `Simulation` in one dimension gives identical states for the two time integrations.

#### 2.6.2 The Hancock step

For second order in time, the reconstructed states next to the interfaces advance by half a step (§3.4). The solver itself computes the true change, but the fluxes of the two edge states of a cell, differenced, estimate the change within it to first order: $\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1/2}_{i\pm1/2} = \boldsymbol{U}^n_{i\pm1/2} + (\Delta t/2\Delta x)[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{V}^n_{i-1/2}) - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{V}^n_{i+1/2})]$ (eq. 24). Converted back to primitive variables, these states enter the Riemann solver at their interfaces. This is the Hancock step. Where the slope of a variable is zero, its edge states and fluxes are equal, and the step changes nothing. The step needs many conversions: the reconstruction in $\boldsymbol{V}$, the conversion to $\boldsymbol{U}$, the fluxes and the update of $\boldsymbol{U}$, the conversion back to $\boldsymbol{V}$, and only then the Riemann solver.

Following [Mignone and Tzeferacos](#mignone-tzeferacos-2010) (2010), the step can be taken in primitive variables instead. Without sources, eq. 2 in one dimension becomes $\partial\boldsymbol{V}/\partial t = -\boldsymbol{A}_x\,\partial\boldsymbol{V}/\partial x$ (eq. 25). As each sweep is one-dimensional, one matrix serves both, with the velocities exchanged. For the variables $(\rho, v_x, v_y, P)$, its rows are $(v_x, \rho, 0, 0)$, $(0, v_x, 0, 1/\rho)$, $(0, 0, v_x, 0)$ and $(0, \rho c_\mathrm{s}^2, 0, v_x)$ (eq. 26, with the correction below). Since $\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{lim}$ approximates $\partial\boldsymbol{V}/\partial x$, the half-step becomes $\boldsymbol{V}^{n+1/2}_{i\pm1/2} = \boldsymbol{V}^n_{i\pm1/2} - (\Delta t/2)\boldsymbol{A}_x(\boldsymbol{V}^n_i)\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{lim}$, with the matrix taken at the centre of the cell (eq. 27, with the correction below). Its results hardly differ from those of eq. 24, and, being faster, it is the default. Taking the matrix at the edge states would make virtually no difference and require two evaluations.

**[GK]** Three details of eqs. 25 to 27 need correcting, and the code has all three right. Eq. 25 holds with $\boldsymbol{A}_x = (\partial\boldsymbol{U}/\partial\boldsymbol{V})^{-1}\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}/\partial\boldsymbol{V}$, the matrix of eq. 26, and not with the Jacobian $\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}/\partial\boldsymbol{V}$ that the text names, whose second row would be $(v_x^2, 2\rho v_x, 0, 1)$. The last row of eq. 26 prints $c_\mathrm{s}^2/\rho$, where the equation of the pressure, $\partial P/\partial t + v_x\,\partial P/\partial x + \rho c_\mathrm{s}^2\,\partial v_x/\partial x = 0$, needs $\rho c_\mathrm{s}^2 = \gamma P$. With this entry, the eigenvalues of the matrix are $v_x - c_\mathrm{s}$, $v_x$ twice and $v_x + c_\mathrm{s}$, the speeds behind eq. 9. Eq. 27 adds $(\Delta t/2)\boldsymbol{A}_x\boldsymbol{s}_\mathrm{lim}$, whereas eq. 25 requires its subtraction.

#### 2.6.3 The Hancock step in the code

`Simulation.primitiveEvolution` returns the rate of change $-\boldsymbol{A}_x\,\partial\boldsymbol{V}/\partial x$ from a state and its derivatives along the sweep, with $\gamma P$ in the last row (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). For an isothermal gas, the derivative of the pressure is that of the density times $\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, and the pressure has no equation of its own. In `reconstructionLinear`, the time integration `hancock` adds $\Delta t/(2\Delta x)$ times this rate, from the state at the centre and the limited difference, to both edge states, and then checks their pressures. The time integration `hancock_cons` follows eq. 24 with the conserved states and fluxes of both edges, and converts the results back. Its docstring expects the two forms to agree almost exactly, so that each checks the other, while a comment calls their results identical. In one dimension, however, `hancock_cons` takes no step at all. It selects the cells by a slice fixed to the interior in $y$, which is empty for a single row, so that the run equals one with the Euler integrator. **[GK]** A run of `Simulation` on a smooth advection problem confirms this: in one dimension, `hancock_cons` reproduces the Euler integrator exactly, while in two dimensions it agrees with `hancock` to $10^{-13}$.

The combination of linear reconstruction and the Hancock step goes by the name MUSCL–Hancock, after the monotonic upstream-centred scheme for conservation laws of [van Leer](#van-leer-1979) (1979) (§3.4). In its split form it remains stable for all CFL numbers below 1 ([Mignone et al.](#mignone-et-al-2012) 2012). Runge–Kutta schemes, which take several steps and average them with weights, reach second order in time perhaps more simply, but they tend to be slower, as they evaluate the Riemann fluxes several times.

#### 2.6.4 The advection test of Fig. 1

Fig. 1 advects a sharply bounded blob of dense gas through the domain with $v_x = 0.5$ and $v_y = 0.4$, and shows it once it has crossed the domain about once, after some 550 steps, next to the exact solution. Without reconstruction and with the Euler integrator, HLL smears the blob heavily, while HLLC helps much, since the edge of the blob is a contact discontinuity, and the Hancock step adds nothing for constant states (2.6.1). The minmod limiter with the Euler integrator and HLLC improves the blob markedly but deforms it. With the Hancock step instead, minmod is more diffusive but freer of these artefacts. MC with the Euler integrator and HLL becomes unstable. MC with the Hancock step and HLLC, the default of `HydroScheme`, gives the best result.

### 2.7 Gravity

#### 2.7.1 The modes of gravity

Ulula offers three kinds of gravitational potential (§3.5). A fixed acceleration $g$ is taken as the potential $\Phi = gy$, whose source acts on the momentum along $y$, or along $x$ in one dimension (Fig. 3). A constant potential set by the user has its gradients $\partial\Phi/\partial x$ and $\partial\Phi/\partial y$ computed once and applied as sources to the momenta (Fig. 4). In both cases $\partial\Phi/\partial t$, and with it the source of the energy, vanishes. With self-gravity, the potential and its gradients are computed anew each time the sources are added, twice in a step (2.3.4; Fig. 5), and self-gravity can be combined with an additive fixed potential.

`Simulation.setGravityMode` chooses among `none`, the default, `fixed_acc`, `fixed_pot`, `poisson` and `fixed+poisson` (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). It also takes the direction of a fixed acceleration, `gravity_dir`, 0 for $x$ and 1 for $y$, the default, as well as the acceleration `g` and the gravitational constant `G` in code units, both 1 by default. It must precede `setDomain`, as gravity adds variables (3.2), and it switches the source terms on. In one dimension, a fixed acceleration acts along $x$ whatever direction is given. Self-gravity requires periodic boundaries (2.2.1). The mode `fixed+poisson` adds the fixed potential of the user to that of self-gravity at every solution. After the initial conditions, `Simulation.solveGravity` must run once with `initial_setup = True`, as the base class of the setups does.

#### 2.7.2 Fixed potentials

In its first call, `solveGravity` sets the potential of a fixed acceleration to $gx$ or $gy$ over all cells, ghost cells included. For a fixed potential, it takes the gradients by central differences, $(\Phi_{i+1} - \Phi_{i-1})/(2\Delta x)$ and likewise in $y$, over all cells but the outermost layer. The setup must therefore give the potential in the ghost cells as well, since the boundary conditions fill only the hydrodynamic variables (2.2.2). For `fixed+poisson`, the first call copies the potential that the user set into a variable of its own, `GU`, before self-gravity overwrites the potential.

#### 2.7.3 Self-gravity by fast Fourier transform

For self-gravity, the Laplacian is approximated by its second-order difference, $\nabla^2\Phi_{i,j} \approx (\Phi_{i+1,j} + \Phi_{i-1,j} + \Phi_{i,j+1} + \Phi_{i,j-1} - 4\Phi_{i,j})/\Delta x^2 = 4\pi G\rho_{ij}$ (eq. 28), which Fourier space solves exactly, as [Zingale](#zingale-2021) (2021) derives. The transform of the density, $\tilde\rho_{lm}$, times a Green's function gives the potential in Fourier space, $\tilde\Phi_{lm} = 2\pi G\tilde\rho_{lm}\Delta x^2/[\cos(2\pi k_{x,lm}) + \cos(2\pi k_{y,lm}) - 2]$ (eq. 29), and an inverse transform gives $\Phi_{ij}$. Here $k_x$ and $k_y$ are the dimensionless wavenumbers of the transform: $0, 1/N_x, 2/N_x$ and on to $0.5 - 1/N_x$, then $-0.5$ and on to $-1/N_x$, for $N_x$ cells in $x$, and likewise in $y$. The transform assumes periodic boundaries, hence the restriction. The Green's function is undefined for $l = m = 0$, the DC mode, which stands for a uniform background density ([Gnedin, Kravtsov and Rudd](#gnedin-et-al-2011) 2011). In a periodic domain, a uniform offset of the density amounts to mass of infinite extent without net gravitational effect, and the potential has no defined zero point. The paper sets the denominator of eq. 29 to 1 for this mode alone. It reports that the discrete Laplacian of eq. 28, applied to the result, then reproduces the source of the Poisson equation closely, once the mean of that source is removed.

`solveGravity` computes the Green's function once, in its first call, from the frequencies of the transform of NumPy, as the inverse of $2(\cos 2\pi k_x + \cos 2\pi k_y - 2)/\Delta x^2$, or of $2(\cos 2\pi k_x - 1)/\Delta x^2$ in one dimension, a factor that it sets to 1 for the DC mode before inverting it. At every call, it transforms $4\pi G\rho$ over the physical domain, multiplies by the Green's function, transforms back and keeps the real part as the potential of the physical cells. The gradients follow by central differences with periodic wrapping, and the boundaries are filled for the potential and its gradients (2.2.2). In one dimension the code transforms the single row as a one-dimensional array. Setting this factor rather than the denominator of eq. 29 to 1 gives the potential the mean of its source, $4\pi G\bar\rho$, as a comment intends, rather than the $2\pi G\Delta x^2\bar\rho$ that the wording of the paper implies. **[GK]** Either constant drops out of the gradients and, in a periodic domain, whose mass is fixed, also out of the change of the potential that enters the energy (2.7.4). A run of `solveGravity` on a random density gives a potential whose discrete Laplacian matches $4\pi G(\rho - \bar\rho)$ to about $10^{-11}$, in two dimensions and in one, with the mean $4\pi G\bar\rho$. The Green's function agrees with eq. 9.16 of Zingale's text (`computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md`, 2.6.2), so that the pointer of the docstring of `solveGravity` to its §9.2 holds.

#### 2.7.4 The gravitational sources

`Simulation.addSourceTerms` adds the sources over a given time to the conserved variables (2.3.4). For a fixed acceleration, it changes only the momentum along the acceleration, by $-\rho g\,\Delta t$. Its docstring explains why the energy stays unchanged: as $E$ contains the potential energy, the momentum gained in falling is balanced by the change of the potential (2.1.1). For the other modes, it subtracts $\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial x\,\Delta t$ and $\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial y\,\Delta t$ from the momenta. With self-gravity, it first solves for the potential of the current density and adds $\rho$ times the change of the potential since the previous solution to $E$. This integrates the source $\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t$ over that interval. An isothermal gas, which has no energy variable, receives only the sources of momentum. **[GK]** The added energy equals the change of $\rho\Phi$ at fixed density, so that the pressure recovered with the new potential stays the same.

### 2.8 Problem setups and tests

#### 2.8.1 The setups

All code for one problem lives in a class derived from one shared base class (§4.1; 3.6). In the simplest case, a setup overrides only two functions, one that gives it a short name and one that sets the initial state, the equation of state and boundary conditions included. Further functions can provide a known solution for the plots, limits for the plots, or actions at run time. Writing setups is part of learning with Ulula, but fifteen come prepared: well-known tests of hydrodynamic codes and algorithms, and standard physics, sound waves and instabilities among it, six in one dimension and nine in two (§4.1). Each has an example in `ulula/examples/examples.py`, whose function `main` lists them all, commented out, to be run one at a time, and an image of its result on a page of the documentation (`docs/source/setups_1d.rst`, `docs/source/setups_2d.rst`).

#### 2.8.2 Advection in one dimension

`SetupAdvection1D` carries a profile of density with the constant velocity $v_x = 1$ through the periodic domain $[0, 1]$, at the pressure 1, in an ideal gas with $\gamma = 5/3$ (`ulula/setups/advection_1d.py`). The profile is by default the sine wave $\rho = 0.6 + 0.4\sin(4\pi x)$, or else a top hat of density 1 between $x = 1/3$ and $2/3$ on a background of 0.1. The known solution is the initial profile shifted by $v_x t$, at the same velocity and pressure. The docstring calls this perhaps the simplest meaningful test, of the stability of schemes and of the choice of limiters and Riemann solvers, and §4.1 adds that it reveals much about interpolation and limiting. The example advects the top hat on 100 cells to $t = 1.1$ with twelve combinations, of the Euler integrator or the Hancock step, constant states, minmod or MC, and HLL or HLLC, and catches the runs that fail (`runAdvection1D`). The image of the documentation shows six of them (`docs/images/setup_advection_1d.png`). Constant states with the Euler integrator and HLL smear the top hat into a hump of height 0.84. Minmod with the Euler integrator and HLL rounds its edges, while MC with the Euler integrator and HLLC breaks up into oscillations that reach 1.3. With the Hancock step, minmod and HLL round the edges as well, MC and HLL keep them steep but dip slightly below the background at the foot on the left, and MC and HLLC give the sharpest profile without visible oscillations. The sine wave serves the test of convergence (§4.2).

#### 2.8.3 The sound wave

`SetupSoundwave` drives a sound wave into air from the left edge of a domain 1 km long (`ulula/setups/soundwave.py`). The gas has the density of air, $1.28\times10^{-3}$ g cm$^{-3}$, $\gamma = 7/5$ and $\mu = 29$, with the standard pressure of $1.01325\times10^6$ dyn cm$^{-2}$ for an ideal gas, or the temperature of 300 K for an isothermal one. The code units are 1 km, 1 s and $10^{12}$ g, in which the density of air is 1.28. The wave has the frequency 1 Hz and the relative amplitude 0.01 by default. A boundary function sets the ghost cells on the left to the linear wave $\delta = A\sin(\omega t - kx)$, with $k = \omega/c_\mathrm{s}$, $\rho = \rho_0(1 + \delta)$ and $v_x = c_\mathrm{s}\delta$, and the pressure $P_0(\rho/\rho_0)^\gamma$ for the ideal gas or $\rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$ for the isothermal one, and converts them to conserved variables. The right edge is an outflow boundary. The known solution is this wave up to its front at $c_\mathrm{s}t$, with the undisturbed gas beyond. The docstring notes that linear theory holds only for small amplitudes, as larger waves steepen, and §4.1 that the setup demonstrates that sound waves exist, how fast they travel and how they steepen. The example runs the ideal gas with the amplitude 0.01 on 300 cells to $t = 4$ s, with plots every 0.5 s in metres, seconds and kilograms (`runSoundwave`). The image of the documentation shows the wave after 2 s, close to the linear solution, with crests about 295 m apart, a velocity amplitude of 2.9 m/s and its front near 590 m. **[GK]** These numbers fit the isothermal sound speed at 300 K, $\sqrt{k_\mathrm{B}T/(\mu m_\mathrm{p})} = 292$ m/s, rather than the ideal gas of the example, whose sound speed $\sqrt{\gamma P_0/\rho_0} = 333$ m/s would place the front at 666 m.

#### 2.8.4 The shock tube

`SetupShocktube` sets up a Riemann problem in one dimension, by default that of [Sod](#sod-1978) (1978): $\rho = 1$ and $P = 1$ left of $x = 0.5$, $\rho = 1/8$ and $P = 1/10$ right of it, the gas at rest with $\gamma = 7/5$, in the domain $[0, 1]$ with outflow boundaries (`ulula/setups/shocktube.py`). The discontinuity splits into a rarefaction that moves left, and a contact discontinuity and a shock that move right. The setup thus tests the handling of strong shocks, the effect of slope limiting and the softening of discontinuities at low resolution, and by §4.1 mainly the accuracy of Riemann solvers. Its exact solution is based on lecture notes of [van den Bosch](#van-den-bosch) and [Höfner](#hofner), and its docstring warns that it may fail for some parameters. An implicit equation for the Mach number $M$ of the shock, solved with the root finder `brentq` of SciPy between 1.0001 and 20, gives the state behind the shock from the jump conditions, with its velocity from the conservation of mass in the frame of the shock. The state between the rarefaction and the contact has the same pressure and velocity, and the density of the isentrope through the left state. The rarefaction follows the self-similar fan between $x_0 - c_\mathrm{s,L}t$ and $x_0 + (v_2 - c_{\mathrm{s},2})t$, where $v_2$ and $c_{\mathrm{s},2}$ belong to that state. The code multiplies $M$ by 0.986, as the solution was off by this factor, for a reason that a comment calls unknown, and another comment rejects the velocity behind the shock given by [van den Bosch](#van-den-bosch) as wrong. The formulae assume both states at rest. The example runs 100 cells to $t = 0.2$ with constant states, with the limiter of van Leer, with MC, and with van Leer and HLLC (`runShocktube`). The second and the fourth are the same scheme, since HLLC is the default. The image of the documentation shows the default scheme at $t = 0.2$ close to the exact solution, with the shock near $x = 0.85$, the contact near 0.69 and the star region at the velocity 0.93 and the pressure 0.30, and §3.3 notes that MC can cause effects at the edges in this test.

**[GK]** The implicit equation of the code, $M - 1/M = c_\mathrm{s,L}[(\gamma + 1)/(\gamma - 1)][1 - t_1^{(\gamma - 1)/(2\gamma)}]$ with $t_1 = (P_\mathrm{R}/P_\mathrm{L})[2\gamma M^2/(\gamma + 1) - (\gamma - 1)/(\gamma + 1)]$, lacks a division of its right side by $c_\mathrm{s,R}$. The division follows when the velocity behind the shock, $2c_\mathrm{s,R}(M - 1/M)/(\gamma + 1)$, is equated with that behind the rarefaction. With it, $M = 1.65563$, and the shock speed of 1.75216, the pressure $P^* = 0.30313$ and the velocity $v^* = 0.92745$ of the star region are the exact values of Sod's problem. Without it, $M = 1.68073$, which the factor 0.986 brings to 1.65720, so that the shock of the plotted solution runs about 0.1 per cent fast, and the factor fits only near Sod's parameters. The velocity behind the shock of [van den Bosch](#van-den-bosch), $2(M - 1/M)/(\gamma + 1)$, becomes correct when multiplied by $c_\mathrm{s,R}$.

#### 2.8.5 Free fall

`SetupFreefall` lets a Gaussian blob fall under the constant acceleration $g = 1$ in the domain $[0, 1]$, whose outflow boundaries let the whole domain fall freely (`ulula/setups/freefall.py`). The density is $0.1 + \exp[-(x - 0.8)^2/(2\sigma^2)]$ with $\sigma = 0.05$, at rest and at the uniform pressure 1, which keeps the blob in pressure equilibrium. The known solution moves the profile to $x = 0.8 - gt^2/2$, with the velocity $-gt$ and the pressure unchanged. The setup checks fixed-acceleration gravity with outflow boundaries and how accurate the gravitational source term is. The example runs 300 cells to $t = 1.2$, with plots every 0.1 (`runFreefall`). The image of the documentation shows the blob at $t = 0$, 0.5 and 1 on the known solution, its peak of 1.1 sinking slightly by the last time. The free fall also serves the test of convergence (§4.2).

#### 2.8.6 The atmosphere

`SetupAtmosphere` models the atmosphere of the Earth in one dimension, between walls at 0 and 30 km, under the gravitational acceleration of the Earth (`ulula/setups/atmosphere.py`). The air has $\gamma = 7/5$ and $\mu = 29$, and the code units are 1 km, 1 h and $10^{12}$ g. In the default isothermal mode, at 270 K, the hydrostatic solution is $\rho = \rho_0\exp(-x/h_0)$, with the density of air at the ground and the scale height $h_0 = \varepsilon(\gamma - 1)/g = k_\mathrm{B}T/(\mu m_\mathrm{p}g)$. The initial density deliberately takes a scale height larger by the factor $1/0.6$ and is scaled to the mass of the hydrostatic solution, so that the gas must settle. In the ideal mode, the initial state is the isothermal solution, and an update function imposes at every step the pressure that a tabulated profile of temperature gives. The table falls from 287 K at the ground to 217 K at 11 km, stays there up to 20 km and rises beyond, up to 100 km, and is interpolated linearly in height. The known solution is the isothermal one in both modes. The docstring warns that too much mass in the upper atmosphere falls and forms shocks, and that a much higher top makes the low densities and pressures unstable. The example runs the isothermal mode with HLL, as an isothermal gas excludes HLLC, on 200 cells to 10 h, and plots density, velocity and temperature every 0.5 h in kilometres, hours and tonnes (`runAtmosphere`). The image of the documentation shows the initial and the final density in tonnes per cubic kilometre: at 10 h it lies close to the exponential profile, near $1.3\times10^6$ at the ground. By §4.1, the atmosphere takes surprisingly long to settle, partly because of the walls. Fig. 6 compares the two modes, plotted in kilometres, tonnes and minutes. With the tabulated temperatures, density and pressure deviate only slightly from the isothermal profiles at 270 K, which the paper takes as support for the isothermal approximation.

#### 2.8.7 The Jeans instability

`SetupJeansInstability` perturbs a uniform isothermal gas, $\rho_0 = 1$ and $P_0 = 1$, so that $c_\mathrm{s} = 1$, by a sine wave of relative amplitude 0.01 and wavelength $\lambda$, by default one wavelength across the periodic domain $[0, 1]$, under self-gravity (`ulula/setups/jeans_instability.py`). A perturbation shorter than the Jeans wavelength $\lambda_\mathrm{J}$ oscillates, a longer one collapses, and the setup chooses $G$ so that $\lambda_\mathrm{J}$ takes its given value, 1.3 by default. Its docstring notes that stability in practice seems to need $\lambda_\mathrm{J}/\lambda \sim 1.5$. The setup has no known solution. The example runs 100 cells to $t = 1$ with HLL, plotting density, pressure, potential and its gradient every 0.1 (`runJeansInstability`). The image of the documentation shows the collapse at $t = 0.9$. The density has grown to about 8 at the crest of the sine, $x = 0.25$, over a background near 0.3, the potential has its minimum there, and its mean lies near 83, the constant of 2.7.3.

**[GK]** The setup takes $G = k_\mathrm{J}^2c_\mathrm{s}^2/\sqrt{4\pi\rho_0}$, with $k_\mathrm{J} = 2\pi/\lambda_\mathrm{J}$, where the dispersion relation of the instability, $\omega^2 = c_\mathrm{s}^2k^2 - 4\pi G\rho_0$, requires $G = k_\mathrm{J}^2c_\mathrm{s}^2/(4\pi\rho_0)$. With $\rho_0 = 1$, $G$ is thus $\sqrt{4\pi}$ times too large, and the true Jeans wavelength is $(4\pi)^{-1/4} \approx 0.53$ times the nominal one, so that stability needs $\lambda_\mathrm{J}/\lambda > 1.88$, which the empirical value of the docstring approximates. Runs of the setup with $\lambda = 1$ on 128 cells to $t = 3$ show growth for nominal Jeans wavelengths up to 1.8, and oscillations below the initial amplitude for 1.95 and 2.2.

#### 2.8.8 Advection in two dimensions

`SetupAdvection2D` moves the whole fluid towards the north-east, with $v_x = 0.5$ and $v_y = 0.4$, through the periodic unit square, at the pressure 1 in an ideal gas with $\gamma = 5/3$ (`ulula/setups/advection_2d.py`). The density is by default a top hat of density 2 and radius 0.2 at the centre, on a background of 1, or else $1.5 + 0.25[\sin 4\pi(x - v_x t) + \sin 4\pi(y - v_y t)]$. A function gives the pattern at any time, but the setup defines no true solution, as these serve only plots in one dimension. By its docstring, the edges diffuse into the surroundings at a speed that the solver sets. Without reconstruction the scheme is extremely diffusive, linear interpolation diffuses less, especially with an aggressive limiter, and sharp gradients with a scheme of first order in time quickly make the test fail. The example runs $100 \times 100$ cells to $t = 2.3$ with the twelve combinations of the advection in one dimension (`runAdvection2D`), and the image of the documentation shows the eight panels of Fig. 1 (2.6.4).

#### 2.8.9 The Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

`SetupKelvinHelmholtz` sets a band of density 2 between $y = 0.25$ and $0.75$, moving with $v_x = 0.5$, in gas of density 1 moving with $-0.5$, at the uniform pressure 2.5, in the periodic unit square (`ulula/setups/kelvin_helmholtz.py`). The default initial conditions are smooth, after [Robertson et al.](#robertson-et-al-2010) (2010). A profile $R = (1 + e^{1/(2\Delta)})^2/\{[1 + e^{2(y - 0.25)/\Delta}][1 + e^{2(0.75 - y)/\Delta}]\}$ with $\Delta = 0.05$ joins the two states, $\rho = 1 + R$ and $v_x = -0.5 + R$, and a perturbation $v_y = \Delta\sin(2\pi x/\lambda)$ seeds the instability, with $\lambda$ the domain width divided by the number of waves, one by default. Its amplitude is thus 0.05, while a parameter of 0.1 that the setup defines for this case goes unused. The sharp alternative sets $R = 1$ within the band, and a perturbation of amplitude 0.001 that varies little in $y$ helps the grid noise, which a comment says seeds the instability, as in [Springel](#springel-2010) (2010). The docstring recommends the smooth version as the more physical test. The example runs $200 \times 200$ cells to $t = 3$, with plots every unit of time, or makes a movie to $t = 4$ that lasts 20 s (`runKelvinHelmholtz`). The image of the documentation shows the band, its first wave, the roll-up and two developed vortices.

#### 2.8.10 Cloud crushing

`SetupCloudCrushing` places a cloud of radius 0.02 and density 100 at rest at $(0.1, 0.25)$ in a wind of density 1 that moves with $v_x = 0.1$, both at the pressure 1, in a domain of $1 \times 0.5$ with outflow boundaries (`ulula/setups/cloud_crushing.py`). The sharp edge of the cloud launches shocks, which soon leave the domain. A boundary function holds the wind state in the ghost cells on the left, since gas reflected off the cloud could otherwise flow back in through an outflow boundary. By its docstring, the cloud is destroyed quickly, at a rate and in a manner that depend strongly on resolution, and the setup shows a rectangular domain and boundaries that combine standard ones with one of the user. §4.1 cites [Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994) for the problem, and notes that its outcome varies with the hydrodynamic method ([Agertz et al.](#agertz-et-al-2007) 2007) and on the physics included ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2018) 2018). The example runs $300 \times 150$ cells to $t = 14$, with plots of density and $v_x$ every unit of time (`runCloudCrushing`). The image of the documentation shows the cloud at $t = 0$, 1, 2, 4, 6 and 12. It is pressed into a crescent, stretched and carried downstream to about $x = 0.6$, its density falls from 100 to about 6, and a flow reverses behind it. **[GK]** The boundary function sets only the primitive variables of the ghost cells, which suffices, as the solver reads the ghost cells only through them. The wind is subsonic, at Mach 0.08 with respect to its own sound speed of 1.29, and it moves at 0.78 of the sound speed of the cloud, which is a hundred times colder. [Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994), by contrast, treat clouds struck by shocks, as their title says.

#### 2.8.11 The Sedov–Taylor explosion

`SetupSedovTaylor` injects the energy $E = 50$ at the centre of the unit square, at the density 1 and pressure 1, with outflow boundaries (`ulula/setups/sedov_taylor.py`). The energy enters as extra pressure, $(\gamma - 1)$ times an energy density in a Gaussian of 0.75 cell widths, scaled to $E$ over the grid. The docstring sets this radius near one cell, which avoids artefacts of the square cells, and a comment reports that radii near one cell worked better than larger ones. §4.1 calls this the classic blast wave ([Taylor](#taylor-1950) 1950; [Sedov](#sedov-1959) 1959), with the energy spread over a narrow kernel to avoid effects of resolution, and states that Ulula reproduces the analytical solution. As the energy far exceeds the thermal energy of the domain, about 1, the solution is self-similar. The class `SedovSolution`, adapted from Gandalf ([Hubber et al.](#hubber-et-al-2018) 2018), computes it, and its docstring traces the solution to [Book](#book-1991) (1991) and [Korobeynikov et al.](#korobeynikov-et-al-1961) (1961). It evaluates the parametric self-similar solution in $\nu = 1$, 2 or 3 dimensions, for an ambient density $\propto r^{-w}$, at $10^5$ values of its parameter and interpolates it in $\eta = r/R_\mathrm{s}$. The shock radius is $R_\mathrm{s} = C(Et^2/\rho_0)^{1/(\nu + 2 - w)}$, with $C$ from an integral over the energy, and the shock speed $2R_\mathrm{s}/[(\nu + 2 - w)t]$. The density, pressure and velocity are their values behind the shock, $(\gamma + 1)\rho_0/(\gamma - 1)$, $2\rho_0V_\mathrm{s}^2/(\gamma + 1)$ and $2V_\mathrm{s}/(\gamma + 1)$, times the profiles. The setup uses $\nu = 2$ and $w = 0$, and provides the solution for radial plots only. Its parameter $\gamma$, which the docstring calls the adiabatic index of the gas, enters only the exact solution, while the simulation keeps its default of 5/3, so that another value would compare unlike gases. The example runs $200 \times 200$ cells to $t = 0.02$, with radial and two-dimensional plots of density, pressure and total velocity (`runSedovTaylor`). The image of the documentation shows a round blast wave with its shock at $r \approx 0.43$. The radial profiles follow the exact solution except at the shock, where the peak density is 3.3 against the jump to 4 and the pressure 80 against 87. Beyond the shock, the pressure stays at the ambient 1, which the self-similar solution neglects.

#### 2.8.12 The Gresho vortex

`SetupGreshoVortex` sets a vortex at the origin of the periodic domain $[-1, 1]^2$, at the density 1 in an ideal gas with $\gamma = 5/3$ (`ulula/setups/gresho_vortex.py`). The azimuthal velocity is $5r$ for $r \leq 0.2$, $2 - 5r$ up to $r = 0.4$ and zero beyond, and the pressure, $5 + 25r^2/2$, then $9 - 4\ln 0.2 + 25r^2/2 - 20r + 4\ln r$, and $3 + 4\ln 2$ outside, balances the rotation. The vortex is steady, so that the known solution is the initial state. The docstring calls it a test of symmetry and of the conservation of angular momentum, in which any smoothing of the ring reveals an imperfect conservation of angular momentum, and takes it from [Gresho and Sani](#gresho-sani-1987) (1987), with [Liska and Wendroff](#liska-wendroff-2003) (2003). By §4.1, grid codes often struggle with this stringent test, and Ulula performs well. The example runs $200 \times 200$ cells to $t = 6$, with radial and two-dimensional plots of density, pressure and the radial and azimuthal velocities every two units of time (`runGreshoVortex`). The image of the documentation shows the vortex at $t = 6$. The density stays within 0.002 of 1, the radial velocity within about 0.02 in eight lobes, and the pressure on its profile, while the peak of the azimuthal velocity is smoothed from 1 to about 0.92. **[GK]** The pressure is continuous at $r = 0.2$, where both pieces give 5.5, and at $r = 0.4$, where both give $3 + 4\ln 2$.

#### 2.8.13 The Rayleigh–Taylor instability

`SetupRayleighTaylor` places gas of density 2 above $y = 0.5$ on gas of density 1, under the fixed acceleration $g = 1$ downwards, in a domain three times as high as wide, $1/3$ by 1 with walls (`ulula/setups/rayleigh_taylor.py`). The pressure is integrated hydrostatically from 1 in the top row, adding $g\rho\Delta x$ for each row downwards. A velocity $v_y = A\sin[2\pi(\phi + fx')]\exp[-(y - 0.5)^2/(2\delta^2)]$, with $x'$ the horizontal coordinate in units of the width and $\delta = 0.05$, perturbs the interface. By default $A = 0.1$, $f = 0.5$ and $\phi = 0$, a single peak at the centre, and a second wave with its own amplitude, frequency and phase can be added. The docstring describes the mushroom that forms, and the setup shows fixed-acceleration gravity with walls and the dependence of the instability on the scale of the perturbation. By §4.1, linear theory predicts the initial growth of the perturbation ([Clarke and Carswell](#clarke-carswell-2014) 2014), which Ulula can verify. The example runs $80 \times 240$ cells to $t = 6$, with plots of density and $v_y$ every 0.5 (`runRayleighTaylor`). The image of the documentation shows the single mode: a light bubble rises at the centre, heavy gas sinks at the walls, rolls form at the sides, and the layers mix. Fig. 3 uses two superposed waves of equal amplitude instead, of about 1.5 and 12 wavelengths across the box, at $t = 1$ to 5 and 10. At fixed amplitude the short mode grows first, and alone it is visible at $t = 1$, but the long mode goes unstable as well and, with the short one and the boundaries, forms a complex pattern.

#### 2.8.14 Tidal disruption

`SetupTidalDisruption` moves a Gaussian blob of peak density 2 and width 0.07, centred at $(0.35, 0.35)$, past a point mass at the centre of the unit square with outflow boundaries (`ulula/setups/tidal_disruption.py`). The fixed potential is $\Phi = -0.1/(0.01 + r)$ about the centre, set in all cells. The background has the density 0.05 and pressure 1, and the whole fluid moves with $v_y = 0.3$. The setup shows a fixed potential and the behaviour at strong gradients of it, and has no known solution. The example runs $120 \times 120$ cells to $t = 3$, with plots of density and potential every 0.2 (`runTidalDisruption`). The image of the documentation shows the potential, down to about $-6$ in the central cells, and the blob stretched into a tail towards the point mass. A dense core gathers there, and the rest wraps around it and spreads.

#### 2.8.15 The Keplerian disk

`SetupKeplerianDisk` sets a disk of density 1 between $r = 0.25$ and $0.75$, in gas of density 0.01, at the uniform pressure 1, in the potential of a softened point mass, $\Phi = -(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{-1/2}$ with $\epsilon = 0.1$, in the domain $[-1, 1]^2$ (`ulula/setups/keplerian_disk.py`). All gas rotates with $v_\phi = r(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{-3/4}$, which a comment derives from $v_\phi^2 = r\,\mathrm{d}\Phi/\mathrm{d}r$, so that rotation alone balances gravity. The edges are sharp by default, or Gaussian with the width 0.02. The boundaries are periodic, but a boundary function resets all ghost cells to the initial state at every step and converts them to conserved variables, so that the boundaries rotate. An alternative profile in the code goes unused. The disk is steady in theory, so that the known solution is the initial state. The docstring calls the test notoriously difficult for grid codes, and §4.1 notes that they struggle to keep its angular momentum and its sharp edges (Fig. 4). The example uses the limiter of van Leer with the CFL number 0.8 and runs $150 \times 150$ cells to $t = 3$, with radial and two-dimensional plots every 0.5 (`runKeplerianDisk`). Fig. 4 shows the disk at $t = 0$, 5 and 10, with instabilities growing at its edges, and the disk becomes partly supported by pressure at its centre instead of by rotation alone. The image of the documentation shows spiral disturbances at the inner edge, radial velocities of about $\pm 0.1$ and a raised pressure in the centre.

#### 2.8.16 The merger

`SetupMerger` places two Gaussian blobs of peak density 2 and width 0.05 at $(0.35, 0.35)$ and $(0.65, 0.65)$, at rest in gas of density 0.05 and pressure 1, in the periodic unit square under self-gravity with $G = 1$ (`ulula/setups/merger.py`). The blobs fall together, and pressure and gravity together shape complex but symmetric patterns, until a single blob remains. The setup shows self-gravity in two dimensions and the preservation of symmetry. The example runs $200 \times 200$ cells to $t = 10$, with plots of density, potential and its gradients every 0.5, or a movie on $100 \times 100$ cells to $t = 20$ (`runMerger`). Fig. 5 shows density and potential at $t = 0$ to 10 every two units of time, and the image of the documentation shows the collision along the diagonal, with ejecta along the other diagonal, symmetric about the centre.

#### 2.8.17 Conservation and convergence

`run` checks whether mass, momentum and energy are conserved where they should be (§4.2; 3.3). Outflow boundaries violate all three laws and walls that of momentum (2.2.2). Without gravity the laws hold to machine precision, whereas the gravitational sources of eqs. 1 and 2 break strict conservation. Several setups have known solutions that Ulula can plot for comparison. The advection tests reveal how diffusive the schemes are (Fig. 1), the shock tube and the Sedov–Taylor explosion how they capture shocks, especially by the Riemann solver, and the Gresho vortex and the Keplerian disk the conservation of angular momentum, which challenges grid codes. Fig. 7 shows the convergence with resolution, from 16 to 256 cells, of the advection of the sine wave in one dimension, the free fall and the Gresho vortex. Its caption describes the error as the absolute fractional difference of the density from the equilibrium, or initial, solution after one unit of time, in the $L_1$ norm, normalised to 1 at 64 cells. All three fall slightly less steeply than $N^{-2}$, the slope of a perfect scheme of second order. They drop from 8 to 14 times the error at 64 cells, at 16 cells, to about 0.1 times it at 256 cells. The paper concludes that the MUSCL–Hancock scheme of §3 converges essentially as expected, also with gravitational sources and in the Gresho vortex.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture draws on steps 1 to 3 and 8 to 10 of `plan.md`.

### 3.1 Packaging and installation

Ulula is the Python package ulula, built with setuptools 61.0 or later from `pyproject.toml`, which takes the version from `ulula/utils/version.py`. The package holds the subpackages `core`, `examples`, `physics`, `setups` and `utils`, is marked as unsafe to run from a zip archive, and needs Python 3.6 or later. It depends on NumPy ([Harris et al.](#harris-et-al-2020) 2020), SciPy ([Virtanen et al.](#virtanen-et-al-2020) 2020), Matplotlib ([Hunter](#hunter-2007) 2007) and h5py (`pyproject.toml`; acknowledgements of the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025)). `README.rst` serves as the description of the package, and `README.md` gives the same text in Markdown. Ulula installs from PyPI with `pip install ulula` (`docs/source/installation.rst`). For development, a fork of the repository on BitBucket is cloned and installed with `pip install -e .`, so that changes to the source take effect at once. Updates from the main repository arrive by synchronising the fork on BitBucket and pulling. An older way, adding the folder to `PYTHONPATH`, is still described but no longer recommended, as it is less robust with virtual environments. **[GK]** The wheel of 1.0.2 on PyPI is pure Python, for any version of Python 3. The base class of the setups imports six, a library for code that runs under both Python 2 and 3, for its metaclass (3.6). `pyproject.toml` does not declare it, and it arrives with Matplotlib, which requires python-dateutil, which in turn requires six.

### 3.2 The simulation object and its data

The solver of Ulula is one class, `Simulation`, and a second class, `HydroScheme`, holds the choice of algorithm (`docs/source/simulation.rst`). A simulation proceeds in a fixed order. A `Simulation` is created, and its hydro scheme, equation of state, code units and gravity may be set, as well as functions of the user that change the domain or the boundaries at run time. `Simulation.setDomain` then sets the domain in cells and in coordinates and creates the arrays of variables. The initial conditions are written in primitive variables into the array `V`, as each problem setup does, and a setup with gravity calls `Simulation.solveGravity` with `initial_setup = True`. `Simulation.timestep` then advances the state until the simulation ends, with plots as desired. The runtime function `run` carries out this workflow, which a user may also write out and modify. The setters of the scheme, the equation of state, the code units and the gravity raise an error once the domain is set, as `setDomain` builds the arrays from these settings, while the functions of the user may also be set later. The constructor applies the defaults by calling every setter without arguments (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `Simulation`).

`HydroScheme` holds the four identifiers of the components that part 2 introduces, and four parameters of the time step (2.3). They are the CFL number `cfl`, 0.8 by default, the largest CFL number tolerated in the second sweep, `cfl_max`, 0.95, the factor `cfl_reduce_factor`, 1.2, by which the time step shrinks when that limit is exceeded, and the number of attempts, `cfl_max_attempts`, 3. Its constructor rejects a CFL number or a maximum of 1 or more, a maximum that does not exceed the CFL number, and a factor that does not exceed 1. `Simulation.setHydroScheme` turns the identifiers into function pointers for the reconstruction, the limiter and the Riemann solver. It sets one ghost cell for constant and two for linear reconstruction, since the slopes reach a second neighbour, and rejects unknown identifiers, those of the time integration included.

The docstring of `Simulation` lists its fields in seven groups. The domain is described by whether it has two dimensions, by the cell width, the same in $x$ and $y$, by the numbers of cells in each direction and of ghost cells at each edge, by the first and last indices of the physical cells and the coordinates of the cell centres, by the type of boundary, and by a flag that the domain is set. The fluid variables comprise whether pressure is tracked, the numbers of hydrodynamic and of all variables, dictionaries of the indices of the primitive and of the conserved variables, the arrays `U` and `V`, and the states at the left and right cell edges. The scheme and the time step comprise the `HydroScheme`, whether source terms are active, the time, the number of steps and the direction of the last sweep. Further groups hold the equation of state, with its mode, $\gamma$, $\gamma - 1$ and its inverse, $\mu$, the fixed internal energy and the pressure floor, then the code units in centimetres, seconds and grams, the gravity, with its mode, the direction of a fixed acceleration, $g$ and $G$, and the functions of the user for updates and boundaries. The arrays of variables have one index for the variable and one for each direction, over physical cells and ghost cells alike.

The variables carry abbreviations of two letters, which give their positions in the arrays, select them in plots and name them in initial conditions (`docs/source/simulation.rst`). The primitive variables are `DN`, `VX`, `VY` and `PR`, for density, the two velocities and pressure, and the conserved ones `MS`, `MX`, `MY` and `ET`, for the densities of mass, momentum and total energy. With gravity, the primitive array also holds the potential `GP`, its gradients `GX` and `GY`, and a fixed potential of the user, `GU`, which have no conserved counterparts. In terms of mass $m$, length $l$ and time $t$, densities have the unit $m/l^3$, velocities $l/t$, momentum densities $m/(l^2 t)$, pressure and energy density $m/(l t^2)$, potentials $l^2/t^2$ and their gradients $l/t^2$. As the equations keep their form under changes of these units, the code units can take any values. They leave the outcome unchanged and appear only in plots, unless these are drawn in code units.

`Simulation.setDomain` also lays out the data (2.2.1). It numbers the variables: density and mass density share the index 0, the velocities and momenta 1 and 2, and pressure and total energy 3 for an ideal gas, while an isothermal gas has no fourth variable. The indices become attributes, such as `DN` and `MX`, and enter the dictionaries of the primitive and of the conserved variables. With gravity, the primitive variables gain the potential, then its two gradients unless the acceleration is fixed, and finally the fixed potential of the user in the mode that adds it to self-gravity (2.7). Three counts follow. The hydrodynamic variables are those with an equation of their own, whose fluxes are computed. The state variables add the potential, which the conversions and fluxes need although it has no equation, and all variables add its gradients and the potential of the user. The array `V` holds all variables and `U` the hydrodynamic ones. Each has a copy of the same shape that keeps the state at the start of a time step, so that a failed step can be repeated (2.3). For linear reconstruction, the states at the left and right cell edges have arrays of their own over the state variables. For constant reconstruction they are the array `V` itself, since the states at the edges then equal those at the centres. Slices prepared once select the variables, whether hydrodynamic, state or all, the physical cells in each direction and in the whole domain, the cells shifted by one or two places, and the cells to the left and to the right of every interface of the domain, which the solver reuses.

### 3.3 The runtime function

Four units make up the core of Ulula (§3.6). The class `Simulation` holds the solver of §3. Each problem setup is a class of its own, derived from the base class `Setup`. A module for plotting and analysis extracts quantities from a simulation. A driver, the function `run`, runs a setup, given the final time or a largest number of steps, the hydro scheme and the outputs wanted. Two lines of Python then run a simulation. The documentation shows this for the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability: a setup object is created and passed to `run` with the final time 4 and 200 cells in $x$, and a `HydroScheme` passed as well chooses other algorithms (`docs/source/run.rst`). **[GK]** Counted without blank lines, comments and docstrings, release 1.0.2 has 3,049 active lines of Python, of which the examples take 196 and the setups 1,226. Without the examples, the 2,853 lines bear out the "just under 3000" lines of §3.6, and the setups its "about 1200".

`run` takes the setup, the hydro scheme, the number of cells in $x$, 200 by default, with the setup fixing that in $y$, the final time, 1 by default, an optional largest number of steps and an optional restart file (`ulula/core/run.py`, `run`). Further parameters control the printed progress, every 100 steps by default, and the checks of conservation, which are on by default. Files are written after a number of steps or at intervals of time, plots likewise, and a movie on request. The remaining keyword arguments pass to the plotting functions, each checked against their signatures. A new run creates a `Simulation`, sets the hydro scheme and lets the setup set the initial conditions (3.6), while a restart loads the simulation from its file and requires a final time beyond the time of the file. The limits of the plots, the true solution and the colour maps that the setup defines pass on to the plotting functions.

The checks of conservation record the sums of the conserved variables over the physical cells at the start, and every printed step reports their relative change: of mass, of energy for an ideal gas, and of each momentum that is checked. Outflow boundaries switch the checks off, as they conserve nothing, and momenta are checked only with periodic boundaries and an initial sum above $10^{-6}$ in code units, as a vanishing initial momentum gives no measure. The loop over the steps computes each step from the CFL condition (2.3.3) and stops with an error if it falls below $10^{-10}$, reporting the largest sound speed, which then usually signals an unphysical state. It writes files, plots and movie frames at their times, takes the step, prints, writes the outputs due after a number of steps, and ends at the largest number of steps. It ends with the first step that reaches the final time and does not shorten it, so that the run can pass the final time a little. At the end, `run` prints the steps taken and the processor time per step, makes the movie and plots the final state, and returns the `Simulation`.

An output at a fixed time is meant to leave the run untouched, as the docstring of `run` says and the version history promises since 0.2.1. Where such a time falls within the next step, `getSimAtTime` copies the simulation, advances the copy to that time and hands it to the output. The copy, however, is shallow, made by `copy.copy`, so that it shares the arrays of the simulation. Advancing it advances the arrays of the run itself without its time, and the plots then show the simulation, whose arrays already hold the state at the output time. A restart passes only the boundary function of the setup to `load` (3.4), so that a setup with an update function, such as the atmosphere, cannot be restarted through `run`, although `load` would accept both. **[GK]** Runs of the code confirm both. With output every 0.1, the advection of a density wave to $t = 0.5$ ends up to 0.04 away in the conserved variables from the same run without output, a tenth of the amplitude of the wave, and the restart of a setup with an update function stops with the error of `load`. A run without output stops at $t = 0.5023$ for a final time of 0.5.

### 3.4 Files, plots and movies

`Simulation.save` writes a simulation to a file in HDF5, by default `ulula_` followed by the number of the step (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). Its groups hold the version of the code, the eight parameters of the hydro scheme, and the equation of state, with $\mu$ and the pressure floor stored as 0 when they are not set and the fixed internal energy only for an isothermal gas. They also hold the code units, the gravity with its mode, direction, $g$ and $G$, flags for the functions of the user, the domain with its extents, cell width, numbers of cells and ghost cells and its boundaries, and the time, the number of steps and the direction of the last sweep. The group `grid` holds every primitive variable. The documentation says that it holds the physical domain without its ghost cells (`docs/source/simulation.rst`), but the code writes the whole arrays, ghost cells included. **[GK]** A file written for $8 \times 6$ cells with two ghost cells holds arrays of $12 \times 10$.

The files serve both as snapshots and for restarts (`docs/source/run.rst`). `load` rejects files older than version 1.0.0, the oldest it reads (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `load`). It rebuilds the settings in the order of the setters and the domain, initialises the gravity, and restores the time, the number of steps and the direction of the last sweep. It then reads the primitive variables, derives the conserved ones and fills the boundaries. Functions cannot be saved, so a file that records an update or boundary function of the user requires that function again on loading. **[GK]** `versionIsOlder` (`ulula/utils/utils.py`) stops at the first component in which the second version is lower, but not at one in which it is higher, so that it would take 2.0.0 for older than 1.2.0. With the limit 1.0.0, whose later components are zero, this cannot happen.

The module `plots` names sixteen quantities in its dictionary `fields`, each with a name, a label in LaTeX and a colour map, `RdBu_r` for quantities with a sign and `viridis` for the others (`ulula/core/plots.py`; `docs/source/plots.rst`). They are the primitive and conserved variables, the total, radial and azimuthal velocities, the internal energy, the temperature, and the potential with its gradients and the potential of the user. The labels can be changed in the dictionary, to write $u$ for velocities, for instance. `getQuantities` returns the quantities over all cells, with conversion factors, labels and the factor and label of lengths. It derives the radial and azimuthal velocities about the origin of the coordinates, the internal energy from $E$ less the kinetic and potential energy, and the pressure and energy of an isothermal gas from its fixed internal energy. Temperatures always come in kelvin and need $\mu$. Units for plots must be either all code units or all physical units, since mixed ones would be hard to read.

`plot1d` plots the quantities along a line or, for a two-dimensional simulation, as a radial average. The line runs along the direction with more cells, or along $x$ when both have as many, through the row or column whose index is half the number of cells, counted in the full array with its ghost cells. It can include the ghost cells, marked by grey lines at the edges of the domain. The radial average is taken about the centre of the domain, out to half its shorter side, with four bins per cell by default and even numbers of cells required. It weights each cell by the area that it shares with each annulus, from `circleSquareOverlap`, after code of Philip Mansfield (`ulula/utils/utils.py`; `docs/source/versions.rst`). The docstring promises the average for any two-dimensional simulation, but the slices of the code take as many cells across the shorter side as along the longer, which fails for a domain that is not square. **[GK]** A run confirms that a radial profile of $16 \times 8$ cells stops with an error, while one of $16 \times 16$ works. The true solution of a setup is drawn dashed, its limits and logarithmic scales are applied, and the panels stand side by side or, since 1.0.1, stacked. `plot2d` draws one colour map per quantity, with a colour bar above it, in the proportions of the domain and with the limits, scales and colour maps of the setup. Both functions return the figure and its panels without showing or saving them, and `run` calls a function of the user on each plot before saving it.

A movie takes frames at equal intervals of time, as many as its length in seconds times its frame rate, by default 4 s at 25 frames per second, at most one per step (`run`). The frames are images in PNG, which ffmpeg combines into MP4 or the package pillow into GIF, and are deleted afterwards. A finer movie needs a smaller CFL number and a higher frame rate. Neither ffmpeg nor pillow is a declared dependency of the package (3.1).

### 3.5 Units

As the Euler equations are free of scales, `Simulation` works in code units, which can stand for any scales of length, time and mass (§3.6). `Simulation.setCodeUnits` sets them in centimetres, seconds and grams, 1 by default, before the domain is set (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). The class never uses them. Only the plots do, in units that the user chooses and that need not match the code units. The docstring adds that the solution depends on the units only where the physics goes beyond the Euler equations alone, as with gravity. The module `units` lists the units of plots with their values in cgs and labels: lengths from nanometres to gigaparsecs, among them the astronomical unit and the parsec, times from nanoseconds to gigayears, with the year as $3.15569252\times10^7$ s, and masses from the atomic mass unit and the proton mass to the masses of the Earth and the Sun (`ulula/physics/units.py`; `docs/source/plots.rst`). The module `constants` holds Boltzmann's constant, the proton mass and the gravitational constant in cgs, the critical density of the Universe in solar masses per cubic kiloparsec for $h = 1$, and values for the Earth: its gravitational acceleration, the density and mean particle mass of air, and the standard pressure (`ulula/physics/constants.py`).

### 3.6 Problem setups

A setup is a class derived from `Setup`, an abstract base class (`ulula/core/setup_base.py`; §4.1). The decorator `six.add_metaclass` gives it the metaclass `abc.ABCMeta`, and its methods `shortName` and `setInitialConditions` are abstract, so that every setup must define both. The constructor takes the code units, 1 by default. `initialConditions` wraps the work of a setup. It sets the code units of the simulation and calls `setInitialConditions`, which chooses the equation of state, gravity and domain and writes the primitive variables. It stops if any physical cell has a density or pressure that is not positive. It then passes the update and boundary functions of the setup to the simulation where the setup overrides them, initialises the gravity, converts to conserved variables and fills the boundaries. The other methods have defaults that do nothing. `trueSolution` returns a known solution for the plots in one dimension, an array or None for each quantity, in code units. `plotLimits` returns lower and upper limits and logarithmic scales, `plotColorMaps` colour maps, and `updateFunction` and `setBoundaryConditions` act at run time (2.2.3). The base class tells whether a setup overrides the last two by calling them with None: its own versions return False, while an override returns nothing or fails without a simulation. `internalEnergyFromTemperature` converts a temperature into the internal energy in code units of an isothermal gas (2.1.2). `run` passes the solution, the limits and the colour maps on to the plotting functions (3.3).

### 3.7 Performance

As Python is slow, the overhead of the code might take much of the computing time, but all operations on arrays in Ulula go through NumPy and so run in C (§4.3). Fig. 8 measures the processor time per step against the total number of cells, ghost cells included, from 10 to $10^6$, on a MacBook Pro of 2025. It covers one and two dimensions, each between the cheapest problem and scheme, isothermal with the Euler integrator and HLL, and the most expensive, MUSCL–Hancock with HLLC and gravity. The time is well described by an overhead per step plus a constant cost per cell, the costs of the Python framework and of the operations of NumPy. The overhead, about $2\times10^{-4}$ s per step in two dimensions or 5,000 steps per second, matters only for the smallest problems. Beyond them, the time grows linearly at about $4\times10^{-7}$ s per cell in two dimensions, or 0.4 s per million cells, and at about half that in one dimension, which sweeps once instead of twice. The paper concludes that the speed of NumPy limits Ulula. `run` prints the processor time per step and the steps per second at the end of a run (3.3). **[GK]** The time per cell and step in two dimensions corresponds to $2.5\times10^6$ cell updates per second on one processor, and the time per cell and sweep, $2\times10^{-7}$ s, is the same in one and two dimensions.
