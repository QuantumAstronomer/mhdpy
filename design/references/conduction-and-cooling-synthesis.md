# Synthesis of conduction and cooling

This synthesis digests a collection of papers on thermal conduction and radiative cooling in astrophysical gas, kept in `reference-works/Conduction and Cooling/Docs`. The collection is gathered around [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, [arXiv:1608.06290](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.06290)), a study of how clouds of a galactic fountain make the hot corona of their galaxy cool and condense, with thermal conduction included (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper of the collection is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local copy, as in [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §3, eq. 10), and its arXiv identifier, or else its DOI or ADS bibcode, is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

In star-forming galaxies, stellar feedback can both suppress and stimulate the accretion of gas from the circumgalactic medium ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, abstract). It stimulates accretion through gas of the disc that it ejects into the halo as fountain clouds, which interact with the hot corona around the galaxy. Where the cold, metal-rich gas of the disc, at $T \lesssim 10^4$ K, mixes with the hot gas of the corona, at $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K, the cooling time of part of the corona can fall steeply, so that this part condenses and is accreted. Accretion driven by the fountain had been studied only under the conditions of the Milky Way (§1). The paper extends the two-dimensional simulations of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)), which followed a cold cloud through the corona of the Milky Way, to haloes whose coronae have different temperatures and so span a wide range of virial masses. Its simulations, at a resolution of parsecs, add thermal conduction, which the earlier ones lacked. Conduction may be a key process for the efficiency of the condensation, as it may slow the condensation down or, under some conditions, inhibit it ([Begelman and McKee](#begelman-mckee-1990) 1990; [Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a). 2.8.1 sets out the astrophysical background of the paper, and 2.8.2 the set-up of its simulations.

The simulations show that the condensation of coronal gas depends strongly on the galactic environment ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, abstract). It grows less efficient as the virial temperature and mass of the halo increase, and is fully ineffective for virial masses above $10^{13}\,M_\odot$. The corona thus cools quickly in haloes of low to intermediate virial mass, $M_\mathrm{vir} \lesssim 3 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$, while the ability to cool the corona declines from late-type to early-type disc galaxies. Accretion may then switch off, and star formation be quenched, in massive systems. These virial masses rest on a definition of the virial radius that 2.8.2 qualifies.

The summary of the paper restates its conclusions ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §6). In coronae up to $2 \times 10^6$ K, hydrodynamic instabilities at the interface strip cold gas from the cloud, and it mixes with the hot gas in a turbulent wake, where radiative cooling forms cold cloudlets. Conduction keeps these cloudlets from surviving, as it makes the wake warmer and smooths its gradients of temperature, but it does not inhibit condensation. After 30 to 40 Myr condensation grows nearly exponentially, and after 60 Myr the condensed gas amounts to 18 per cent or more of the initial mass of the cloud. In coronae of $4 \times 10^6$ K and hotter, conduction smooths the gradient of velocity at the interface, which prevents the instabilities and the destruction of the cloud, keeps the cloud compact and hinders mixing. Condensation thus declines as the corona gets hotter, through radiative cooling, which dominates in cooler coronae, and conduction, which grows more efficient in hotter ones. As the coronal temperature tracks the virial mass, the ability of a galaxy to cool its corona falls with the mass of its halo: it is high below $3 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ and nil above $10^{13}\,M_\odot$. Condensation driven by fountain clouds may thus have sustained star formation in galaxies like the Milky Way after cold-mode accretion stopped, and its failure in massive galaxies may explain the quenching of star formation by mass and by environment (2.8.7).

### 2.1 Isotropic conduction and its suppression by magnetic fields

#### 2.1.1 The Spitzer conductivity

Thermal conduction transfers energy down a gradient of temperature: at the interface between two phases of gas, hot electrons carry heat into the colder medium, and the gradient is smoothed ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.1). In a fully ionised plasma, the classical theory of [Spitzer](#spitzer-1962) (1962) gives the heat flux $\mathbf{q}_\mathrm{class} = -\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T$ (eq. 1), with the conductivity $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp} = 1.84 \times 10^{-5}\,T^{5/2}/\ln\Psi$ erg s$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$ cm$^{-1}$ (eq. 2). Its Coulomb logarithm is $\ln\Psi = 29.7 + \ln[(T_\mathrm{e}/10^6\,\mathrm{K})/(n_\mathrm{e}/\mathrm{cm}^{-3})^{1/2}]$, with $n_\mathrm{e}$ the density and $T_\mathrm{e}$ the temperature of the electrons (eq. 3). [Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977, [1977ApJ...211..135C](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977ApJ...211..135C/abstract), §IIa, eq. 2) give this logarithm for $T > 4.2 \times 10^5$ K. In many astrophysical settings the classical theory does not apply directly, and conduction is much less efficient than the Spitzer value.

#### 2.1.2 Suppression by tangled magnetic fields

In a magnetic field, the conducting electrons move along the field lines rather than isotropically, so that conduction across the field is strongly reduced ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.1). The effect of a tangled field is usually modelled by multiplying the Spitzer flux by a dimensionless factor $f \lesssim 1$, so that $\mathbf{q} = -f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T$ (eq. 4). For a tangled field, [Rechester and Rosenbluth](#rechester-rosenbluth-1978) (1978) and [Chandran and Cowley](#chandran-cowley-1998) (1998) estimated a coefficient 100 to 1000 times below the Spitzer value. [Narayan and Medvedev](#narayan-medvedev-2001) (2001) found that if the turbulence spans a wide range of length scales, as strong or intermediate MHD turbulence may, the efficiency depends on the ratio of the smallest scale of the turbulence to the scale of the structure. The factor $f$ then grows as the smallest scale shrinks, and converges to a maximum of about 0.2. **[GK]** [Narayan and Medvedev](#narayan-medvedev-2001) (2001, [arXiv:astro-ph/0110567](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0110567), §2) derive the reduction by 100 to 1000 for a tangled field with a single coherence length, after [Rechester and Rosenbluth](#rechester-rosenbluth-1978) (1978) and [Chandran and Cowley](#chandran-cowley-1998) (1998), and find a conductivity of about $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}/5$ once the turbulence spans at least two decades in scale.

The simulations contain no magnetic field, and $f$ stands in for its effect on conduction ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2.1). The maximum of 0.2 of [Narayan and Medvedev](#narayan-medvedev-2001) (2001) holds for a single phase of plasma, whereas the simulations concern the interface between two fluids, cloud and corona, with different magnetic fields. There, conduction can be reduced further if magnetic reconnection, which breaks and reconnects field lines of the two fluids, is inefficient ([Biskamp](#biskamp-2000) 2000; [Priest and Forbes](#priest-forbes-2000) 2000). Efficient reconnection lets the conducting electrons carry heat from the hot medium to the cold one, while inefficient reconnection keeps them in their own fluid and suppresses conduction strongly. The role of reconnection in a chaotic field is hard to trace and lies beyond the paper, which fixes $f = 0.1$ as a compromise between the upper limit of Narayan and Medvedev and a possible reduction by inefficient reconnection. Sim. 9 tests $f = 0.2$ (2.8.2, 2.8.6).

### 2.2 Saturated conduction

#### 2.2.1 The saturated heat flux

[Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977, §IIa) derive an upper bound to the heat flux that the electrons can carry. The Spitzer conductivity holds in a fully ionised hydrogen plasma with many collisions over the scale of the temperature (eqs. 1, 2; 2.1.1). It already includes the electric field that keeps the electric current at zero, which reduces it by a factor of about 0.4, and it is proportional to the mean free path $\lambda$ for the exchange of energy between electrons: $\kappa = 1.31\,n_\mathrm{e}k\lambda(kT_\mathrm{e}/m_\mathrm{e})^{1/2}$, with $\lambda \approx 10^4\,T^2/n$ cm (eqs. 3 to 5). The classical flux $\mathbf{q} = -\kappa\nabla T$ (eq. 6) thus scales as $\lambda/L_T$, where $L_T = T/|\nabla T|$ is the scale height of the temperature, and the classical theory assumes $\lambda \ll L_T$. Where the mean free path becomes comparable to $L_T$ or larger, the flux no longer equals $-\kappa\nabla T$, an effect that they call saturation.

The maximum flux can be written as $(3/2)n_\mathrm{e}kT_\mathrm{e}v_\mathrm{char}$, with a characteristic velocity of the order of the thermal velocity of the electrons ([Parker](#parker-1963) 1963), but a suitable distribution function can make $v_\mathrm{char}$ arbitrarily large ([Manheimer and Klein](#manheimer-klein-1975) 1975) ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IIa). Its actual value is set by the source of heat and by two constraints on the distribution of the electrons: it must carry no current, and it must be stable against the plasma instabilities that drive an anisotropic distribution towards isotropy. The estimate of [Parker](#parker-1963) (1963), $v_\mathrm{char} = (3kT_\mathrm{e}/m_\mathrm{e})^{1/2}$, has the electrons stream past the ions at the thermal velocity, which violates the first constraint and is unstable against the ion-acoustic instability. [Morse and Nielson](#morse-nielson-1973) (1973) found, for laser-heated plasmas, a maximum of $v_\mathrm{char} = 0.66(kT/m_\mathrm{e})^{1/2}$ for a distribution without current, while the simulations of [Manheimer and Klein](#manheimer-klein-1975) (1975), relaxed to a steady state, gave a value several times smaller. For a Maxwellian source of heat, Cowie and McKee bound the flux by that of a Maxwellian gas facing an infinite gradient, a hot gas abutting a cold absorber: the free-molecular flux, with $v_\mathrm{char} = (8/9\pi)^{1/2}(kT/m_\mathrm{e})^{1/2}$. Reduced by the same factor of 0.4 as the classical flux, for the electric field that stops the current, this gives the saturated flux $q_\mathrm{sat} = 0.4\,(2kT_\mathrm{e}/\pi m_\mathrm{e})^{1/2}n_\mathrm{e}kT_\mathrm{e}$ (eq. 7). It depends little on the distribution function, falling by only about 10 per cent for a uniform one. It lies 8.1 times below the estimate of Parker and 3.1 times below that of Morse and Nielson, and is comparable with that of Manheimer and Klein.

To allow for its uncertainty, Cowie and McKee write the saturated flux as $q_\mathrm{sat} = 5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3 = 5\Phi_\mathrm{s}cp$, with $c^2 = p/\rho$ the isothermal sound speed, $p$ the pressure, $\rho$ the density and $\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ a factor of order unity ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IIa, eq. 8). For equal temperatures of electrons and ions in a fully ionised gas of cosmic abundances, eq. 7 gives $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1.1$; for $T_\mathrm{e} \neq T_\mathrm{i}$, $\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ takes a factor $[2T_\mathrm{e}/(T_\mathrm{e} + T_\mathrm{i})]^{3/2}$. Plasma turbulence and magnetic fields can reduce $q_\mathrm{sat}$. Some turbulence is expected at saturation, to keep the electrons nearly isotropic, as the estimate already assumes, and stronger turbulence that cuts the effective mean free path well below $L_T$ lowers $\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ correspondingly. Both fluxes apply only along a magnetic field, since conduction across it is set by the gyroradius and is negligible in most astrophysical problems. For a uniform field at an angle $\theta$ to the gradient of temperature, the classical flux along the gradient falls by $\cos^2\theta$: one factor projects the gradient onto the field, the other projects the flux along the field back onto the gradient. The saturated flux, independent of the gradient, falls by the second factor alone, which enters $\Phi_\mathrm{s}$. The ratio of the classical to the saturated flux is $\sigma = \kappa T_\mathrm{e}/(5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3L_T) = 4.6\lambda/(\Phi_\mathrm{s}L_T)$, for $T_\mathrm{e} = T_\mathrm{i}$ and helium as a tenth of the particles (eqs. 9, 10). The flux passes from classical ($\sigma \ll 1$) to saturated ($\sigma \gg 1$) near $\sigma \sim 1$, where $\lambda \sim 0.2\Phi_\mathrm{s}L_T$, so that the gas is still dominated by collisions when saturation sets in.

In the solar wind, observations show a heat flux below the classical one, and models with classical conduction give electron temperatures above those observed ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IIb). In the model of Cowie and McKee, instabilities chiefly make the electrons isotropic, while the flux along the field is about $q_\mathrm{sat}$ of eq. 7. Averages of the data of the satellite Vela 4 give $n_\mathrm{e} = 7$ cm$^{-3}$, $T_\mathrm{e} = 1.4 \times 10^5$ K and a heat flux of $7 \times 10^{-3}$ erg cm$^{-2}$ s$^{-1}$, against $6.3 \times 10^{-3}$ from eq. 7. The quiet solar wind, slower than 350 km s$^{-1}$, gives 8.3 cm$^{-3}$, $1.3 \times 10^5$ K and $5 \times 10^{-3}$, against $6.7 \times 10^{-3}$. [Feldman et al.](#feldman-et-al-1973) (1973) gave the most direct evidence of saturation, a ratio $q/nT^{3/2}$ that stays nearly constant while $T$ varies by a factor of 2. With the profiles of the one-fluid model of [Cuperman and Metzler](#cuperman-metzler-1973) (1973), the ratio of the saturated to the classical flux also matches their empirical reduction of the classical conductivity beyond a few solar radii. The saturated flux thus agrees with the solar wind, although a full two-fluid calculation with it is still needed. **[GK]** Eq. 7 reproduces both computed fluxes. Cowie and McKee attribute the averages to two papers by Montgomery of 1972 in J. Geophys. Res. 77, but the pages they give, 5503 and 5432, belong to a paper of Hones et al. on the magnetotail and to one of Montgomery, Bame and Hundhausen on high-speed streams, so that the source of the averages could not be identified.

#### 2.2.2 Evaporation of a spherical cloud

Conduction had seldom been thought important for the interstellar gas, which seemed too cool and dense for it, until absorption lines of O VI and the soft X-ray background revealed gas near $10^6$ K, whose evolution conduction must dominate, since the classical flux rises as $T^{7/2}$ at a fixed scale of the temperature ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §I). One of its most important effects is the evaporation of clouds. Earlier solutions were particular and mostly numerical, and hard to scale to other temperatures and densities. Cowie and McKee therefore seek analytic solutions for a spherical cloud in a hot, tenuous gas, in the first of three papers: this one neglects radiation and magnetic fields, and the second and third were to add them.

[Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977, §III) consider a spherical cloud of radius $R$ in a hot gas of density $n_f$ and temperature $T_f$ far from the cloud, neglecting radiation, ionisation and magnetic fields. They seek time-independent rates of mass loss, $\dot m = 4\pi r^2\rho v$ (eq. 11), as eigenvalues of the conservation of energy, $\nabla\cdot[\rho\mathbf{v}(v^2/2 + 5c^2/2)] + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q} = 0$ (eq. 12), with $T \to T_f$ far away and $T \approx 0$ at the surface of the cloud. Such solutions exist only for spheres, and plane or cylindrical clouds need time-dependent methods. Where the classical flux holds throughout, the enthalpy and heat fluxes vanish at the cold surface, and integrating eq. 12 gives $(5/2)\dot m gc^2 = 4\pi r^2\kappa\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}r$, with $g = 1 + M^2/5$ for the Mach number $M = v/c$ (eqs. 17, 18). For $g$ near 1, the temperature follows $\tau = (1 - y^{-1})^{2/5}$, with $y = r/R$ and $\tau = T/T_f$ (eq. 20). The cloud then loses mass at the classical rate $\dot m_\mathrm{class} = 16\pi\mu\kappa_fR/(25k) = 2.75 \times 10^4\,T_f^{5/2}R_\mathrm{pc}(30/\ln\Lambda)$ g s$^{-1}$, with $\mu$ the mean mass per particle, $\kappa_f$ the conductivity at $T_f$ and $R_\mathrm{pc}$ the radius in parsecs (eqs. 16, 21). It evaporates in $t_\mathrm{evap} = 3.3 \times 10^{20}\,\bar n_\mathrm{c}R_\mathrm{pc}^2T_f^{-5/2}(\ln\Lambda/30)$ yr, with $\bar n_\mathrm{c}$ its mean density of hydrogen and helium as a tenth of the particles (eq. 22).

The energy equation can be integrated apart from the motion because the flow is not highly supersonic ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IIIb). With classical conduction, the ratio of the classical to the saturated flux is $\sigma = Mg/(2\Phi_\mathrm{s})$, so that saturation would set in at a Mach number $2\Phi_\mathrm{s}/g$ of order unity (eq. 23). The equation of motion then gives the flow, whose velocity, pressure and inward heat flux are extremal where $T/r^2$ peaks, at $y = 1.2$, the sonic point of transonic flow (eqs. 24 to 27, Fig. 1). The evaporation is steady if the gas crosses the interface, to $y = 1.5$, in a time $t_\mathrm{flow} = 1.70 \times 4\pi R^3\rho_f/\dot m$ short compared with $t_\mathrm{evap}$, which for a cloud in pressure equilibrium requires $\rho_\mathrm{c} \gtrsim 5\rho_f$ (eqs. 28, 29).

The classical solution fails where its flux would exceed the saturated one ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IV). In it, $\sigma$ vanishes at the cloud and far away and peaks in the interface (eq. 30), and saturation sets in when the global parameter $\sigma_0$ of 2.2.4 exceeds about 1. Saturation lowers the evaporation, but the rate cannot fall below $8\pi R^2\rho_fc_f\Phi_\mathrm{s}$, since the heat flux integrated over the cloud is at least $5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho_fc_f^3\,4\pi R^2$. The hydrodynamic equations still hold when the mean free path is long, provided the plasma stays nearly isotropic, which plasma instabilities are assumed to ensure; in the solar wind, the pressure is anisotropic by less than 20 per cent (§IVa). The interface then has three zones (§IVb, Fig. 2). An inner classical zone, from the surface to $y_\mathrm{s1}$, is cool enough for its conductivity to stay low. A saturated zone follows up to $y_\mathrm{s2}$, and an outer classical zone beyond it, where the gradient is small. The flux switches abruptly between its classical and saturated forms at the zone boundaries. Balancing the outward enthalpy flux and the inward heat flux at $y_\mathrm{s2}$ gives $\dot m = 4\pi R^2\rho_fc_f[(2\Phi_\mathrm{s}/g)(p_\mathrm{s2}/p_f)(c_f/c_\mathrm{s2})y_\mathrm{s2}^2]$ (eq. 35), in which $y_\mathrm{s2}^2$ reflects the larger effective area of the interface.

In the saturated zone, the Mach number is constant, $M_\mathrm{s}(1 + M_\mathrm{s}^2/5) = 2\Phi_\mathrm{s}$, and the profiles are power laws, $\tau \propto y^{4/(1+M_\mathrm{s}^2)}$, $p \propto y^{-2M_\mathrm{s}^2/(1+M_\mathrm{s}^2)}$ and $\rho \propto y^{-2(2+M_\mathrm{s}^2)/(1+M_\mathrm{s}^2)}$ ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IVc, eqs. 36 to 39). No such steady solution exists in plane geometry. For $\Phi_\mathrm{s} \gtrsim 0.6$ the flow in the saturated zone is supersonic and ends in a weak shock in the outer zone, which they neglect. Matching the classical profiles of the inner and outer zones, $\tau = w^{2/5}(1 - y^{-1})^{2/5}$ and $\tau = (1 - w/y)^{2/5}$ with $w = \dot m/\dot m_\mathrm{class}$, to the saturated one at the zone boundaries fixes $w$, $y_\mathrm{s1}$ and $y_\mathrm{s2}$ (§IVd, eqs. 42 to 53). For subsonic flow, the gradients of temperature and of Mach number must be continuous at $y_\mathrm{s1}$, which places $y_\mathrm{s1} = (11 + M_\mathrm{s}^2)/10$ at the maximum of $M$. For supersonic flow, $y_\mathrm{s1}$ lies where the unique transonic solution reaches $M_\mathrm{s}$. In all cases $w \leq 1$: saturation reduces evaporation below the classical rate. Saturated evaporation begins at $w = 1$, where $\sigma_0$ is of order unity, 1.95 for $M_\mathrm{s} \approx 2\Phi_\mathrm{s} \ll 1$ and 1.08 for $M_\mathrm{s}^2 = 2$ and $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$ (eq. 54).

For highly saturated evaporation, $\sigma_0 \gg 1$, the outer zone is nearly isothermal, the pressure at $y_\mathrm{s2}$ is $p_\mathrm{s2} = p_f\exp(-M_\mathrm{s}^2/2)$, and the equations give $w = [\sigma_0^{-5}H^{1+M_\mathrm{s}^2}\exp(-2.5M_\mathrm{s}^2)]^{1/(6+M_\mathrm{s}^2)}$ ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IVe, eqs. 55 to 59). Here $H$ depends on $M_\mathrm{s}$, from about 28 for slow subsonic flow to about 11.5 for supersonic flow (eqs. 50, 51; Fig. 3). This approximation agrees with numerical solutions within 30 per cent even at the onset of saturation. Cowie and McKee therefore write the rate generally as $\dot m = 4\pi R^2\rho_fc_f\Phi_\mathrm{s}F(\sigma_0)$, with $F = 2\sigma_0$ in the classical case and $F = 2[(\sigma_0H)^{1+M_\mathrm{s}^2}\exp(-2.5M_\mathrm{s}^2)]^{1/(6+M_\mathrm{s}^2)}$ in the saturated one (eqs. 60 to 62). $F$ is of order unity near $\sigma_0 \sim 1$ and depends only weakly on $M_\mathrm{s}$ and $\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ (Fig. 4). Numerically, $\dot m = 3.25 \times 10^{18}\,n_fT_f^{1/2}R_\mathrm{pc}^2\Phi_\mathrm{s}F(\sigma_0)$ g s$^{-1}$, with $F = 2.73\sigma_0^{3/8}$ for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$, and the cloud evaporates in $t_\mathrm{evap} = 2.8 \times 10^6\,(\bar n_\mathrm{c}/n_f)R_\mathrm{pc}/[T_f^{1/2}\Phi_\mathrm{s}F(\sigma_0)]$ yr (eqs. 63, 64). In the example of Fig. 2, with $\sigma_0 = 170$ and $M_\mathrm{s} = 1$, the saturated zone spans 1.2 to 2.3 cloud radii, and the gradient of temperature jumps at $y_\mathrm{s2}$ only because the form of the flux switches abruptly there. For highly saturated evaporation the pressure in the cloud can well exceed the ambient one, $p_\mathrm{c}/p_f = 2.43\sigma_0^{1/4}$ for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$ (eq. 66).

The theory assumes steady evaporation, equal temperatures of electrons and ions, and a mean free path that is not too long ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §IVf). The hot electrons must not penetrate the saturated zone into the inner classical one, which holds up to $\sigma_0 \approx 100\,\Phi_\mathrm{s}^{-1}$; beyond it the results should stay qualitatively right until the range of the electrons approaches the column density of the cloud, near $\sigma_0 \approx 15\bar n_\mathrm{c}/(n_f\Phi_\mathrm{s})$. Evaporation is steady if $t_\mathrm{flow} \approx Ry_\mathrm{s2}/(M_\mathrm{s}c_f)$ is shorter than $t_\mathrm{evap}$, which for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$ requires $\bar n_\mathrm{c}/n_f \gtrsim 12\sigma_0^{0.56}$ (eqs. 68, 69). The ion temperature falls below that of the electrons within the saturated zone, which raises the efficiency of evaporation, roughly as if $\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ were multiplied by $2^{3/2}$. In summary, the classical rate of eq. 21 holds for $\sigma_0 \lesssim 1$, with generally subsonic flow and a cloud pressure only somewhat above the ambient one. For $\sigma_0 \gtrsim 1$ the flux saturates in the outer interface, the rate follows eq. 63, the flow in the saturated zone has a constant Mach number, and the pressure in the cloud can well exceed the ambient pressure, the theory being accurate up to $\sigma_0 \approx 100/\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ (§VII). [McKee and Cowie](#mckee-cowie-1977) (1977) were to show that radiative losses stop evaporation for $\sigma_0 \lesssim 0.03/\Phi_\mathrm{s}$, so that classical evaporation spans about as wide a range of $\sigma_0$ as the saturated evaporation that the theory treats accurately, and a third paper was to show that magnetic fields do not significantly inhibit evaporation under a wide range of conditions. **[GK]** The third paper of the series, [Balbus and McKee](#balbus-mckee-1982) (1982), treats suprathermal evaporation instead.

#### 2.2.3 Conduction fronts and clouds in supernova remnants

[McKee and Cowie](#mckee-cowie-1975) (1975) had described evaporation as a conduction front advancing into the cooler gas, from the conservation of mass and momentum alone ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §V). As for ionisation fronts, the front moves relative to the gas ahead of it either slower than $c_\mathrm{c}^2/(2c_f)$, a D-type front with rarefaction, or faster than $2c_f$, an R-type front with compression, and fronts are weak or strong according to their jump in density. Including the conservation of energy and spherical divergence gives the velocity of the front, $v_\mathrm{cond} = \dot m/(4\pi R^2\rho_\mathrm{c})$ (eq. 70). For classical evaporation it is $2\sigma_0\Phi_\mathrm{s}(p_f/p_\mathrm{c})(c_\mathrm{c}^2/c_f) \lesssim c_\mathrm{c}^2/c_f$, so that classical conduction fronts are D-type (eq. 71). Saturated fronts are about D-critical, with $v_\mathrm{cond} = 1.42\sigma_0^{1/8}c_\mathrm{c}^2/c_f$ for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$ (eq. 72). Both follow from a cloud much denser than its surroundings: an R-type front would leave a layer of very high pressure near the cloud, which would expand and relax to a D-type front. Even without that assumption, saturation limits one-dimensional, non-radiative R-type fronts to velocities near $2c_f$, $1.86 \times 2c_f$ for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$, and permits none for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} < 0.2$ (eq. 73). In a strong point explosion such as a supernova remnant, conduction is saturated early on, and a classical flux would wrongly give a weak R-type front far ahead of the blast wave ([Chevalier](#chevalier-1975a) 1975a). With the saturated flux, the front keeps close to the shock, and the volume of heated gas stays about that of the usual blast wave.

The theory assumes an isotropic hot gas and neglects its dynamics, which does not hold for clouds overtaken by the shock of a supernova remnant, out of pressure equilibrium and with bow shocks and convective transfer of energy ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977, §VI). The shock crosses the cloud quickly compared with the evaporation time, but slowly compared with the flow time through the interface, so that the cloud can be treated as momentarily at rest. The flow of hot gas past the cloud bends the magnetic field around it, which reduces evaporation, and it can mix the cloud turbulently into the hot gas, which enlarges the evaporating surface and tangles the field. Convection can also steepen the gradient of temperature: for a flow at a fraction $\delta < 1$ of the sound speed it keeps $T \approx T_f$ beyond $y \approx 1 + (\sigma_0/\delta)^{1/2}$, which raises the classical rate to $\dot m_\mathrm{class}(\sigma_0/\delta)^{-1/2}$ for $\sigma_0 \ll \delta$, or $F = 2(\sigma_0\delta)^{1/2}$ (eq. 74), while for $\delta \lesssim \sigma_0$ the classical rate holds. Since radiative losses quench evaporation for $\sigma_0 \lesssim 0.03\Phi_\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, the gain is at most a factor of about 5. For saturated evaporation convection matters little, as the flow behind the shock is subsonic while the evaporation proceeds near the sound speed, and the rate from the front of the cloud stays at least $4\pi R^2\rho_fc_f$, with $\rho_f$ and $c_f$ just behind the bow shock, which corresponds to $\Phi_\mathrm{s}F = 1$.

#### 2.2.4 The global saturation parameter

[Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977, §IV, eq. 31) collect the constants of the local ratio in the classical solution into the global saturation parameter $\sigma_0 = (2/25)\kappa_fT_f/(\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho_fc_f^3R)$. It equals $1.84\lambda_f/(R\Phi_\mathrm{s})$, with $\lambda_f$ the mean free path in the ambient gas, and for $\ln\Lambda = 30$ becomes $\sigma_0 = (T_f/1.54 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{K})^2/(n_fR_\mathrm{pc}\Phi_\mathrm{s})$ (eq. 32). As the maximum of $\sigma$ in the classical interface is of order unity times $\sigma_0$, saturation sets in at $\sigma_0 \gtrsim 1$ (2.2.2). **[GK]** For fully ionised gas with helium as a tenth of the particles, $n_f$ the density of hydrogen and $\ln\Lambda = 30$, eq. 31 gives a constant of $1.52 \times 10^7$ K in eq. 32, close to the printed value, and eq. 7 gives $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1.11$.

Conduction grows steeply with temperature, as its conductivity is proportional to $T^{5/2}$ (eq. 2), so that small differences of temperature give large differences in the heat exchanged, and cold structures evaporate much faster at high temperatures ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.4). Saturation, where the temperature changes on scales below the mean free path of the electrons, can temper this. The paper of 2016 measures it for a structure of size $l$ by $\sigma_0 = (T_\mathrm{cor}/1.54 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{K})^2\,f/(\Phi_\mathrm{s}n_\mathrm{cor}l)$, with $n_\mathrm{cor}$ in cm$^{-3}$ and $l$ in parsecs (eq. 13), which is eq. 32 of Cowie and McKee with the classical flux reduced by $f$. Saturation becomes significant for $\sigma_0 \gtrsim 1$ ([Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) 1977; [Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, [1993ApJ...404..625D](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJ...404..625D/abstract)). In the simulations it is negligible for the cloud as a whole, with $\sigma_0 \ll 1$ for $l = R_\mathrm{cl} = 100$ pc, and only at $T_\mathrm{cor} = 8 \times 10^6$ K does $\sigma_0$ approach 1, so that saturation might slightly influence the evolution of the cloud. For the cold cloudlets of the wake, $\sigma_0 \propto l^{-1}$ is larger, and saturation matters more as the corona gets hotter. **[GK]** With $f = 0.1$ and $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{f}$, eq. 13 gives $\sigma_0 = 0.013$, 0.053, 0.21 and 0.85 for $l = 100$ pc at coronal temperatures of 1, 2, 4 and $8 \times 10^6$ K. Unlike the local ratio of eq. 7 of the paper of 2016, it contains $f$, as the ratio of the reduced classical flux to the saturated one (2.2.7).

#### 2.2.5 A smooth transition between the regimes

[Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) (1993, abstract, §1) give an analytic solution for the saturated evaporation of a spherical cloud in which the conductivity passes continuously from its diffusive to its saturated form, as numerical calculations usually treat it. Interest in interstellar conduction began with the absorption lines of O VI that the satellite Copernicus found along many lines of sight ([Jenkins and Meloy](#jenkins-meloy-1974) 1974), which point to gas at 3 to $5 \times 10^5$ K, a range in which gas cools very fast. Interfaces between hot gas and embedded cool clouds offer a source of such gas, heated through this range rather than cooled, and the three-phase model of the interstellar medium of [McKee and Ostriker](#mckee-ostriker-1977) (1977) made the evaporation of clouds a key process. Strictly, saturation marks a breakdown of the local transport of heat, but a flux-limited form simplifies the analysis and, used with care, agrees well with more elaborate treatments ([Max, McKee and Mead](#max-et-al-1980) 1980). Where [Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977) join three zones under prescribed conditions (2.2.2), numerical calculations commonly take the harmonic mean of the diffusive and the saturated flux, which tends to the smaller of the two where they differ greatly ([Balbus and McKee](#balbus-mckee-1982) 1982). With it, Dalton and Balbus solve the problem of Cowie and McKee in closed form, for a spherical, non-magnetic and non-radiative cloud, with flow of low Mach number at constant pressure. The solution can serve as a benchmark for numerical calculations and justifies the three zones as asymptotic expansions. Its conductivity is lower than that of Cowie and McKee, locally by up to a factor of 2, which changes the profiles of temperature that matter for the radiative losses of the front. Turbulence and supersonic ablation, which other works study, are left aside.

The classical flux is $q_\mathrm{cl} = -\kappa\nabla T$, with the conductivity and Coulomb logarithm of Spitzer (2.1.1), the temperatures of electrons and ions being nearly equal in flows of low Mach number ([Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, §2, eqs. 1, 2). The saturated flux is $|q_\mathrm{sat}| = 5\phi\rho c^3$, with $\phi \lesssim 1$ (eq. 3). They normalise $\phi$ so that $\phi = 1$ gives the flux of a plasma of cosmic abundances abutting a boundary at zero temperature, reduced for zero current as the diffusive flux is: the flux of eq. 7 of Cowie and McKee, for which these give $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1.1$ (2.2.1). The saturated flux points exactly against the gradient of temperature and serves as an upper limit to the flux. With the ratio $\sigma = |q_\mathrm{cl}/q_\mathrm{sat}| = |\kappa\,(\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}r)/(5\phi\rho c^3)|$ (eq. 4), the effective flux is the harmonic mean, $1/q = 1/q_\mathrm{sat} + 1/q_\mathrm{cl}$, or $q = -\kappa\,(\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}r)/(1 + \sigma)$ (eqs. 5a, 5b), introduced by [Balbus and McKee](#balbus-mckee-1982) (1982) and since a standard parameterisation. It reduces to the classical flux for $\sigma \ll 1$ and to the saturated one for $\sigma \gg 1$.

For subsonic flow at constant pressure, $P = \rho kT/\mu$, and constant rate, $\dot m = 4\pi r^2\rho v$, the conservation of energy integrates to $(5/2)\rho vr^2c^2 = -r^2q$, with $c^2 = kT/\mu$ the isothermal sound speed, the kinetic energy neglected and the constant of integration zero for a cold cloud ([Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, §2.1, eqs. 6 to 9). With $y = r/R$ and $\tau = T/T_f$, the conditions $\tau \to 0$ as $y \to 1$ and $\tau \to 1$ as $y \to \infty$ make $w = \dot m/\dot m_\mathrm{cl}$ an eigenvalue, with $\dot m_\mathrm{cl} = 16\pi\mu R\kappa_f/(25k)$ the classical rate (§2.2, eqs. 10 to 15). The constants of the equation form the saturation parameter of Cowie and McKee, $\sigma_0 = 2\kappa_fT_f/(25\phi\rho_fc_f^3R)$ (eq. 16; 2.2.4), the global counterpart of $\sigma$. With all quantities at $T_f$, $\sigma_0 = (2/5)(L_T/R)\sigma$, and since $\sigma = 4.6\lambda/(\phi L_T)$, $\sigma_0 \sim \lambda_f/(\phi R)$: evaporation saturates where the mean free path in the hot gas much exceeds the radius of the cloud (eqs. 17 to 19). Written for $y(\tau)$, the equation is of Riccati type, $\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}\tau - (5/2)\tau^{3/2}y^2/w = -(5/2)\sigma_0\tau^2$, and the substitution $y = g'/(ag)$, with $a = -(5/2)\tau^{3/2}/w$, turns it into a linear equation solved by modified Bessel functions, $g = \tau^{5/4}[AI_{5/11}(\chi\tau^{11/4}) + BI_{-5/11}(\chi\tau^{11/4})]$, with $\chi = (10/11)(\sigma_0/w)^{1/2}$ (eqs. 20 to 25). The boundary conditions fix the constants and give $\sigma_0 = 2^{-1/11}(11/10)[\Gamma(5/11)/\Gamma(6/11)]\chi^{12/11}I_{5/11}(\chi)/I_{-5/11}(\chi)$ (eqs. 26 to 30; App. A). This fixes $w$ through $\sigma_0$ alone, that is through the state of the ambient gas and the radius of the cloud, and the rate drops steeply as $\sigma_0$ rises from below 1 to well above it (Fig. 1). The profile of temperature $y(\tau)$ then follows in closed form, with modified Bessel functions of both kinds (eq. 31).

For small $\sigma_0$ the solution recovers the classical profile $\tau = (1 - y^{-1})^{2/5}$ of Cowie and McKee ([Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, §2.2, eq. 32). For large $\chi$, highly saturated evaporation, the asymptotic forms of the Bessel functions divide the profile into three regimes that correspond to the three zones of Cowie and McKee (§2.2.1 to §2.2.3). Near the cloud, $y = 1 + \tau^{5/2}/w$, the limit of their inner classical zone, $\tau = w^{2/5}(y - 1)^{2/5}$, for $\tau \ll 1$ (eqs. 39a, 39b). At intermediate temperatures, $\tau = y^4/(\sigma_0^2w^2)$, their saturated zone in its subsonic limit (eqs. 40, 41). As $\tau \to 1$, $\tau = 1 - 2w/(5y)$, the expansion of their outer zone, $\tau = (1 - w/y)^{2/5}$, for $y \gg 1$ (eqs. 45b, 46).

For $\sigma_0 = 35$ the analytic profile matches the approximations of Cowie and McKee closely, so that from about this value on evaporation is well described by the saturated flux, while for $\sigma_0 = 10$ it differs significantly ([Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, §2.3, Fig. 2). The classical profile rises very steeply at the surface of the cloud and then approaches $\tau = 1$ slowly, so that warm evaporated gas reaches far into the surroundings, in a thick interface (Fig. 3). The saturated profile rises less steeply near $y = 1$ but reaches its plateau much sooner: strong saturation insulates the cloud from the hot gas and thins the interface. As the theory breaks down for $\sigma_0 \gtrsim 100/\phi$, where the mean free path exceeds the thickness of the interface (2.2.2), the models stop at $\sigma_0 = 200$. For $\sigma_0 \lesssim 1$ the profiles keep the classical character, an abrupt rise near the cloud and a slow approach to the ambient temperature. As $\sigma_0$ grows past unity, the abrupt rise moves away from the cloud and the plateau closer to it (Fig. 4). Highly saturated evaporation thus gives a thinner layer of warm gas, which absorbs in the ultraviolet, than classical evaporation would. The flow stays subsonic throughout: for $P/k = 10^4$ cm$^{-3}$ K, $\mu = 0.61\,m_\mathrm{H}$, $T_f = 7 \times 10^5$ K and $R = 1$ pc, the Mach number is $M = 0.66\,\tau^{1/2}w/y^2$, below 1 everywhere and falling as $\sigma_0$ rises (App. B, eq. B5, Fig. 8).

Dalton and Balbus use the profiles to compute the ionisation of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, neon and silicon in the interface, neglecting the effect of radiative losses on the flow and the losses from ionising the outer cloud ([Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, §3.1). Collisional ionisation equilibrium does not suit typical interstellar conditions ([Ballet, Arnaud and Rothenflug](#ballet-et-al-1986) 1986). Equilibrium needs the time to ionise, $t_\mathrm{ion} \sim [n_\mathrm{e}(T_\mathrm{max})C^\mathrm{ion}(T_\mathrm{max})]^{-1}$ at the temperature where an ion is most abundant, to be short compared with the time to flow, $t_\mathrm{flow} \sim R/(Mc)$. For a cloud of 1 pc at $P/k = 10^4$ this requires $C^\mathrm{ion}(T_\mathrm{max})/10^{-10} \gg M$, which rarely holds (eqs. 49, 50). They therefore integrate the fractional abundances $X_i$ of the stages of ionisation, $\mathrm{d}X_i/\mathrm{d}t = n_\mathrm{e}[C^\mathrm{ion}_{i-1}X_{i-1} - (C^\mathrm{ion}_i + C^\mathrm{rec}_i)X_i + C^\mathrm{rec}_{i+1}X_{i+1}]$, along the steady flow, with $\mathrm{d}t = \mathrm{d}r/v$ and $v = \dot m/(4\pi r^2\rho)$ (eqs. 47, 48, 51 to 53). The rates combine collisional ionisation with excitation-autoionisation, and radiative with dielectronic recombination, after [Arnaud and Rothenflug](#arnaud-rothenflug-1985) (1985) and [Aldrovandi and Pequignot](#aldrovandi-pequignot-1973) (1973) (eqs. 54a, 54b). The stiff system is integrated with an implicit method of Gear, starting from equilibrium at the temperature of the cloud, set to $0.01\,T_f$, for $T_f = 7 \times 10^5$ K, $\ln\Lambda \approx 31$ and fully ionised hydrogen with a tenth of doubly ionised helium; saturation then matters for clouds smaller than 1 pc. Out of equilibrium, ions survive where equilibrium would have removed them, since the ionisation lags the temperature, which raises their column densities (Fig. 5). A larger $\sigma_0$ lowers $w$, and as all derivatives of the abundances scale as $1/w$, the curves of ionisation steepen, peak more sharply and narrow (§3.2, Fig. 6). The column densities of C IV, N V, O VI and Si IV toward the cloud, integrated to $\tau = 0.99$ with the abundances of [Spitzer](#spitzer-1978) (1978), fall with saturation, by orders of magnitude in some cases (Fig. 7).

The solution depends only on $\sigma_0$, recovering the classical profile for $\sigma_0 \ll 1$ and, for $\sigma_0 \gg 1$, the shape of saturated flow, with an abrupt rise to near the ambient temperature close to the cloud and a thin interface ([Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) 1993, §4). It can be compared with numerical schemes, and with observations, as the warm gas of evaporating clouds can explain the ubiquity of interstellar O VI and of other ions seen in the ultraviolet. [Ballet, Arnaud and Rothenflug](#ballet-et-al-1986) (1986) applied the model of Cowie and McKee to the non-equilibrium ionisation of the interface, and [Böhringer and Hartquist](#bohringer-hartquist-1987) (1987) a dynamically self-consistent numerical model with saturation and radiative losses, and all agree that non-equilibrium is essential. The column densities agree with those of Böhringer and Hartquist within a factor of a few. The exception is O VI at an ambient $5 \times 10^5$ K and a radius of 0.1 pc, where the integration must reach about 80 cloud radii to match their result at 10, since the temperature of the ions lags more; at $10^6$ K or 1 pc the agreement improves greatly. The spherical, non-radiative model oversimplifies, but it shows the importance of saturation for small clouds, and as radiative losses matter less as saturation grows, it becomes more accurate for $\sigma_0 > 1$. Magnetic fields are ignored. A field parallel to the surface of the cloud would inhibit the transport by electrons into it, even if very weak, and so insulate the cloud, but this depends on the geometry. For plane fronts, [Borkowski, Balbus and Fristrom](#borkowski-et-al-1990) (1990) found that the field does not alter the front significantly if its pressure is below the thermal one. #### 2.2.6 Evaporation and condensation in hydrodynamic models

[Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) (2007a, [arXiv:0709.0623](https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.0623), abstract, §1) test the analytic rates of Cowie and McKee under more realistic interstellar conditions with the full hydrodynamic equations. In the three-phase model of [McKee and Ostriker](#mckee-ostriker-1977) (1977), clouds are embedded in a hot, dilute intercloud medium near $10^6$ K and $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$, with which they exchange energy and mass through an interface of steep gradients. Analytic approximations make small clouds in a hot plasma evaporate and live briefly, yet such clouds are observed, high-velocity clouds and cometary globules among them, which suggests that something stabilises them. Earlier work solved the time-independent energy equation, without the changes of mass, heating, mixing and instabilities that a dynamic treatment includes. Vieser and Hensler therefore simulate in two dimensions clouds of different internal structure in a reservoir of hot plasma, with self-gravity, interstellar heating and cooling, and classical and saturated conduction by electrons.

They recall the analytic theory first ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §2). Below $10^4$ K the gas is nearly neutral and conduction by electrons vanishes, while inside the cloud the gradient is too smooth for conduction by neutrals to matter. From the time-independent energy equation, [Graham and Langer](#graham-langer-1973) (1973) found that clouds below a critical radius evaporate while larger ones condense, as their radiative cooling exceeds the heat input, and simulations of clouds in young supernova remnants with the classical flux alone, at up to $10^7$ K, overestimated their mass loss by orders of magnitude ([Chevalier](#chevalier-1975b) 1975b). With radiative cooling, conductive heating is balanced for $\sigma_0 < 0.027/\Phi_\mathrm{s}$ ([McKee and Cowie](#mckee-cowie-1977) 1977). [Begelman and McKee](#begelman-mckee-1990) (1990) showed that heating and cooling dominate structures larger than the Field length $\lambda_\mathrm{F} = [\kappa T/(n^2L_\mathrm{M})]^{1/2}$, with $L_\mathrm{M}$ the larger of the rates of cooling and heating per unit $n^2$, and conduction smaller ones: an isolated cloud loses mass at the classical rate for $R \ll \lambda_\mathrm{F}$ and condenses for $R \approx 0.24$ to $0.36\,\lambda_\mathrm{F}$. The harmonic mean of Dalton and Balbus gives lower rates than Cowie and McKee (Fig. 1). Both theories solve only the energy equation. Yet gas leaving the surface is still cooler than the ambient plasma, since a slight heating already gives it the overpressure that detaches it, and this changes the profile of temperature near the surface and with it the rate of evaporation.

The simulations use a two-dimensional, axisymmetric hydrodynamic code (3.2), in which the energy equation $\partial e/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(e\mathbf{v}) = -P\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} + \Gamma - \Lambda - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}$ holds for the internal energy density $e$, with $P = (\gamma - 1)e$, $\gamma = 5/3$, a heating rate $\Gamma$ and a cooling rate $\Lambda$ ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §3, eqs. 10, 11). The cooling function assumes collisional ionisation equilibrium, combining that of [Böhringer and Hensler](#bohringer-hensler-1989) (1989) above $10^4$ K for solar metallicity with that of [Dalgarno and McCray](#dalgarno-mccray-1972) (1972) below. It resembles the four power laws to which Cowie and McKee reduced the function of [Raymond, Cox and Smith](#raymond-et-al-1976) (1976). Heating comes from cosmic rays ([Black](#black-1987) 1987), X-rays and the photoelectric effect on dust grains ([de Jong](#de-jong-1977) 1977; [de Jong, Dalgarno and Boland](#de-jong-et-al-1980) 1980). The heat flux passes smoothly between the classical and the saturated regimes in the form of [Slavin and Cox](#slavin-cox-1992) (1992), $q = |q_\mathrm{sat}|\{1 - \exp(-q_\mathrm{class}/|q_\mathrm{sat}|)\}$, which takes the smaller flux where the two differ greatly (eq. 12). Conduction is integrated implicitly (2.4.1).

All clouds have a radius of 41 pc in a hot plasma of $5.6 \times 10^6$ K and $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$, which gives $\sigma_0 = 4.88$ for $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = 1$ ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §3, Table 1). Saturation should then reduce the classical rate by 51 per cent, to $5.6 \times 10^{-4}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$, after Cowie and McKee, or by 67 per cent, to $3.7 \times 10^{-4}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$, after Dalton and Balbus. The most realistic model, R5, is in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium, $\rho\nabla\Phi = -\nabla P$ and $\Gamma = \Lambda$, integrated outwards from the temperature of the core and truncated where its energy density equals that of the hot plasma, at 41 pc, with a dense core and a tenuous rim (eqs. 13, 14). Model R1 is a homogeneous cloud of the same surface density and temperature, in pressure equilibrium, with classical conduction alone, and models R2 the same with saturation, at resolutions of 1, 0.5 and 0.33 pc and on grids of different extent. Model R3 adds the structure of a core and a halo, R4 self-gravity, and R5 heating and cooling, at 1 pc. The homogeneous clouds hold $3.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ and the others $6.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$.

In model R1, with $\rho = 8 \times 10^{-24}$ g cm$^{-3}$ and $T = 1000$ K, the heat input at the edge raises the temperature there, and the overpressure pushes gas away, still far cooler than the ambient gas, while the inner cloud contracts ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §4.1). The recoil drives a pressure wave through the cloud and makes it pulsate on its sound-crossing time, a pulsation excited artificially by switching the simulation on. After some $10^5$ yr the profile of temperature becomes quasi-static and resembles the analytic one for $\sigma_0 < 1$, and the cloud evaporates at about $0.8 \times 10^{-3}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$, close to the classical $1 \times 10^{-3}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ of Cowie and McKee and of Dalton and Balbus, the evaporated gas nearing the speed of sound. A finer grid changes neither the structure nor the rate. In models R2, saturation limits the heat input, and the evaporated gas no longer escapes fast but mixes with the hot medium into a transition layer around the cloud (§4.2). Rayleigh–Taylor instability grows in this layer, as warm dense gas is accelerated into hot rarefied gas; for an acceleration to 0.05 Mach over 3 pc its linear growth time is about 0.8 Myr, as in the simulation, while in R1 the pulsations prevent its growth. After 30 Myr the layer spans 40 to 48 pc, its temperature rising from about $1.5 \times 10^3$ K to $10^5$ K as its density falls, nearly in pressure equilibrium. The gas leaves at only 0.05 Mach, about 21 km s$^{-1}$, and the rate, about $1.4 \times 10^{-5}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$, is a fortieth of the analytic one. The work of the expanding shell against the external pressure lowers the specific thermal energy by a factor of almost 500 across the layer, which explains the low Mach number. The profile of temperature matches the analytic profile for $\sigma_0 = 100$ rather than 4.8, and the local saturation parameter in the simulation is about 100: the global $\sigma_0$ does not fix the rate, which depends on the profile that the transition layer shapes. The models R2 depend little on resolution and boundaries, so that cells of 1 pc suffice.

A cloud with a core and a halo, model R3, has some 10 particles cm$^{-3}$ at about 10 K in its core, 300 K at 10 pc and 1000 K at 30 pc ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §4.3). It forms a similar transition layer with Rayleigh–Taylor instability, sound waves raise the temperature by about 150 K between 30 and 40 pc, and the core stays almost inert. The rate, $1.2 \times 10^{-5}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$, is slightly lower than in R2, since only the outer, homogeneous part of the cloud feeds evaporation. With self-gravity, model R4 needs a warmer core, about 230 K, for hydrostatic equilibrium (§4.4). Self-gravity stabilises the surface against Rayleigh–Taylor instability ([Murray et al.](#murray-et-al-1993) 1993), and the cloud divides into an inert core, a zone dominated by conduction with nearly uniform density, and the transition zone. Pressure waves from the recoil of the evaporated gas travel through the cloud, reflect at a jump in density at 18 pc and push cold gas into the transition zone, so that the rate follows the temperature of that zone, and the dynamics inside the cloud must not be neglected. Table 2 gives $1.4 \times 10^{-5}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ for R4, while the text gives an average of about $1.5 \times 10^{-4}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$, slightly larger than in R3.

Model R5 adds heating and cooling ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §4.5). The velocity field outside the cloud now points towards it, as hot gas accretes onto its surface, while the density inside stays nearly unaffected, and the transition zone is much narrower, a few parsecs thick. The cooling function rises steeply near $10^4$ K, so that colder gas is heated efficiently to about $10^4$ K and then balances heating and cooling, an equilibrium that the Field length governs. In the outermost shell of the cloud, $\lambda_\mathrm{F}$ amounts to $1.4 \times 10^3$ cloud radii, so that no Rayleigh–Taylor instability arises and no finer grid is needed. Within the interface, $\lambda_\mathrm{F}$ drops well below the radius as the density rises inwards, and the dense gas cools efficiently to 8000 to $10^4$ K. The interface then does not dissolve but gathers surrounding gas. Between 30 and 40 Myr the profile of temperature becomes quasi-stationary, and the energy input balances cooling at about $10^4$ K and $r_\star \approx 41$ pc, where $\lambda_\mathrm{F}$ is at most $10^{-2}$ cloud radii: the regime of condensation, where the cells are larger than the mean free path and hydrodynamics applies ([Koyama and Inutsuka](#koyama-inutsuka-2004) 2004). Hot gas condenses at about $3.5 \times 10^{-6}\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ (Fig. 21), and a turbulent velocity field mixes the accreted gas into deeper layers. A shell with at least 1 per cent of intercloud gas grows to about 10 pc in 50 Myr.

The global saturation parameter thus does not determine the mass loss, and the structure of the cloud's density matters little, since the surface sets the heat input ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, §5). Three conclusions follow. Evaporation alters its own environment, a positive feedback. The classical flux is invalid for real interstellar clouds, and conduction is reduced to its saturated limit. With radiative cooling, saturated conduction can make hot gas condense onto clouds in a range of parameters where analytic theory predicts evaporation, which offers a way to accrete and mix intercloud gas into clouds. #### 2.2.7 Saturation in the simulations of 2016

The Spitzer formula fails where the scale length of the temperature falls below the mean free path of the conducting electrons, since the classical description assumes a short mean free path ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.1). The heat flux is then replaced by the saturated flux of [Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977), $|\mathbf{q}_\mathrm{sat}| = 5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3$, with $\rho$ the density and $c$ the sound speed (eq. 5), the isothermal one in the definition of Cowie and McKee (2.2.1). The efficiency $\Phi_\mathrm{s} \lesssim 1$ absorbs the uncertainties of the flux-limited treatment and the suppression of the flux by magnetic fields. It credits [Dalton and Balbus](#dalton-balbus-1993) (1993) with a form that passes smoothly between the two regimes, $\mathbf{q}_\mathrm{eff} = -\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T/(1 + \sigma)$ (eq. 6), the harmonic mean that Dalton and Balbus take from [Balbus and McKee](#balbus-mckee-1982) (1982) (2.2.5). Here $\sigma = \kappa_\mathrm{Sp}|\nabla T|/(5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3)$ is the ratio of the classical to the saturated flux (eq. 7). The flux is thus strongly reduced for $\sigma \gtrsim 1$, and it never exceeds the saturated value.

For the simulations, the module of Athena for isotropic conduction computes the flux $\mathbf{q} = -f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T/(1 + \sigma)$, which combines eqs. 4 and 6 ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2, eq. 8). Magnetic fields may also reduce the saturated flux, through an efficiency $\Phi_\mathrm{s} \sim \sqrt{f}$ (§2.2.1), by the argument of [Cowie and McKee](#cowie-mckee-1977) (1977) with the factors $\cos^2\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ (2.2.1). The simulations take $\Phi_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{f}$, neglecting any other reduction of the saturated flux. **[GK]** With $\sigma$ formed from $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$, as in eq. 7 and in the code (App. A, eq. A7), eq. 8 limits the flux for $\sigma \gg 1$ to $5f\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho c^3$. The saturated flux is then reduced by $f^{3/2}$, about 0.03 for $f = 0.1$, rather than by $\sqrt{f}$, about 0.3, as the argument intends. A reduction by $\sqrt{f}$ needs $\sigma$ formed from $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$, as the ratio of the reduced classical flux to the saturated one; the global parameter of eq. 13 is formed so (2.2.4).

### 2.4 The numerical integration of conduction

#### 2.4.1 The semi-implicit scheme of Vieser and Hensler

Building conduction into an existing hydrodynamic code is complicated by its time step, $\tau_\mathrm{cond} = (e/T\kappa)[\min(\mathrm{d}r, \mathrm{d}z)]^2$, much shorter than the hydrodynamic one where temperatures or conductivities are high ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, App. A, eq. A.1). Vieser and Hensler solve the equation of the internal energy for conduction, $\partial e/\partial t = r^{-1}\partial_r(r\kappa\,\partial_rT) + \partial_z(\kappa\,\partial_zT)$, rewritten for the temperature by the factor $T/e$, in cylindrical coordinates (eqs. A.2, A.3). The two directions are treated separately in the same way. Along $r$, at fixed $z$, the new temperatures of a row are found by an alternating-direction implicit (ADI) method, and once the row has converged the next row follows. The directions are coupled by the fractional steps of [Yanenko](#yanenko-1971) (1971), after the implicit scheme of [Crank and Nicolson](#crank-nicolson-1947) (1947) and [Juncosa and Young](#juncosa-young-1957) (1957) (§3). A purely explicit update would limit the step to the conduction time step, beyond which it is unstable. They therefore weight an implicit part by $0.5 < \alpha < 1$ and an explicit part by $1 - \alpha$, as $(T^{n+1}_{i,j} - T^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = (1 - \alpha)L^n_{i,j} + \alpha L^{n+1}_{i,j}$, with the radial operator in conservative form, $L_{i,j} = [2/(r_{i,j} + r_{i,j+1})](T_{i,j}/e_{i,j})[r_{i,j+1}\kappa_{i,j+1}(T_{i,j+1} - T_{i,j}) - r_{i,j}\kappa_{i,j}(T_{i,j} - T_{i,j-1})]/\Delta r^2$ (eq. A.4). Each row then forms a tridiagonal system (eq. A.5). The weight follows from the ratio $\beta = \tau_\mathrm{hyd}/\tau_\mathrm{cond}$ as $\alpha = (1 - \beta - e^{-\beta})/[\beta(e^{-\beta} - 1)]$, limited to $0.5 \leq \alpha \leq 1$, so that the implicit part dominates where conduction is fast and makes up at least half elsewhere (eqs. A.6 to A.8, Fig. A.1). Since $\kappa$ depends strongly on temperature, the conductivities are updated with the new temperatures and eq. A.4 is solved again, until the temperatures change by less than $10^{-4}$.

They test the method on problems with known solutions, a constant conductivity and $\kappa \propto T^{5/2}$, for which a self-similar solution exists, with results of similar quality, and show the constant case ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) 2007a, App. A). A spherical conduction front spreads through a static medium of frozen, uniform density, $n = 6.6 \times 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$, on a cylindrical grid of $200 \times 100$ cells with $-0.032 < z < 0.032$ pc and $0 < r < 0.032$ pc. It starts from the analytic solution at 0.5 s, with the conductivity of a plasma at $5.6 \times 10^6$ K. Within the hottest region the relative error is 2 to 3 per cent, somewhat larger at the edge of the front, where the temperature is orders of magnitude lower, and at late times the zero gradient imposed at the boundaries shows (Fig. A.2). The scheme of the paper of 2016 (2.4.2) takes the same weight $\alpha(\beta)$, but linearises the conductivity through the variable $z$ instead of iterating, advances each direction by half a step, and works in Cartesian coordinates.

#### 2.4.2 The semi-implicit scheme of the simulations of 2016

[Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, App. A) integrate the heat equation, a parabolic partial differential equation, in its two-dimensional Cartesian form $\partial T/\partial t = (T/e)[\partial_x(\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\,\partial_x T) + \partial_y(\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\,\partial_y T)]$ (eq. A1), with $e = P/(\gamma - 1)$ the internal energy density and $\gamma = 5/3$. An alternating-direction implicit (ADI) method splits the difference equations by direction. A sweep along $x$, at fixed $y$, is followed by a sweep along $y$ that starts from the new temperatures, each over half the time step. The equation is non-linear, since $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp} \propto T^{5/2}$ (eq. 2), and it is solved with a method for non-linear parabolic equations from [Press et al.](#press-et-al-2007) (2007). To keep the hydrodynamic time step free of the limit of conduction, and the computation short, the time integration is semi-implicit, after [Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) (2007a): the implicit part is weighted by a factor $0.5 < \alpha < 1$ and the explicit part by $1 - \alpha$. **[GK]** At constant density $e \propto T$, so that eq. A1 is the energy equation $\partial e/\partial t = \nabla\cdot(\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\nabla T)$ written for the temperature, and its factor $T/e = (\gamma - 1)/(nk_\mathrm{B})$, with $n$ the density of particles, does not depend on $T$.

In the sweep along $x$, the temperature advances over half a step as $(T^{n+1}_{i,j} - T^n_{i,j})/(\Delta t/2) = \alpha\,(T^{n+1}_{i,j}/e^{n+1}_{i,j})(z^{n+1}_{i+1,j} - 2z^{n+1}_{i,j} + z^{n+1}_{i-1,j})/\Delta x^2 + (1 - \alpha)\,(T^n_{i,j}/e^n_{i,j})[\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}(T^n_{i+1/2,j})(T^n_{i+1,j} - T^n_{i,j}) - \kappa_\mathrm{Sp}(T^n_{i-1/2,j})(T^n_{i,j} - T^n_{i-1,j})]/\Delta x^2$ ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, App. A, eq. A2). The implicit part uses the variable $z = \int\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}\,\mathrm{d}T = (2/7)\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}T$ (eq. A3), in which the flux is linear, and linearises it about the old temperature, $z^{n+1}_{i,j} = (2/7)\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}(T^n_{i,j})T^n_{i,j} + (T^{n+1}_{i,j} - T^n_{i,j})\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}(T^n_{i,j})$ (eq. A4). The explicit part takes the conductivity at the faces of the cells, and App. A does not say how the temperature there is formed. Eqs. A2 to A4 then form a tridiagonal system for the new temperatures of each row, $A_{i,j}T^{n+1}_{i-1,j} + B_{i,j}T^{n+1}_{i,j} + C_{i,j}T^{n+1}_{i+1,j} = D_{i,j}$ (eq. A5), which the Thomas algorithm solves efficiently ([Press et al.](#press-et-al-2007) 2007). The weight $\alpha$ depends on the ratio $\beta = \tau_\mathrm{hydro}/\tau_\mathrm{cond}$ of the hydrodynamic to the conductive time step, $\alpha = (1 - \beta - e^{-\beta})/[\beta(e^{-\beta} - 1)]$, limited to 0.5 from below and to 1 from above (eq. A6). The implicit part thus dominates where the conductive time step is much shorter than the hydrodynamic one, and contributes half or more elsewhere. **[GK]** With this weight, the weighted scheme reproduces exactly the decay of a single mode over one step: for $\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}t = -y/\tau_\mathrm{cond}$, requiring $[1 - (1 - \alpha)\beta]/(1 + \alpha\beta) = e^{-\beta}$ gives eq. A6. As $\beta$ grows from zero to infinity, $\alpha$ rises from 1/2 to 1, so that the limits only guard the formula numerically.

App. A shows the algorithm for eq. 1 ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, App. A). For the flux of eq. 8, the coefficient $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}/(1 + \sigma)$ takes the place of $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$, treated as a single coefficient that depends on temperature. In the sweep along $x$, $\sigma$ is discretised as $\sigma_{i,j} = \kappa_\mathrm{Sp}(T_{i,j})\,|(T_{i+1,j} - T_{i-1,j})/(2\Delta x)|/(5\Phi_\mathrm{s}\rho_{i,j}c^3_{i,j})$, and analogously along $y$ (eq. A7), so that each sweep measures only the gradient along its own direction. **[GK]** The closed form of $z$ in eq. A3 holds for a coefficient proportional to $T^{5/2}$. The coefficient $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}/(1 + \sigma)$ also depends on the gradient, and in the saturated limit it scales as $T^{3/2}/|\nabla T|$, so that eq. A3 then holds only approximately. The paper does not say how it forms $z$ for this coefficient.

#### 2.4.3 The test of a conduction front

The scheme is tested on the propagation of a plane conduction front through a static, uniform medium whose density stays constant ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, App. A). The temperature then follows the analytic solution of [Reale](#reale-1995) (1995), $T = T_\mathrm{c}(1 - x^2/x_\mathrm{f}^2)^{2/5}$ (eq. A8a), with the central temperature $T_\mathrm{c} = 0.6\,(Q^2/at)^{2/9}$ (eq. A8b) and the position of the front $x_\mathrm{f} = 1.01\,(Q^{5/2}at)^{2/9}$ (eq. A8c). Here $a = 5.6 \times 10^{-7}(\gamma - 1)/(k_\mathrm{B}n)$ is a constant, and $Q$, the integral of $T$ over the whole space, is $1.5 \times 10^9$ K pc, which gives a maximum temperature of $2 \times 10^6$ K at $t = 1$ Myr. The run starts from the analytic solution at 1 Myr, for a plasma of particle density $n = 10^{-3}$ cm$^{-3}$. Its grid is 1 kpc long, with 500 cells of 2 pc, the resolution of the reference simulation (2.8.2), and zero gradients of temperature at all boundaries. Fig. A1 compares the numerical and the analytic solutions, with their relative error, at 1, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 Myr. They agree very well, with a relative error below 0.01 per cent in the hottest region. Up to 40 Myr, the relative error diverges where the temperature falls to zero, where it is meaningless. At 50 Myr the front has reached the boundary, where the imposed zero gradient makes the two solutions differ visibly, with a relative error of about 70 per cent. **[GK]** The coefficients 0.6 and 1.01 are those of the plane self-similar solution for a conductivity proportional to $T^{5/2}$, normalised so that $Q$ is the integral over the whole line: 0.605 and 1.012. With the parameters of the test they give $T_\mathrm{c} = 2.05 \times 10^6$ K and $x_\mathrm{f} = 444$ pc at 1 Myr. The constant $a$ corresponds to $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp} = 5.6 \times 10^{-7}\,T^{5/2}$, a Coulomb logarithm of 32.9, close to the value of 33.2 that eq. 3 gives at $10^6$ K and $10^{-3}$ cm$^{-3}$. The test exercises the classical conductivity alone, as neither $f$ nor saturation enters it.

### 2.5 Radiative cooling in collisional ionisation equilibrium

#### 2.5.1 Cooling in the energy equation

Radiative cooling and conduction enter the equation of the energy density $e$, $\partial e/\partial t + \nabla\cdot[(e + P)\mathbf{v}] = -\rho^2\Lambda - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}$ ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2, eq. 9). Here $\mathbf{v}$ is the velocity, $\rho$ the density and $P = (\gamma - 1)U$ the pressure, with $U$ the internal energy density and $\gamma = 5/3$. The cooling rate $\Lambda$ depends on temperature and metallicity, and the simulations take it from the tables of [Sutherland and Dopita](#sutherland-dopita-1993) (1993, [1993ApJS...88..253S](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1993ApJS...88..253S/abstract)) for collisional ionisation equilibrium. **[GK]** With $\Lambda$ in erg cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, as Sutherland and Dopita tabulate it and as §4.2 of the paper quotes it, the loss term is a power per unit volume only if $\rho$ there stands for number densities. The paper does not say which number densities multiply $\Lambda$.

### 2.7 The time integration of cooling

#### 2.7.1 Operator splitting and the limit on the time step

In Athena, as the paper describes it, the cooling term is added to the energy equation at first order, by operator splitting and with an explicit update ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §2.2). At high resolution, the cooling time in the simulations mostly exceeds the time scale of the hydrodynamic processes. To avoid instabilities where cooling is very effective, the hydrodynamic time step is nevertheless limited to 10 per cent of the cooling time. 3.1 compares this update with the public version of the code.

### 2.8 Fountain clouds and coronal condensation

#### 2.8.1 Galactic coronae and fountain clouds

[Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §1) start from the prediction of current cosmological models that star-forming galaxies like the Milky Way are embedded in atmospheres of hot gas at the virial temperature, $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K, which reach out to hundreds of kiloparsecs from the centre of the galaxy ([Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) 2006). On the grounds of big-bang nucleosynthesis and of the cosmic microwave background, these cosmological coronae are widely held to contain a significant part of the warm-hot baryons that are missing from the census of the Universe ([Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) 2006; [Komatsu et al.](#komatsu-et-al-2009) 2009; [Shull, Smith and Danforth](#shull-et-al-2012) 2012). Their surface brightness in X-rays is thought too faint for the current instruments ([Bregman](#bregman-2007) 2007), yet hot haloes have been found around some massive spiral galaxies. Around the giant spirals NGC 1961, UGC 12591 and NGC 266, X-ray emission reaches more than 50 kpc from the centre ([Dai et al.](#dai-et-al-2012) 2012; [Bogdán et al.](#bogdan-et-al-2013a) 2013a, [b](#bogdan-et-al-2013b); [Anderson, Churazov and Bregman](#anderson-et-al-2016) 2016). These coronae have masses comparable with the baryonic masses of their discs, $\lesssim 10^{11}\,M_\odot$, and account for 10 to 50 per cent of the baryons missing from these galaxies. **[GK]** The paper cites only the first of the two papers of [Bogdán et al.](#bogdan-et-al-2013a) (2013a, [arXiv:1212.0541](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.0541)), which treats NGC 1961 and NGC 6753; the corona of NGC 266 is the subject of the second ([2013b](#bogdan-et-al-2013b)).

For the Milky Way, [Spitzer](#spitzer-1956) (1956) first proposed a hot corona, as a medium whose pressure confines the high-velocity clouds (HVCs; [Wakker and van Woerden](#wakker-van-woerden-1997) 1997). Most of the evidence for it is indirect. It comes from absorption spectroscopy in the ultraviolet ([Sembach et al.](#sembach-et-al-2003) 2003), the head-tail structure of several HVCs ([Putman, Saul and Mets](#putman-et-al-2011) 2011), the dispersion measures of pulsars in the Large Magellanic Cloud ([Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) 2010) and the stripping of dwarf galaxies of the Local Group by ram pressure ([Grcevich and Putman](#grcevich-putman-2009) 2009; [Gatto et al.](#gatto-et-al-2013) 2013). More recently, hot gas around the Galactic disc was detected through the emission lines of O VII and O VIII in the soft X-ray background of the Milky Way ([Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015). **[GK]** The paper names rotation measures of pulsars in the Magellanic Stream, but [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) (2010, [arXiv:1003.3273](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3273)) constrain the corona with the dispersion measures of pulsars in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Spitzer's proposal, which explains interstellar clouds far from the Galactic plane by their balance of pressure with a rarefied hot gas, is the article cited here; the paper's list of references names instead his book Physics of Fully Ionized Gases of the same year.

Coronae thus seem a significant reservoir of gas, which star-forming galaxies could accrete to sustain star formation at the observed rates ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). How galaxies like the Milky Way keep accreting gas to feed their star formation is a long-standing problem. Their rate of star formation has declined only mildly over their lifetime of about 10 Gyr ([Aumer and Binney](#aumer-binney-2009) 2009; [Fraternali and Tomassetti](#fraternali-tomassetti-2012) 2012). Their content of gas appears to have stayed roughly constant over the Hubble time ([Bauermeister, Blitz and Ma](#bauermeister-et-al-2010) 2010; [Zafar et al.](#zafar-et-al-2013) 2013). The gas of the thin disc typically sustains star formation for a few gigayears only, so that spiral galaxies need, at every epoch, a supply of external gas of about $1\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ to make up for the gas turned into stars ([Sancisi et al.](#sancisi-et-al-2008) 2008). This gas is thought to be poor in metals, to satisfy models of the chemical evolution of spiral galaxies ([Pagel](#pagel-2009) 2009; [Matteucci](#matteucci-2012) 2012) and to explain the observed gradients of metallicity ([Cavichia et al.](#cavichia-et-al-2014) 2014; [Pezzulli and Fraternali](#pezzulli-fraternali-2016) 2016).

For decades, HVCs were held to be the main source of cold gas poor in metals for the Milky Way. The most recent estimates, however, put their accretion at only about $0.08\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ ([Putman, Peek and Joung](#putman-et-al-2012) 2012), more than an order of magnitude below the Galactic rate of star formation ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). In nearby galaxies, too, the accretion of cold extragalactic gas seems to account for only about 10 per cent of the rate of star formation ([Sancisi et al.](#sancisi-et-al-2008) 2008; [Di Teodoro and Fraternali](#di-teodoro-fraternali-2014) 2014). Accretion from the coronae therefore seems the only viable possibility, but the coronal gas is hot and rarefied, and it must cool before it can form stars ([Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015).

How the gas cools and is accreted is debated ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). The first hypothesis held that thermal instabilities in the corona form cold clouds ([Maller and Bullock](#maller-bullock-2004) 2004; [Kaufmann et al.](#kaufmann-et-al-2006) 2006). Later studies showed, however, that in galaxies like the Milky Way thermal conduction and buoyancy together damp the thermal instability of the corona, so that cold clouds smaller than 10 kpc can form only beyond 100 kpc from the disc ([Binney, Nipoti and Fraternali](#binney-et-al-2009) 2009; [Nipoti and Posti](#nipoti-posti-2013) 2013). This contradicts the distances of the HVCs, about 5 to 20 kpc ([Wakker](#wakker-2001) 2001; [Wakker et al.](#wakker-et-al-2007) 2007). Cosmological simulations of galaxies like the Milky Way with adaptive mesh refinement, and simulations by smoothed particle hydrodynamics that treat the mixing of phases appropriately, show no spontaneous cooling of the corona either ([Joung et al.](#joung-et-al-2012) 2012; [Hobbs et al.](#hobbs-et-al-2013) 2013). [Joung et al.](#joung-et-al-2012) (2012) found that cold clouds form in the hot corona only through non-linear perturbations, such as a cosmological filament.

The alternative is a mechanism of galactic origin that triggers the cooling of coronal gas, and much observational evidence points to a constant interaction between the cold gas of star-forming galaxies and their coronae ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). Sensitive observations of H I in star-forming disc galaxies find 5 to 10 per cent of their H I a few kiloparsecs above the disc, in the so-called extraplanar layer of H I ([Swaters, Sancisi and van der Hulst](#swaters-et-al-1997) 1997; [Oosterloo, Fraternali and Sancisi](#oosterloo-et-al-2007) 2007). In the Milky Way, most of this emission comes from the intermediate-velocity clouds (IVCs), complexes of cold gas with the metallicity of the disc at about 2 kpc from the Sun ([Marasco and Fraternali](#marasco-fraternali-2011) 2011). The kinematics of the H I halo is regular, but the halo rotates more slowly than the neutral gas of the disc, and its rotation slows with height above the plane. A vertical gradient of $-15$ km s$^{-1}$ kpc$^{-1}$ has been measured in several spiral galaxies, the Milky Way among them, both in neutral and in ionised gas ([Heald et al.](#heald-et-al-2006) 2006; [Oosterloo, Fraternali and Sancisi](#oosterloo-et-al-2007) 2007; [Marasco and Fraternali](#marasco-fraternali-2011) 2011). Most of this gas is thought to consist of fountain clouds, gas that stellar feedback ejects from the disc ([Bregman](#bregman-1980) 1980; [Houck and Bregman](#houck-bregman-1990) 1990), which travels through the coronal gas and falls back onto the disc within about 80 to 100 Myr.

[Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2006) (2006) built a dynamical model of fountain clouds on ballistic orbits through the halo and applied it to the extraplanar H I of the nearby galaxies NGC 891 and NGC 2403 ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §1). The model reproduces the vertical distribution of the H I halo but underestimates its vertical gradient of rotation, as the observed rotation is slower than the model predicts. [Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2008) (2008) found that the kinematics follows if the fountain clouds lose angular momentum by accreting ambient gas of lower angular momentum. To find the physical process behind this interaction, [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)) simulated in two dimensions a cold cloud, at $10^4$ K and with the metallicity of the disc, travelling through the corona of the Milky Way. The cold gas of the fountain and the hot gas of the corona mix efficiently in a turbulent wake behind the cloud. The mixing shortens the cooling time of the hot gas dramatically, so that part of the corona condenses and is accreted onto the disc. With the model of [Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2008) (2008) and this condensation, [Marasco, Fraternali and Binney](#marasco-et-al-2012) (2012) reproduced the H I halo of the Milky Way, with a present accretion of coronal gas onto the disc of about $2\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$. This agrees with the accretion that the Milky Way requires, about 1 to 3 $M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ ([Chomiuk and Povich](#chomiuk-povich-2011) 2011; [Putman, Peek and Joung](#putman-et-al-2012) 2012). [Fraternali et al.](#fraternali-et-al-2013) (2013) extended the model with warm gas in the turbulent wake, and so reproduced the positions and velocities of most of the warm absorbers observed in the Galactic halo. Accretion driven by the galactic fountain thus seems a viable way for star-forming galaxies to gain the gas that sustains their star formation, and the paper extends its study to other galactic environments (1.2).

#### 2.8.2 The set-up of the simulations

The simulations of [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §3) ran with the code Athena ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008), digested in `athena-synthesis.md`. They use two-dimensional Cartesian geometry, except Sim. 11 of Table 2, which runs in three dimensions to estimate the differences between the geometries. The boundaries on all four sides, six in three dimensions, are semi-permeable, so that gas can flow out of the domain. As in [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)), a cold cloud, rich in metals, travels through a hot and static corona with an initial velocity, its velocity of ejection from the disc. Real fountain clouds orbit above the disc in the gravitational potential of the galaxy and fall back onto it, but the simulations neglect both the gravitational acceleration and the variation of the coronal density along the orbit. The clouds stay at roughly the same distance from the disc: they rise at most a few kiloparsecs above the plane, and their distance from the centre of the galaxy varies by less than 30 per cent ([Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2006) 2006; [Marasco, Fraternali and Binney](#marasco-et-al-2012) 2012). If the corona is in hydrostatic equilibrium in the potential of the galaxy, its density along the orbit is then not expected to vary much. §3 gives neither the size of the domain nor its number of cells. **[GK]** [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2011) (2011, [arXiv:1103.5358](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.5358), §2, Table 1), whose set-up the paper adopts, used 1,024 cells across the direction of motion and about 6,100 along it, of about 2 pc, with the code ECHO rather than Athena.

Table 1 gives the parameters that all simulations share ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). The cloud starts at a temperature $T_\mathrm{cl} = 10^4$ K and a velocity $v_\mathrm{ej} = 75$ km s$^{-1}$, the relative velocity between cloud and ambient medium below which the corona stops absorbing momentum ([Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2011) 2011). The corona has a particle density $n_\mathrm{cor} = 10^{-3}$ cm$^{-3}$. This is lower than the electron density of $2.6 \times 10^{-3}$ cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 10$ kpc that [Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) (2006) found. It compares with the total particle density of $10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$ at 10 kpc above the plane that [Heitsch and Putman](#heitsch-putman-2009) (2009) adopted, and with the mean electron density of $5 \times 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$ within 50 kpc that [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) (2010) inferred from indirect evidence. The metallicity is $Z_\mathrm{cl} = 1.0\,Z_\odot$ in the cloud and $Z_\mathrm{cor} = 0.1\,Z_\odot$ in the corona, in agreement with the values estimated for the galaxies whose hot haloes are observed in X-rays ([Bogdán et al.](#bogdan-et-al-2013a) 2013a; [Hodges-Kluck and Bregman](#hodges-kluck-bregman-2013) 2013; [Anderson, Churazov and Bregman](#anderson-et-al-2016) 2016). For the Milky Way the value is not well defined, but absorption spectra in the far ultraviolet and the emission lines of O VII and O VIII give 0.1 to 0.3 $Z_\odot$ ([Sembach et al.](#sembach-et-al-2003) 2003; [Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015). The density of the cloud follows from pressure equilibrium with the corona. **[GK]** The paper attributes the mean electron density to [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2011) (2011), a study of the hot halo of NGC 1961. The value comes from [Anderson and Bregman](#anderson-bregman-2010) (2010), who derive it from the dispersion measures of pulsars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, as the mean density between the Sun and that galaxy, 50 kpc away.

Table 2 lists eleven simulations, which differ in the coronal temperature $T_\mathrm{cor}$, the resolution, the conduction and its efficiency, and the radius $R_\mathrm{cl}$ of the cloud ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). Sim. 2 serves as reference, in two dimensions, with $T_\mathrm{cor} = 2 \times 10^6$ K, $R_\mathrm{cl} = 100$ pc, cells of 2 pc and conduction of efficiency $f = 0.1$, the factor on the Spitzer conductivity (2.1); its cloud has a mass of $2.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$. Sim. 1 is the same without conduction. Sim. 5, 6 and 7 change the coronal temperature to 1, 4 and $8 \times 10^6$ K, with cloud masses of 1.2, 4.8 and $9.6 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$, and Sim. 3 and 4 change the cells to 4 and 1 pc. Sim. 8 places a cloud of radius 60 pc and mass $2.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ in the corona of $8 \times 10^6$ K, and Sim. 9 doubles the efficiency of conduction to $f = 0.2$. Sim. 10 and 11 repeat Sim. 2 with cells of 10 pc, in two and in three dimensions. **[GK]** At the density of Sim. 7, a radius of 60 pc gives a mass of $2.1 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$, while a quarter of the mass of Sim. 7, which §4.4 names for Sim. 8 together with that radius, needs a radius of 63 pc.

The main aim is the interaction between cloud and corona, and the efficiency of the condensation of coronal gas, in environments of different temperature ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). The coronal gas has about the virial temperature of the halo of dark matter in which the galaxy resides, $T_\mathrm{vir} = G\mu m_\mathrm{p} M_\mathrm{vir}/(2k_\mathrm{B} r_\mathrm{vir})$ (eq. 10), with $\mu$ the mean molecular weight of the coronal gas, $m_\mathrm{p}$ the mass of the proton, $k_\mathrm{B}$ the Boltzmann constant and $G$ the gravitational constant. Within the virial radius $r_\mathrm{vir} = [3M_\mathrm{vir}/(4\pi\Delta_\mathrm{vir}\rho_\mathrm{cr})]^{1/3}$, the mean density of the halo is $\Delta_\mathrm{vir}$ times the critical density $\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ of the Universe (eq. 11). The virial overdensity $\Delta_\mathrm{vir}$ follows from the dissipationless collapse of a spherical top hat, and depends on the cosmological model and on time. For flat cosmologies, with $\Omega_\mathrm{m} + \Omega_\Lambda = 1$, the paper takes the approximation $(18\pi^2 + 82x - 39x^2)/\Omega_\mathrm{m}(z)$ of [Bryan and Norman](#bryan-norman-1998) (1998). Here $x = \Omega_\mathrm{m}(z) - 1$, and $\Omega_\mathrm{m}(z)$ is the ratio of the mean density of matter to the critical density at redshift $z$. In the $\Lambda$CDM model with $\Omega_\mathrm{m} = 0.3$, $\Delta_\mathrm{vir}$ is then about 350 at $z = 0$. The coronal temperature thus measures the virial mass, $T_\mathrm{cor} \propto M_\mathrm{vir}^{2/3}$, and varying it explores a wide range of virial masses. **[GK]** The fit of [Bryan and Norman](#bryan-norman-1998) (1998, [arXiv:astro-ph/9710107](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9710107), §2.1, eq. 6), $18\pi^2 + 82x - 39x^2$, is the overdensity relative to the critical density, about 101 at $z = 0$ for $\Omega_\mathrm{m} = 0.3$. Only its division by $\Omega_\mathrm{m}(z)$ yields a value near 350, about 337, which is the overdensity relative to the mean density of matter and so multiplies $\Omega_\mathrm{m}\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ rather than $\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ in eq. 11. The paper prints the division under the last term alone, which would give about 57. With $\rho_\mathrm{cr}$ in eq. 11, an overdensity of 350 puts the mean density within $r_\mathrm{vir}$ at about $1/\Omega_\mathrm{m} \approx 3.3$ times that of the definition of Bryan and Norman, so that a given coronal temperature corresponds to a virial mass $\Omega_\mathrm{m}^{1/2} \approx 0.55$ times as large.

Each simulation starts with the cloud in pressure equilibrium with the corona ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). Experiments out of equilibrium showed that the cloud readjusts and reaches pressure equilibrium within about 5 Myr. The density of the cloud is thus fixed by the environment, and rises with the coronal temperature. The initial radius of 100 pc, that of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010), is the same in all simulations but Sim. 8, so that the mass of the cloud follows its density, from $1.2 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ to $9.6 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ in Sim. 5, 2, 6 and 7. These masses agree with those estimated for the Galactic IVCs with good constraints on their distances ([Wakker](#wakker-2001) 2001). The Jeans mass of a standard cloud, with $T_\mathrm{cl} = 10^4$ K and $R_\mathrm{cl} = 100$ pc, is $1.6 \times 10^8\,M_\odot$, so that self-gravity can be neglected. **[GK]** With $P = nk_\mathrm{B}T$ in both phases, the cloud holds $n_\mathrm{cl} = n_\mathrm{cor}T_\mathrm{cor}/T_\mathrm{cl}$ particles per unit volume, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4 and 0.8 cm$^{-3}$ at coronal temperatures of 1, 2, 4 and $8 \times 10^6$ K. The masses of Table 2 then correspond to about 1.16 proton masses per particle of the cloud, a mean molecular weight between those of ionised and of neutral gas, nearer the latter. §3 states no mean molecular weight. [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2011) (2011, §2), whose cloud of the same radius and temperature in the same corona has the same mass, let it vary with temperature.

The simulations of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)) started from clouds that were unrealistically spherical ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). Hydrodynamic simulations of supernova explosions show that the gas ejected from the disc is strongly irregular in shape ([Melioli, de Gouveia Dal Pino and Geraissate](#melioli-et-al-2013) 2013). A cloud that is not spherical offers the corona a wider surface of contact, and so a faster and more efficient interaction. To let the spherical cloud deform quickly, and to make the simulations more realistic, the cloud is given turbulent motions. In every cell of the cloud, the two components of the velocity are drawn at random from a Gaussian distribution with a dispersion of 10 km s$^{-1}$. This agrees with the velocity dispersion, mainly due to turbulence, observed in the H I of the Milky Way and of nearby disc galaxies ([Tamburro et al.](#tamburro-et-al-2009) 2009). A bulk velocity of 75 km s$^{-1}$ along $x$, the direction of motion of the cloud, is added to this turbulent field.

In two dimensions, one of the directions perpendicular to the motion of the cloud is suppressed, so that the simulations follow the flow around an infinite cylinder that moves perpendicular to its axis, with an initially circular cross-section of radius $R_\mathrm{cl}$ ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §3). The quantities that they yield per unit length of the cylinder are converted to those of an initially spherical cloud of the same radius by multiplying them by the length $4R_\mathrm{cl}/3$, within which the cylinder holds the mass of the sphere. The masses of Table 2 include this correction.

#### 2.8.3 Mixing and condensation in a corona of $2 \times 10^6$ K

The results concern the transfer of mass between the cloud and the ambient medium, and the growth of the mass of cold gas, the gas below $10^{4.3}$ K ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4). Above this temperature hydrogen is almost fully ionised, with less than 10 per cent of H I ([Sutherland and Dopita](#sutherland-dopita-1993) 1993). Fig. 1 shows the temperature after 20, 40 and 60 Myr in the corona of $2 \times 10^6$ K, without conduction in Sim. 1 and with it in Sim. 2 (§4.1). As in the simulations of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010, [2011](#marinacci-et-al-2011)), the ram pressure of the motion exerts a drag that decelerates and deforms the cloud, and the relative motion drives Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities at the interface. Cold gas stripped from the cloud mixes efficiently with the hot gas in a turbulent wake, where radiative cooling can become effective. With conduction, the wake is less elongated but wider. A magnification of part of the wake after 60 Myr (Fig. 2) shows, without conduction, a compact wake of many cloudlets in which gas cools below about $5 \times 10^5$ K. With conduction it shows a mixture of gas at different temperatures, cold clouds and filaments embedded in hotter gas near $10^6$ K. Conduction, a diffusive process, makes the wake more widespread and warmer and smooths its gradients of temperature, and so partly hinders the formation and survival of the cold cloudlets that radiative cooling of the wake would form.

Whether radiative cooling or conduction dominates follows from the criterion of [Begelman and McKee](#begelman-mckee-1990) (1990) for a two-phase medium of clouds in a hot plasma ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.2). Cooling dominates on scales $l$ above the Field length $\lambda_\mathrm{Field} = [\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}T_\mathrm{hot}/(n_\mathrm{cold}^2\Lambda(T_\mathrm{cold}))]^{1/2}$ (eq. 12), named after the work of [Field](#field-1965) (1965), who showed that thermal instabilities are suppressed below this scale. Here $T_\mathrm{hot}$ is the temperature of the hot plasma, and $n_\mathrm{cold}$ and $\Lambda(T_\mathrm{cold})$ the number density and the cooling rate of the cold gas. On scales $l \ll \lambda_\mathrm{Field}$, conduction governs the temperature of structures embedded in the hot plasma, and on scales $l \gg \lambda_\mathrm{Field}$ radiative cooling does. For $T_\mathrm{cor} = 2 \times 10^6$ K and $n_\mathrm{cl} = 0.2$ cm$^{-3}$, with $\Lambda(T_\mathrm{cold}) = 10^{-23.93}$ erg cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, the mean cooling rate of Sutherland and Dopita between $10^4$ and $10^{4.3}$ K, the Field length is about 20 pc. This is about five times smaller than the radius of the cloud, so that its global evolution should differ little with or without conduction. It is comparable, however, with the cloudlets of the wake, whose survival decides the growth of the cold mass. **[GK]** With these quantities and the Coulomb logarithm of eq. 3 at the coronal density, eq. 12 gives about 117 pc with $\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$ and 37 pc with $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$. For 1 and $8 \times 10^6$ K, with the cloud densities of 2.8.2, it gives 22 and 103 pc with $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$, where the paper states about 10 and 70 pc (2.8.5). The paper does not say how it evaluates eq. 12. Its comparisons with the radius of the cloud hold also for the values with $f\kappa_\mathrm{Sp}$.

The mass of gas below $10^{4.3}$ K grows with time in both simulations, as more and more coronal gas cools in the wake ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.2, Fig. 3). Its growth becomes nearly exponential after about 30 Myr without conduction and after about 40 Myr with it. The delay is the time that the gas stripped from the cloud needs to mix with the coronal gas, plus the time that the coronal gas needs to cool below $10^{4.3}$ K ([Marasco, Fraternali and Binney](#marasco-et-al-2012) 2012), and conduction lengthens the latter by hindering condensation. Condensation is therefore systematically larger without conduction. After 60 Myr the condensed gas amounts to about 26 per cent of the initial mass of the cloud without conduction, a value that §5.1 gives as about 25 per cent, and to about 18 per cent with it. Conduction thus slows the condensation of coronal gas but does not inhibit it. In the warm wake near $10^6$ K, gas condenses on smaller scales into cold clouds and filaments, some of which survive beyond the 60 Myr of the simulations.

#### 2.8.4 Resolution and numerical diffusion

Three simulations, with cells of 4, 2 and 1 pc, test the convergence of the code (Sim. 3, 2 and 4; [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.3, Fig. 4). The velocity centroid of the cold gas, its total momentum along the direction of motion divided by its total mass, agrees well at all three resolutions. Even the coarsest grid captures how the cloud slows down, through the ram pressure of the corona and the condensation of coronal gas that was at rest ([Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) 2010; [Marasco, Fraternali and Binney](#marasco-et-al-2012) 2012). The mass of cold gas agrees less well. It is systematically higher with cells of 4 pc, while the profiles with cells of 2 and 1 pc cross several times and differ by at most a few per cent. The differences come from the material stripped from the cloud and its mixing with the corona. The wake consists of small cloudlets and filaments, and numerical diffusion truncates this hierarchy of substructures at a few cells, smoothing the gradients of temperature. The finer the grid, the more of the cold material sits in substructures, the less the phases mix, and the less coronal gas condenses. With radiative cooling but no physical diffusion, results at different resolutions are therefore hard to bring to convergence.

Conduction helps to restrain the numerical diffusion ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.3). Like numerical diffusion, it destroys small structures and creates a homogeneous mixture at intermediate temperatures, but on a scale set by the physical conditions, chiefly the temperature of the corona (eq. 1), rather than by the grid. At very low resolution numerical diffusion dominates, and at higher resolution conduction can take over. The turbulence of the wake produces a hierarchy of ever smaller structures, with a power-law distribution of sizes. Their smallest size is set by the resolution, but their survival largely by conduction, which is efficient below the Field length of about 20 pc. The smallest cloudlets, of a few parsecs, cannot survive, mix with hotter gas and trigger condensation, so that they do not affect the growth of the cold mass, and the convergence of Fig. 4 may reflect this change of regime. Cells of 2 pc are therefore judged good enough, and the remaining simulations use them.

#### 2.8.5 Coronae of different temperatures

In a corona of $10^6$ K (Sim. 5), the cloud evolves much as at $2 \times 10^6$ K with conduction: the stripped cold gas mixes with the hot gas in a turbulent wake, which holds much gas below $2 \times 10^5$ K, a sign of very effective cooling ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.4, Fig. 5). In a corona of $8 \times 10^6$ K (Sim. 7), the cloud behaves nearly as a rigid body, without a turbulent wake, so that the two fluids do not mix. The Field length is about 10 pc at $10^6$ K and about 70 pc at $8 \times 10^6$ K, comparable with the radius of the cloud (eq. 12). Conduction thus acts only on the cloudlets of the wake in cooler coronae, but can shape the motion of the whole cloud in hotter ones. There it smooths the gradient of temperature at the interface into a transition region at intermediate temperatures, visible in Fig. 5, and the cloud loses momentum at its surface. Without a strong gradient of velocity, no hydrodynamic instabilities form at the interface, and the cloud takes very long to be destroyed ([Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007b) 2007b). Mixing between cloud and corona is delayed or absent. The cloud of Sim. 7 has the largest mass explored, $9.6 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$, and an inefficient ram pressure might add to its slow destruction, as the drag time grows with the mass of the cloud, $t_\mathrm{drag} \propto M_\mathrm{cl}$ ([Fraternali and Binney](#fraternali-binney-2006) 2006). In Sim. 8, a cloud a quarter as massive, with a radius of 60 pc (2.8.2), evolves much like that of Sim. 7, with its cold gas compact at the head and no turbulent wake. At $8 \times 10^6$ K, conduction thus drives the evolution, whatever the mass and size of the cloud.

Condensation declines as the corona gets hotter ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §4.5, Fig. 6). After 60 Myr the condensed gas amounts to about 30, 18 and 4 per cent of the initial mass of the cloud at 1, 2 and $4 \times 10^6$ K, and to less than 1 per cent at $8 \times 10^6$ K. As the cloud starts at $10^4$ K in all runs, a hotter corona gives a hotter mixture with longer cooling times, and near $10^6$ K the shape of the cooling function makes the cooling time very sensitive to the temperature ([Sutherland and Dopita](#sutherland-dopita-1993) 1993). In coronae much hotter than $2 \times 10^6$ K, the wake does not cool enough to start the exponential growth of condensation. Conduction, more efficient in hotter coronae, acts in two ways. It smooths the gradient of velocity at the interface, which keeps the cloud compact, prevents instabilities and mixing, and slows the destruction of the cloud. Once gas is stripped, it heats the cold gas faster and evaporates it into the corona. At $8 \times 10^6$ K the cold mass stays nearly constant, the cloud neither gaining coronal gas nor losing its own, since its radius is comparable with the Field length and cooling matters too. In Sim. 8, whose radius is slightly below the Field length, conduction and cooling visibly balance, and the cold mass declines very slowly, by about 1 per cent of the initial mass after 60 Myr. In still hotter coronae, cooling probably becomes ineffective and the cloud evaporates.

#### 2.8.6 Geometry, heating and the efficiency of conduction

The two-dimensional geometry is tested by Sim. 10 and 11, with the same parameters and cells of 10 pc, in two and in three dimensions ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §5.1, Fig. 7). Finer cells in three dimensions would have taken very long with a code on a static grid such as Athena. The cold mass grows in both runs, faster in three dimensions, but by less than 15 per cent: after 60 Myr the condensed gas amounts to about 27 per cent of the initial mass in three dimensions and 23 per cent in two. In two dimensions the cloud is an infinite cylinder moving across its axis, and in three a sphere, whose larger surface of contact with the corona lets further instabilities develop and the stripped gas mix faster with the coronal gas. This agrees with [Pittard and Parkin](#pittard-parkin-2016) (2016), who compared the two geometries for a spherical cloud in a hotter, shocked medium. The two-dimensional runs thus slightly underestimate the growth of the cold mass.

Heating, such as photoionisation by background sources or by the star-forming disc, is neglected ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §5.1). It would make the estimates upper limits, since it slows the cooling of the gas. At fixed metallicity the cooling rates depend mainly on temperature, so that condensation starts to grow exponentially once the gas of the wake nears the peak of the cooling function (2.8.3). Heating rates depend strongly on density, heating being more efficient at lower density, so that the gas of the wake must become dense enough for cooling to start efficiently. The main effect of heating is expected to be a delay in the onset of condensation.

The efficiency $f$ of conduction also affects the findings ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §5.1). [Narayan and Medvedev](#narayan-medvedev-2001) (2001) give $f = 0.2$ for a tangled field, but the simulations use 0.1 (2.1.2), and only Sim. 9 tests $f = 0.2$, with the other parameters of Sim. 2. After 60 Myr it condenses about 9 per cent of the initial mass, against about 18 per cent for $f = 0.1$, so that a factor of 2 in the efficiency of conduction gives a factor of 2 in condensation. The 9 per cent is a lower limit on the condensation over the values of $f$ considered, and the simulation without conduction, with $f = 0$ and about 25 per cent, gives the upper limit. Over this range of $f$, condensation always occurs, and the amount of cold gas accreted correlates with the efficiency of conduction.

#### 2.8.7 Implications for disc galaxies

Star-forming galaxies like the Milky Way need a supply of external gas to form stars at the observed rate of about $1\,M_\odot$ yr$^{-1}$ (2.8.1), and cosmological models strongly indicate that it comes from the intergalactic medium, gathered around galaxies as hot coronae ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §5.2). The simulations show that the interaction with cold, metal-rich fountain gas can cool the coronal gas efficiently, which condenses in the wake of the cloud into fresh gas for star formation, but less efficiently as the corona gets hotter. The corona of the Milky Way has about $2 \times 10^6$ K ([Fukugita and Peebles](#fukugita-peebles-2006) 2006; [Miller and Bregman](#miller-bregman-2015) 2015), where condensation is efficient: in 60 Myr the cloud of $2.4 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ condenses coronal gas amounting to about 18 per cent of its mass. Through eqs. 10 and 11, coronal temperatures of $10^6$, $2 \times 10^6$, $4 \times 10^6$ and $8 \times 10^6$ K correspond to virial masses of about $10^{12}$, $3 \times 10^{12}$, $8 \times 10^{12}$ and more than $10^{13}\,M_\odot$. The virial mass estimated for the Milky Way, about $2 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ ([Li and White](#li-white-2008) 2008), is slightly lower, but observations of its corona are sensitive to the gas close to the disc, where feedback may cause slight departures from the virial temperature ([Strickland et al.](#strickland-et-al-2004) 2004). Fountain clouds thus cool their corona very efficiently around $3 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$, poorly around $8 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ and not at all from $10^{13}\,M_\odot$. This suggests a threshold of mass, between about 3 and $8 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$, above which galaxies cannot cool and accrete their corona. **[GK]** With $\mu = 0.6$ and $H_0 = 70$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, eqs. 10 and 11 as printed give 1.2, 3.2, 9.2 and $26 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ for these temperatures, close to the values of the paper. With the overdensity of [Bryan and Norman](#bryan-norman-1998) (1998; 2.8.2), the same temperatures correspond to about 2.1, 6.0, 17 and $48 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$.

[Schawinski et al.](#schawinski-et-al-2014) (2014) showed that star-forming galaxies lie mostly in haloes of low virial mass, while disc galaxies in the green valley almost all have virial masses above about $10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §5.2). The green valley lies between the blue cloud of star-forming galaxies and the red sequence of quiescent ones in the diagram of colour and mass, and its galaxies are leaving, or have left, the main sequence of star formation ([Brinchmann et al.](#brinchmann-et-al-2004) 2004; [Peng et al.](#peng-et-al-2010) 2010; [Schawinski et al.](#schawinski-et-al-2014) 2014). Some process has thus switched off their star formation, and how this quenching happens is debated. Along the Hubble sequence, the early-type disc galaxies near S0 and Sa have the lowest ratio of current to past rates of star formation ([Boselli et al.](#boselli-et-al-2001) 2001). Late-type discs are still forming stars, while early-type ones have declined as their reservoir of gas ran out. Galaxies like the Milky Way, near the limit between the blue cloud and the green valley at $M \gtrsim 10^{12}\,M_\odot$, still form stars at slowly declining rates, and as the history of star formation relates to the virial mass, the environment may regulate the supply of gas. Haloes below a critical mass of shock heating, $M_\mathrm{shock} \sim 2$ to $3 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$, are fed by cold cosmological streams that no virial shock heats, the cold mode of accretion, and form stars, while above this mass cold accretion stops ([Dekel and Birnboim](#dekel-birnboim-2006) 2006; [Kereš et al.](#keres-et-al-2009) 2009). These galaxies would then evolve passively from the blue cloud to the red sequence as their gas runs out. Galaxies like the Milky Way, above $M_\mathrm{shock}$, still form stars at an almost unchanged rate, and need a mechanism that feeds them.

Condensation driven by fountain clouds may explain why galaxies above $M_\mathrm{shock}$ remain on the main sequence, and it complements, rather than excludes, the quenching by the end of cold-mode accretion ([Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) 2016, §5.2). The corona cools efficiently in galaxies of low to intermediate virial mass, but less so from late-type to early-type disc galaxies, which would deprive them of cold gas and could quench them. Since condensation requires the corona to mix with cold gas of the disc, early-type galaxies of any virial mass, which have much less cold gas ([Catinella et al.](#catinella-et-al-2010) 2010), should cool their corona inefficiently. The end of star formation in disc galaxies may thus come not only from heating of the halo, by active galactic nuclei ([Ciotti and Ostriker](#ciotti-ostriker-2012) 2012) or thermal stellar feedback ([Stinson et al.](#stinson-et-al-2013) 2013), but also from the inability of massive galaxies, with $M_\mathrm{vir} > 3 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$, to cool their corona. The mechanism might explain both kinds of quenching that [Peng et al.](#peng-et-al-2010) (2010) identified, by stellar mass and by environment, as its only driver, the ambient temperature, correlates both with the stellar mass of isolated galaxies ([Behroozi, Wechsler and Conroy](#behroozi-et-al-2013) 2013; [Moster, Naab and White](#moster-et-al-2013) 2013) and with the environment. In dense environments such as clusters of galaxies, with high virial temperatures, even late-type disc galaxies could not cool their corona, and their star formation would be quenched.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture records how the papers change their codes, chiefly the modules of conduction and cooling of Athena.

### 3.1 Athena in the simulations of 2016

The simulations of [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016, §2.2) ran with Athena ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008), a parallel, multidimensional code for MHD on grids, digested in `athena-synthesis.md`. It uses Godunov methods of higher order, with a conservative finite-volume discretisation that evolves the volume averages of mass, momentum and total energy. The authors modified its module for isotropic conduction to compute the flux of eq. 8 (2.2.7), which they add directly to the flux of energy. As the explicit update of that module restricts the hydrodynamic time step severely, they replaced it by a semi-implicit update of the temperature, in alternating sweeps along the rows and columns of the grid, each a tridiagonal system (App. A; 2.4.2). Cooling uses the tables of [Sutherland and Dopita](#sutherland-dopita-1993) (1993), added by operator splitting at first order with an explicit update, and limits the time step to a tenth of the cooling time (2.5.1, 2.7.1). The acknowledgements thank Fabrizio Brighenti for suggestions on implementing conduction in Athena (1.1). **[GK]** The public release of the C version of Athena, version 4.2 at [github.com/PrincetonUniversity/Athena-Cversion](https://github.com/PrincetonUniversity/Athena-Cversion) (commit bb328f4 of 9 June 2017), holds both modules in `src/microphysics/`. There, `conduction.c` adds an explicit heat flux to the energy equation, with an isotropic part from a constant diffusivity times the density and an anisotropic part along the magnetic field, and `integrate_diffusion.c` applies it by operator splitting. A cooling function enrolled as `CoolingFunc`, such as that of [Koyama and Inutsuka](#koyama-inutsuka-2002) (2002) in `cool.c`, enters the unsplit integrators as an explicit source term. `src/integrators/integrate_2d_ctu.c` applies half a step of it to the interface states and a full step with the density and pressure at the half step. An implicit solver for the function of Koyama and Inutsuka, applied by operator splitting, exists as well (`cool_solver.c`). The explicit, first-order and operator-split update of cooling that the paper describes is thus neither of the public options, and the paper does not say how it changed them.

### 3.2 The code of Vieser and Hensler

[Vieser and Hensler](#vieser-hensler-2007a) (2007a, §3) build on an Eulerian code whose hydrodynamic part follows [Rozyczka](#rozyczka-1985) (1985): explicit and of second order in space on a staggered grid, in cylindrical coordinates $(r, z)$ with axial symmetry, the cloud on the axis. Two dimensions let the instabilities grow that arise when cold dense gas is accelerated into warm rarefied gas, which a one-dimensional spherical code could not follow. The boundaries at the top and at both ends are semi-permeable, so that gas can flow out, and the axis mirrors the variables. A passive tracer, the colour, holds the fraction of the density that comes from the hot medium, starting at unity around the cloud and at zero inside, and is advected like mass and energy with the monotonic transport of [van Leer](#van-leer-1977) (1977), to trace the condensation of hot gas onto the cloud. The Poisson equation of self-gravity is solved every tenth step, since the structure of the cloud changes slowly compared with its dynamical time. Building heat conduction into this tested code proved more complicated than expected (App. A). Conduction is computed apart from the hydrodynamics, with the semi-implicit scheme of 2.4.1, from a code for heat conduction that Tomek Plewa provided (acknowledgements).
