### 1.2 Aims and scope

The review gives an overview of numerical simulations of multiphase gas in and around galaxies ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, abstract). It calls gas multiphase when gas at several temperatures lies roughly in the same place (§1). The classical theory of the interstellar medium (ISM) meant by phases the cold, neutral clouds of molecular and atomic hydrogen and the warmer, partly ionised medium that fills the volume around them ([Field](#field-1965) 1965; [McKee and Ostriker](#mckee-ostriker-1977) 1977). Work on the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and the intracluster medium (ICM), and simulations that do not track ionisation, often mean instead a fully ionised plasma with several equilibrium temperatures. Two long-lived phases, hot and cold, often coexist with transient gas at intermediate temperatures, made by mixing or by cooling, the two main themes of the review. Their temperatures depend on the system. Cold means neutral or molecular gas below about 300 K in the ISM, but photoionised gas near $10^4$ K in the CGM, which studies of the ISM call warm. The review follows the usage of each field and states the temperatures in each section. Hot means gas of low density at $T \gtrsim 10^6$ K unless stated otherwise.

The review leaves out the small scales of particle-in-cell simulations, the cosmological scales and the intergalactic medium, the solar corona, protostellar and protoplanetary discs, and the accretion discs of active galactic nuclei (AGN) ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1). It focuses on multiphase systems that act directly on the formation and evolution of galaxies, and on the techniques and results of simulations, connecting their scales. Other reviews cover parts of the field: processes on the scale of haloes, thermal instability among them ([Donahue and Voit](#donahue-voit-2022) 2022), mixing layers and the crushing of clouds ([Faucher-Giguère and Oh](#faucher-giguere-oh-2023) 2023), galactic winds ([Thompson and Heckman](#thompson-heckman-2024) 2024) and cosmic rays ([Ruszkowski and Pfrommer](#ruszkowski-pfrommer-2023) 2023).

Multiphase gas occurs in the ISM of the Milky Way and of other galaxies, from a few kelvin in the coldest molecular clouds to several $10^{10}$ K inside supernova remnants ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.1). In the CGM of large galaxies, a virial shock heats the infalling gas into the hot phase that fills the volume, while thermal instability, the stripping of satellites by ram pressure and winds from the central or the satellite galaxies seed the cooler phases. The ICM, at higher pressures, spans a still broader range. The interface between disc and halo, where the hotter and thinner CGM meets the warm and cool phases of the ISM, is multiphase too, driven among others by outflows. Outflows such as that of the starburst galaxy M82 show temperatures over eight orders of magnitude in the same place, and the winds of AGN carry multiphase gas under strong radiation and fast kinematics. Fig. 1 shows examples in galactic winds, gas stripped by ram pressure, high-velocity clouds and the CGM.

Thermal instability (TI) is the classical explanation for multiphase gas ([Field](#field-1965) 1965, after [Parker](#parker-1953) 1953). Gas in thermal equilibrium, whose heating does not depend on temperature, is unstable where its radiative losses grow as its temperature falls ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.1). This holds where the cooling function falls with temperature, from about $10^5$ to $10^7$ K, and the heating comes from photoionisation. Fig. 2, left, shows the rates of cooling and heating of gas of solar metallicity at two densities under the ultraviolet background at redshift zero, computed with the code Cloudy ([Ferland et al.](#ferland-et-al-2013) 2013). In gas near $10^7$ K at constant pressure, an overdense perturbation cools faster, is compressed by the surrounding pressure and cools faster still, since the losses scale as $n^2$. It settles where heating balances cooling again, near $10^4$ K for the pressure of the halo of the Milky Way, $P/k_\mathrm{B} \sim 10^2$ K cm$^{-3}$, at a temperature that depends on the density, the ionising radiation and the metallicity. Two stable phases then coexist in pressure equilibrium, $n_\mathrm{cool} T_\mathrm{cool} = n_\mathrm{hot} T_\mathrm{hot}$, as long as thermal pressure alone supports them. Magnetic fields or cosmic rays lower the density of the cool phase. The denser cool gas forms small clumps in the hot medium ([McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) 2018, Fig. 4). This origin is often called precipitation. Fig. 2, right, shows its regimes, from hot, thin gas through unstable gas to the dense, cold gas of the ISM, in the phase diagram of a simulated isolated galaxy.

Origins in the dynamics of the gas matter as much. Turbulent mixing at interfaces, through the Kelvin–Helmholtz (KH) and Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961), makes gas of intermediate temperature and density from gas that is already multiphase ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.1). Shocks compress and heat the gas that they sweep, and thermal conduction makes gas of intermediate temperature at interfaces between hot and cold gas. In real systems all of these act at once. Analytic work has taught much about each of them, but their combination and the non-linear evolution of the instabilities over long times drive the simulations. Most simulations that the review discusses assume solar metallicity. The cooling rates depend strongly on metallicity, above all between $10^5$ and $10^7$ K, but many studies express their results in dimensionless form or through cooling times, which can be rescaled, so that their conclusions apply more generally (footnote 3).

The review closes by placing its set-ups on one ladder of scales ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3, Fig. 3). Idealised simulations, from below a parsec to tens of parsecs, isolate single processes at high resolution and allow controlled studies of parameters. Larger simulations of bubbles, patches of discs, isolated galaxies and cosmological zooms capture the global context, but rely on limited resolution and on models below the scale of the grid. The review sees the two as complementary parts of one framework. Large simulations set the pressures, densities, metallicities, turbulence, radiation and kinematics in which small ones should run, and small ones interpret the processes in large ones, test analytic theory and calibrate models below the grid. Criteria of survival are thus being tested on ensembles of clouds in realistic outflows, and criteria of thermal instability in haloes with realistic sources of turbulence. Observations remain the ground truth against which simulations at all scales are judged.

### 2.1 Clouds in hot winds without cooling

#### 2.1.1 The crushing of clouds

Simulations of the crushing of clouds are central to the study of multiphase flows ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). In their simplest form, a spherical cold cloud of density $\rho_\mathrm{cold}$ and radius $r_\mathrm{cl}$ is exposed to a wind of density $\rho_\mathrm{hot}$ and velocity $v_\mathrm{wind}$. The set-up goes back to the picture of the ISM of [McKee and Ostriker](#mckee-ostriker-1977) (1977), which asks how cold clouds interact with the hot bubbles of supernovae, and so how cold high-velocity clouds can be accelerated. The acceleration of cold gas in galactic winds is still an active question ([Thompson and Heckman](#thompson-heckman-2024) 2024, among other reviews), and more than 50 papers have treated the set-up and its variants (footnote 11 points to a list of them kept by W. Banda-Barragán).

That the interaction destroys the cloud was clear early on ([Nittmann, Falle and Gaskell](#nittmann-et-al-1982) 1982). [Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994) established that the shear and the acceleration by ram pressure raise KH and Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities, while a shock crosses the cloud ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2; see also [Murray et al.](#murray-et-al-1993) 1993). The three processes destroy the cold gas on the time of cloud crushing, $t_\mathrm{cc} \sim \chi^{1/2} r_\mathrm{cl}/v_\mathrm{wind}$ (eq. 5), which equals the growth times of the instabilities (2.2.1). The wind needs a longer time, $t_\mathrm{drag} \sim \chi\, r_\mathrm{cl}/v_\mathrm{wind}$ (eq. 6), to give the cloud enough momentum to accelerate it. Since $t_\mathrm{drag}/t_\mathrm{cc} \sim \chi^{1/2} \gg 1$, the cloud is destroyed before it is accelerated. How cold gas nevertheless reaches the observed velocities of hundreds to a thousand km s$^{-1}$ is the problem of entrainment ([Zhang et al.](#zhang-et-al-2017) 2017).

High-resolution simulations in three dimensions followed the mixing and destruction in detail ([Cooper et al.](#cooper-et-al-2009) 2009; [Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2015) 2015; [Brüggen and Scannapieco](#bruggen-scannapieco-2016) 2016) ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). They extended the set-up to higher overdensities ([Cooper et al.](#cooper-et-al-2009) 2009), higher Mach numbers ([Scannapieco and Brüggen](#scannapieco-bruggen-2015) 2015), other shapes of clouds and further physics ([Brüggen and Scannapieco](#bruggen-scannapieco-2016) 2016). Magnetic fields change the morphology of the cold gas, suppress the mixing and speed up the acceleration. [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2015) (2015) argued that the drag of magnetic fields could solve the problem of entrainment. [Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) (2020a) showed that magnetic fields shorten the time of acceleration only by a factor of a few. That suffices for overdensities $\chi \lesssim 30$, while the overdensities $\gtrsim 100$ that matter most in astrophysics would need fields stronger than the thermal pressure, with a plasma beta $\beta < 1$. **[GK]** The review cites this result, and the pulsations, expanding winds and transfer of momentum of 2.3, as Gronke and Oh (2020b), the letter that its list gives to their paper on shattering, Is multiphase gas cloudy or misty? ([arXiv:1912.07808](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07808)). The abstract of their paper on entrainment, 2020a ([arXiv:1907.04771](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.04771)), names these subjects, that of the paper on shattering none of them.

#### 2.1.2 Related set-ups

The review also names wind tunnels into which the cold gas enters by other means than hydrodynamic interaction ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). In galaxies stripped by ram pressure, gravity holds the gas against the wind. Some studies treat the physics of stripping in small boxes, others the long-term evolution and the multiphase structure of the stripped tail in large domains. A CGM around the infalling galaxy can promote further condensation in the tail, and magnetic fields suppress structure on small scales, so that the tails come out smoother and less clumpy. Other wind tunnels blow cold stellar winds, $\lesssim 10^4$ K, into a hot and possibly moving medium of $\gtrsim 10^6$ K, such as the wind of the AGB star Mira in the Local Bubble, whose observed tail they reproduce well, or the faster and hotter winds of Wolf–Rayet stars. Such studies focus on global outcomes rather than on the multiphase interface, but the same mixing, cooling and surface instabilities act, and the scalings of 2.2 and 2.3 often apply.

#### 2.1.3 The shock–cloud problem of Klein, McKee and Colella

[Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994, [doi:10.1086/173554](https://doi.org/10.1086/173554)) wrote from the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory and the University of California at Berkeley, Colella from its Department of Mechanical Engineering. The paper was received on 28 May 1993 and appeared on 1 January 1994. It presents a comprehensive numerical study of the simplest interaction of a shock with an interstellar cloud: a steady, planar shock strikes an isolated, spherical cloud, and radiative losses, thermal conduction, magnetic fields and gravity are neglected (abstract, §1). The problem then depends on two numbers, the Mach number $M$ of the shock and the density contrast $\chi \equiv \rho_\mathrm{c0}/\rho_\mathrm{i0}$ of cloud and intercloud medium (eq. 2.1), and for strong shocks the dependence on $M$ scales out. Variations treat a softer equation of state in the cloud, $\gamma_\mathrm{c} = 1.1$ instead of $5/3$, as a hint of radiative losses, a cylindrical cloud, and a cloud in a wind without a shock. The authors meant the study as a benchmark for more realistic calculations. They name six open questions: the rate of stripping and the mass stripped, the transfer of momentum, the appearance of the shocked cloud, the effect of a radiative intercloud shock, the onset of gravitational instability, and the effect of magnetic fields.

In the ISM, diffuse atomic clouds of $T \sim 10^2$ K and $n \sim 40\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and molecular clouds of $T \sim 10$ K and $n \sim 10^3\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ lie in warm gas of $T \sim 10^4$ K and $n \sim 0.3\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and in hot coronal gas of $T \sim 10^6$ K and $n \sim 3 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §1). The contrast $\chi$ is therefore of order $10^2$ for cold atomic clouds in warm gas and for warm clouds in coronal gas, $10^3$ for molecular clouds in warm gas and $10^4$ for cold atomic clouds in coronal gas (§2.1). Earlier calculations were underresolved. [Nittmann, Falle and Gaskell](#nittmann-et-al-1982) (1982) used flux-corrected transport, others about 30 zones per cloud radius, with numerical diffusion at the boundary that hid the mixing by instabilities, or about 40 zones with the piecewise parabolic method. [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992) (1992) ran the first three-dimensional calculation, found the vortex rings of two dimensions unstable, but confirmed that the cloud is disrupted. Apart from one study, all treated cloud and intercloud medium as one fluid that differs only in density.

#### 2.1.4 Timescales and limits

When the intercloud shock of velocity $v_\mathrm{b}$ meets the cloud, it drives a shock into it, whose velocity follows from pressure balance with the shocked intercloud gas, $v_\mathrm{s} \simeq (\rho_\mathrm{i0}/\rho_\mathrm{c0})^{1/2} v_\mathrm{b} = v_\mathrm{b}/\chi^{1/2}$, as long as the intercloud shock is not radiative ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §2.1, eq. 2.2). The intercloud shock sweeps across the cloud in $t_\mathrm{ic} \equiv 2a_0/v_\mathrm{b}$ (eq. 2.3), with the initial radius $a_0$. The cloud is crushed in the cloud-crushing time $t_\mathrm{cc} \equiv \chi^{1/2} a_0/v_\mathrm{b} = a_0/v_\mathrm{s}$ (eq. 2.4), the basic timescale of the shocked cloud. The blast wave accelerates the cloud in two stages: the cloud shock gives it $v_\mathrm{s}$, and the shocked intercloud gas then drags it along until it moves with that gas at $\frac{3}{4} v_\mathrm{b}$, for $\gamma = 5/3$. For a large contrast the second stage dominates, with the equation of motion $m_\mathrm{c}\,dv'_\mathrm{c}/dt = -\frac{1}{2} C_\mathrm{D}\,\rho_\mathrm{i1}\,v_\mathrm{c}'^2 A$ (eq. 2.5). Here $v'_\mathrm{c}$ is the velocity of the cloud relative to the shocked intercloud gas, $C_\mathrm{D} \sim 1$ the drag coefficient, $\rho_\mathrm{i1} \simeq 4\rho_\mathrm{i0}$ the density of the shocked intercloud gas and $A$ the cross-section of the cloud. A constant cross-section gives the drag time $t_\mathrm{drag,0} = \chi a_0/(C_\mathrm{D} v_\mathrm{b}) = \chi^{1/2} t_\mathrm{cc}/C_\mathrm{D}$ (eq. 2.6). The KH instability of wavenumber $k$ grows in $t_\mathrm{KH}/t_\mathrm{cc} = (v_\mathrm{b}/v_\mathrm{rel})/(k a_0)$ (eq. 2.7), with the relative velocity $v_\mathrm{rel}$, and the Rayleigh–Taylor instability, driven by the deceleration $g \simeq a_0/t_\mathrm{cc}^2$, in $t_\mathrm{RT}/t_\mathrm{cc} \simeq (k a_0)^{-1/2}$ (eq. 2.8). Short waves grow fastest, but waves with $k a_0 \sim 1$ disrupt most, and both grow in about $t_\mathrm{cc}$, so that the cloud is destroyed in a few $t_\mathrm{cc}$.

The pressure behind a blast wave changes on a time $t_P \simeq 0.1 R_\mathrm{c}/v_\mathrm{b}$, for a cloud at the distance $R_\mathrm{c}$ from the explosion (eq. 2.10). Clouds are small when $t_\mathrm{cc} \ll t_P$, medium when $t_\mathrm{cc} \gtrsim t_P \gtrsim t_\mathrm{ic}$, and large when $t_\mathrm{ic} > t_P$ (§2.2). The paper treats small clouds, which for a contrast of 100 must be smaller than 1 per cent of the radius of a supernova remnant. Its neglect of physics has limits (§2.3). The cooling time behind a strong shock is $2.54 \times 10^{10}\,\beta_\Lambda^{-1}\,v_{\mathrm{s}7}^3/n_0$ s (eq. 2.11), with $v_{\mathrm{s}7} = v_\mathrm{s}/(10^7\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}})$, the density $n_0$ ahead of the shock and a factor $\beta_\Lambda$ of order unity. A cloud shock with a cooling time ten times $t_\mathrm{cc}$ needs $v_{\mathrm{s}7} > 3.33\,(n_\mathrm{c0}\,a_\mathrm{pc})^{1/4}$, with $a_\mathrm{pc} = a_0/(1\,\mathrm{pc})$ (eq. 2.12). Thermal conduction is governed by the saturation parameter $\sigma'_0$ (eq. 2.13), whose ratio to $t_\mathrm{cool}/t_\mathrm{cc}$ in the shocked intercloud gas is $2 \times 10^{-4} \chi^3$ (eq. 2.14). Where cooling is slow, the heat flux is thus saturated for $\chi > 10$, and saturated evaporation ablates a cloud of $\chi \sim 10^2$ in about $t_\mathrm{cc}$ (eq. 2.16). Neglecting conduction therefore assumes magnetic fields too weak to matter for the dynamics but strong enough to inhibit conduction. Gravity is negligible, since a non-radiative shock raises the largest stable mass of the cloud as $M^3$ (eq. 2.17).

#### 2.1.5 The evolution of the shocked cloud

The equations are invariant under $t \to tM$, $v \to v/M$ and $P \to P/M^2$ (eq. 4.3), so that for $M \gg 1$ the evolution in units of $t/t_\mathrm{cc}$ does not depend on $M$ ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §4). The global quantities of runs with $M = 10$ and $M = 1000$ agree to within 15 per cent at $5.05\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ (Table 2), and their morphologies at $2.95\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ closely (Fig. 2).

The interaction passes through four stages (§5). In an initial transient, the blast wave drives a shock into the cloud and reflects a shock that settles into a bow shock or bow wave within about $t_\mathrm{ic}/2$. In the compression, the flow around the cloud converges on the axis behind it after about $t_\mathrm{ic}$, raising a strong reflected shock and driving a shock into the rear of the cloud. The side shocks are weaker, so the cloud is compressed into a pancake. In the re-expansion, the main cloud shock reaches the rear, a strong rarefaction runs back into the cloud, and the cloud expands downstream and, against the low pressure at its sides, laterally until a time $t_\mathrm{m}$ of a few $t_\mathrm{cc}$. In the destruction, which overlaps the re-expansion, instabilities and differential forces fragment the cloud. In the standard case, $\chi = M = 10$, the intercloud shock converges behind the cloud at $0.94\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, or $1.5\,t_\mathrm{ic}$, for other contrasts too. A double Mach reflection and a supersonic vortex ring form behind the cloud, and the shock from the rear meets the main cloud shock at about $1.25\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, which raises the pressure to six and the density to 2.5 times their values behind the main cloud shock (eqs. 5.5, 5.6). The mean pressure of the cloud peaks near $t_\mathrm{cc}$ and settles into rough equilibrium with the intercloud gas after about $4\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ (Fig. 3), and the radial expansion slows by $t_\mathrm{m} \simeq 3.8\,t_\mathrm{cc}$. The final mean density, $2.13\,\rho_\mathrm{c0}$ in the simulation against $1.92\,\rho_\mathrm{c0}$ from an adiabatic estimate (eq. 5.7), is about half the naive factor of 4, so the cloud radiates less than expected and the condition of eq. 2.12 may be relaxed.

The Richtmyer–Meshkov instability of the impulsively accelerated boundary grows linearly in time and so matters only for large perturbations. In the simulations the mesh supplies them, and they shrink as the resolution grows, but real clouds are not smooth ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §5.1). The KH and Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities dominate the destruction, visibly by $2.5\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ (Fig. 5). By $3.8\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ the cloud consists of a flattened core and a plume of fragments that holds over 70 per cent of its mass, with vortex rings along the shear layer where it fragments most (Fig. 6). By $9.6\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ it is completely fragmented, spread over twice its transverse and five times its axial dimension, with no fragment above 2 per cent of its mass (Fig. 7).

For $\chi = 3$, 10, 30 and 100 at $M = 10$, the drag times are 1.53, 2.58, 2.96 and $3.79\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, the destruction times 8.16, 3.79, 3.17 and $3.90\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, the mixing times above 10, 5.13, 3.93 and $3.91\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, and $t_\mathrm{m}$ is 4.00, 3.70, 3.90 and $4.20\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ (§5.2, Table 3), as 2.5.2 defines these times. The scaling with $t_\mathrm{cc}$ thus holds to lowest order, with weak deviations. The drag time grows slowly with $\chi$, since the initial drag scales with $\chi^{1/2} t_\mathrm{cc}$ (eq. 2.6), and the higher relative velocity at large $\chi$ speeds the instabilities, so that $t_\mathrm{dest}/t_\mathrm{cc}$ falls from $\chi = 3$ to 30. Its rise at $\chi = 100$ may reflect a fragile definition of the largest fragment. The cloud is compressed along the axis and then stretches steadily, more so at larger $\chi$, expands radially to about $2a_0$, and becomes prolate. Its axial velocity dispersion is $(0.1$–$0.2)\,v_\mathrm{b}$ for $\chi = 10$, somewhat more for $\chi = 100$, and its radial dispersion $(0.06$–$0.16)\,v_\mathrm{b}$, which persists after the radial growth stops and so measures the vorticity (Figs. 8–10). With $\gamma_\mathrm{c} = 1.1$, the cloud is compressed much more, by factors of 2.0 across and 4.4 along the axis against 1.2 and 2.3, re-expands more slowly and presents a smaller cross-section, so that the drag is weaker and the instabilities more violent (§5.3, Fig. 11). Its pencil-like core still holds 55 per cent of the mass at $3.8\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, against 30 per cent for $\gamma_\mathrm{c} = 5/3$. Its mean compression reaches 7.1 against 2.2 at $6.75\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, and it survives longer, $5.7$ against $3.8\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ (Table 4).

#### 2.1.6 Drag, vorticity and fragmentation

With a constant cross-section, the cloud would move with the shocked gas after $t_\mathrm{drag,0} \simeq \chi^{1/2} t_\mathrm{cc}$, the time it takes to sweep up a column density equal to its own ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §6). Its lateral re-expansion, modelled as $a(t) = [a_0^2 + (C_\mathrm{c} t)^2]^{1/2}$ until $t_\mathrm{m}$ (eq. 6.1), with the sound speed $C_\mathrm{c}$ of the shocked cloud, raises the drag considerably. It enters the solution for the velocity of the cloud through a factor $1 + 0.104\,(t/t_\mathrm{cc})^2$ for $\gamma_\mathrm{c} = 5/3$ (eqs. 6.2, 6.3). If the expansion went on, the characteristic time would grow as $\chi^{1/6}$, but it stops at $t_\mathrm{m} \sim 4\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, after which the relative velocity falls as $1/t$. With $C_\mathrm{D} = 1$ and the mass-weighted velocity of all cloud material, the model agrees well at first. Near $t_\mathrm{m}$ it overestimates the velocity by up to a factor of 1.5 for $\chi = 10$ and 2 for $\chi = 100$, and it converges with the simulations soon after (Fig. 12). For $\chi = 30$ it stays within 12 per cent between 5.85 and $12.2\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, by when the relative velocity has fallen to 7.7 per cent of its initial value. Internal shocks in the cloud, which the model leaves out, cause part of the discrepancy.

Vortex rings play an important part in the destruction ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §7). An ideal isentropic fluid cannot make vorticity, but the shocked flow is not isentropic, and the scheme has numerical viscosity, so the authors show that the vorticity is not an artefact. The baroclinic term $\nabla P \times \nabla(1/\rho) = \nabla T \times \nabla s$ makes the vorticity (eqs. 7.6, 7.7), while gravity cannot and viscosity only spreads it. Three sources matter. The shock sweeping over the cloud gives a circulation $\Gamma_\mathrm{shock} \simeq -2.25\,v_\mathrm{b} a_0 (1 - \chi^{-1/2})$ (eq. 7.9). The flow past the cloud gives $\Gamma_\mathrm{post} \simeq -\frac{9}{64}(\chi^{1/2} t_\mathrm{drag}/t_\mathrm{cc})\,v_\mathrm{b} a_0$ (eq. 7.12). The supersonic vortex ring behind the cloud gives $\Gamma_\mathrm{ring} \simeq +\frac{3}{4} v_\mathrm{b} a_0$ (eq. 7.13), of opposite sign. Inside the cloud the vorticity is smaller by about $\chi^{-1/2}$. For $\chi = M = 100$ the simulation gives $-2\,v_\mathrm{b} a_0$ by $0.3\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ against $-2.03$ predicted, a ring of $+0.73$ against $+0.75$, and a total of $-7.5\,v_\mathrm{b} a_0$ against $-6.7$ from shock and flow (Fig. 13). For $\chi = 10$ the agreement is weaker, $-2.1$ against $-2.7\,v_\mathrm{b} a_0$, since the vorticity of the cloud itself is not estimated. The circulation of the case with $\gamma_\mathrm{c} = 1.1$ is 1.34 times larger, against 1.5 predicted. The authors conclude that numerical viscosity does not affect the circulation significantly. The peak vorticity, set by the thickness of the shear layer and so of the grid, is not converged and scales as $(\Delta x)^{-1}$ (2.6.6).

The fragmentation destroys the cloud on large and small scales ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §8). For $10 \lesssim \chi \lesssim 100$ the destruction time is $t_\mathrm{dest} \simeq 3.5\,t_\mathrm{cc}$, in which the blast wave advances $3.5\,\chi^{1/2} a_0$ (eq. 8.1), so that a cloud of $\chi = 10$ is largely destroyed after ten cloud radii. Without viscosity, surface tension or magnetic fields, the KH and Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities have no smallest scale, and the cloud fragments down to the grid, where zones hold both fluids (2.5.2). Mixing proceeds faster at larger $\chi$, through the higher relative velocity (Fig. 17). The KH instability, whose growth rate scales as $k$ against $k^{1/2}$ for the Rayleigh–Taylor instability, dominates the small-scale fragmentation. In a hierarchical model, in which the cloud breaks into a few large fragments and these again, the cloud travels $\Delta z'_\mathrm{c} \propto -\chi^{1/2} a_0$ in the frame of the shocked gas before it is fragmented completely (eqs. 8.2, 8.3). The simulations give $-1.68\,\chi^{1/2} a_0$ at $t_\mathrm{mix}$ for $3 \le \chi \le 100$ (eq. 8.4, Fig. 18), with 25 per cent more at the largest $\chi$, where the core is stripped from its surface rather than broken up. For $\chi = 3$ to 30 the cloud fragments on large scales before it mixes, while at $\chi = 100$ both happen together. In the frame of the unshocked gas, the cloud moves only about $(6$–$8)\,a_0$ by $t_\mathrm{mix}$ for $10 \lesssim \chi \lesssim 100$ (eq. 8.5), so that a small non-radiative cloud is displaced no more than about eight radii before it is destroyed and mixed.

#### 2.1.7 Related problems and the Cygnus Loop

A cylinder whose axial size is three times its radius, $c_0 = 3a_0$, evolves much like the sphere when time is measured in the modified crushing time $t'_\mathrm{cc} \equiv (\chi a_0 c_0)^{1/2}/v_\mathrm{b}$ (eq. 9.2), with a destruction time of $4.64\,t'_\mathrm{cc}$ against $3.79\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §9.1, Table 5). Modest changes of shape thus do not alter the conclusions. A cloud in a wind, without the shock, was set up as a compressed, flattened ellipsoid of axial radius $c_1 \simeq a_0/4$ and density $\chi$ times that of the shocked intercloud gas, moving at the relative velocity $0.505\,v_\mathrm{b}$ that the shock would leave, a Mach number of 0.90 (§9.2). It is decelerated and destroyed only about 50 per cent more slowly than the shocked cloud, with drag, destruction and expansion times of 3.79, 6.15 and $6.17\,t'_\mathrm{cc}$ (Table 5). Transient bow and transmitted shocks still form, but weaker, and the cloud expands laterally through the Venturi effect and stretches more along the axis. These results agree with those of [Murray et al.](#murray-et-al-1993) (1993) at 25 zones per radius. Its circulation, measured at $-3.0\,|v'_\mathrm{c0}|\,a_0$ against $-1.5$ from eq. 7.12, is less than in the shocked case, in line with its longer destruction. Taking $m_\mathrm{c}/t_\mathrm{dest}$ as the rate of entrainment gives a coefficient of about 0.5 in the scaling of mass loading of [Hartquist et al.](#hartquist-et-al-1986) (1986) (eq. 9.3), consistent with their estimate of order unity.

The case with $\gamma_\mathrm{c} = 1.1$ and $\chi \simeq 10$ resembles a shocked cloud in the Cygnus Loop that [Fesen, Kwitter and Downes](#fesen-et-al-1992) (1992) observed in $\mathrm{H}\alpha$, for $a_0 \simeq 0.5$ pc and $v_\mathrm{b} \simeq 400\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so that $t_\mathrm{ic} = 2440$ yr and $t_\mathrm{cc} = 3900$ yr ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §10). At $1.26\,t_\mathrm{cc} = 1.99\,t_\mathrm{ic}$, close to the observed age of 1.7 to $1.9\,t_\mathrm{ic}$, the computed intercloud shock bends behind the cloud and reattaches through a Mach disk, and a flattened core with swept-back arms forms, as in the observations (Figs. 20, 21). The paper predicts velocity dispersions of about $60\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ along and $40\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ across the axis. It argues that the feature taken for the bow shock is unrelated, since at $\chi \simeq 10$ the bow shock soon becomes a bow wave, and it reads X-ray observations as showing a cloud elongated along the line of sight.

The authors conclude that clouds are decelerated and destroyed in several $t_\mathrm{cc}$, independently of the parameters over the range studied, of the spherical shape and even of the initial blast wave ([Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) 1994, §11). This is much shorter than the time to sweep up the column density of the cloud. The high pressure inside the shocked cloud and the low pressure at its sides expand it laterally and hasten its deceleration and destruction. Since the drag time is in many cases shorter than the destruction time, clouds can be accelerated effectively, contrary to [Nittmann, Falle and Gaskell](#nittmann-et-al-1982) (1982). The principal limitation is the restriction to two dimensions, since the prominent vortex rings are unstable in three, but three-dimensional calculations bear out the results by and large ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992) 1992). §11 gives the range of the drag time as 1.6 to $3.4\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ for $\chi$ from 3 to 100, where Table 3 gives 3.79 at $\chi = 100$, and destruction times falling from 4 to about $2\,t_\mathrm{cc}$ between $\chi = 10$ and 400.

### 2.2 Turbulent radiative mixing layers

#### 2.2.1 The shear layer and the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

Shear layers, or turbulent mixing layers, are among the simplest and most idealised set-ups for multiphase gas ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Two phases of densities $\rho_\mathrm{hot}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{cold}$ lie on either side of a perturbed interface and move along it with a relative velocity $v_\mathrm{shear}$. The KH instability develops and grows on a time $t_\mathrm{KH} \sim \chi^{1/2} \ell / v_\mathrm{shear}$ ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961), with the overdensity $\chi \equiv \rho_\mathrm{cold}/\rho_\mathrm{hot}$ and the length $\ell$ of the perturbation. It drives the mixing of the phases and their exchange of mass, momentum and energy. A sharp interface, smooth only on scales much smaller than $\ell$, is unstable at all wavelengths that the system allows. Such interfaces are common in multiphase media, where the cold phase cools much faster than on any dynamical time, so that shear between phases always brings mixing and an exchange of mass. Four effects can weaken or suppress the instability, and with it the mixing ([Landau](#landau-1944) 1944; [Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961):

- Gravity. With an acceleration $g$ that points from the hot to the cold medium, only scales $\ell < v_\mathrm{shear}^2/(g\chi)$ are unstable, so a strong effective gravity suppresses the instability ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961).
- The thickness of the shear layer. A velocity that changes smoothly over a width $d$ suppresses the modes below about $10d$, which do not feel the shear ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961; [Berlok and Pfrommer](#berlok-pfrommer-2019b) 2019b). In a viscous flow, the instability is thus suppressed where the viscous time $t_\nu \sim (\ell/10)^2/\nu$, with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$, is shorter than $t_\mathrm{KH}$ ([Roediger et al.](#roediger-et-al-2013) 2013; [Marin-Gilabert et al.](#marin-gilabert-et-al-2022) 2022).
- Supersonic flow. Above a critical Mach number, shocks that follow the growing perturbations damp them. With $\mathcal{M} \equiv v_\mathrm{shear}/c_\mathrm{s,hot}$, the threshold is $\mathcal{M}_\mathrm{crit} = (1 + \chi^{-1/3})^{3/2}$ ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, eq. 1; [Landau](#landau-1944) 1944; [Mandelker et al.](#mandelker-et-al-2016) 2016).
- Magnetic fields. By the review, a field component parallel to the interface stabilises the flow for $v_\mathrm{shear} < (v_\mathrm{A,hot}\,v_\mathrm{A,cold})^{1/2}$, with the Alfvén velocity $v_\mathrm{A} = B_\parallel/\rho^{1/2}$ of each medium ([Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1961) 1961). A perpendicular field can suppress mixing as well, since the shear bends it into a stabilising parallel component ([Ryu, Jones and Frank](#ryu-et-al-2000) 2000; [Das and Gronke](#das-gronke-2023) 2023). **[GK]** The linear theory of an incompressible vortex sheet with parallel fields gives stability for $\rho_\mathrm{hot}\rho_\mathrm{cold}\,v_\mathrm{shear}^2 < (\rho_\mathrm{hot} + \rho_\mathrm{cold})(B_\mathrm{hot}^2 + B_\mathrm{cold}^2)$ in these units. A uniform field thus stabilises the flow for $v_\mathrm{shear} < [2(v_\mathrm{A,hot}^2 + v_\mathrm{A,cold}^2)]^{1/2}$, close to $\sqrt{2}\,v_\mathrm{A,hot}$ at large $\chi$. For a uniform field the geometric mean of the review equals $v_\mathrm{A,hot}\,\chi^{-1/4}$, below this threshold by a factor of about $\sqrt{2}\,\chi^{1/4}$.

The linear growth of the KH instability is well understood and serves as a benchmark for codes ([Lecoanet et al.](#lecoanet-et-al-2015) 2015). Its non-linear stage, in which turbulence and secondary instabilities develop the mixing layer in full, needs simulations ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Radiative cooling changes the nature of the instability, since it lets mass pass continuously between the phases, and the review turns to this case.

#### 2.2.2 The model of Begelman and Fabian

The first simulations of radiative turbulent mixing layers ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006; [Kwak and Shelton](#kwak-shelton-2010) 2010; [Kwak, Shelton and Henley](#kwak-et-al-2015) 2015; [Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) 2019) followed the analytic work of [Begelman and Fabian](#begelman-fabian-1990) (1990), which [Slavin, Shull and Begelman](#slavin-et-al-1993) (1993) extended ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Begelman and Fabian predicted that the mean temperature of the layer is the average weighted by the fluxes of mass into it across the interface, $\bar T = (\dot m_\mathrm{hot} T_\mathrm{hot} + \dot m_\mathrm{cold} T_\mathrm{cold})/(\dot m_\mathrm{hot} + \dot m_\mathrm{cold})$ (eq. 2). Most of the gas belongs to the hot or the cold phase, but the gas at $\bar T$ dominates the cooling, so that a mixing layer is usefully treated as a medium of three phases. The hot gas enters at $\dot m_\mathrm{hot} \sim \eta_\mathrm{hot}\,\rho_\mathrm{hot}\,v_\mathrm{mix} A$ (eq. 3), with the area $A$ of the layer parallel to the flow, a characteristic velocity of mixing $v_\mathrm{mix}$, and an efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{hot}$ for the imperfect entrainment of hot gas. It carries in enthalpy at the rate $\dot e \sim (5/2)\,\eta_\mathrm{hot} P v_\mathrm{mix}$, at the pressure $P$. The model assumes that this inflow forms eddies, of scales $l_\mathrm{cold}$ below the full thickness $l_\mathrm{t}$ of the layer, which pull cold gas in at $\dot m_\mathrm{cold} \sim \eta_\mathrm{cold}\,\rho_\mathrm{cold}\,l_\mathrm{cold} A/t_\mathrm{KH}$ (eq. 4), with an efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{cold}$ and the growth time $t_\mathrm{KH} \sim (\rho_\mathrm{cold}/\rho_\mathrm{hot})^{1/2}\,l_\mathrm{cold}/v_\mathrm{mix}$. With efficiencies of similar size, $\eta_\mathrm{cold} \sim \eta_\mathrm{hot}$, eq. 2 gives the geometric mean of the two temperatures, $\bar T \sim (T_\mathrm{cold} T_\mathrm{hot})^{1/2}$. Derivations from geometry or from the fluxes of mass and enthalpy reach the same temperature ([Hillier and Arregui](#hillier-arregui-2019) 2019; [Faucher-Giguère and Oh](#faucher-giguere-oh-2023) 2023, §3.4; footnote 7). **[GK]** The review writes eq. 4 without the area $A$, which a flux of mass needs, as in eq. 3. The two fluxes then stand in the ratio $\dot m_\mathrm{cold}/\dot m_\mathrm{hot} \sim \chi^{1/2}$, and with pressure equilibrium, $\chi = T_\mathrm{hot}/T_\mathrm{cold}$, eq. 2 yields the geometric mean exactly.

The model does not predict the efficiencies, so even with $\eta_\mathrm{hot} \sim \eta_\mathrm{cold}$ it cannot predict the inflow of eq. 3, on which the existence and growth of the phases depend ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). It also relies on rapid mixing and leaves out cooling and viscosity, which may act back on the turbulence ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006). Studies differ on its validity, but agree within a factor of about five that it predicts the abundances of the ions of intermediate temperature ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006; [Kwak and Shelton](#kwak-shelton-2010) 2010; [Kwak, Shelton and Henley](#kwak-et-al-2015) 2015), while [Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) (2019) may find larger deviations. Such simulations must reach a steady state in a box wide enough across the flow, which some runs of [Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) (2006) do not ([Kwak and Shelton](#kwak-shelton-2010) 2010; [Kwak, Shelton and Henley](#kwak-et-al-2015) 2015; footnote 8). Later work points out that the abundances of these ions depend on the distribution of temperature, which needs thermal conduction (2.2.3).

#### 2.2.3 Mixing with cooling

Later work maps how the inflow of hot gas, and the structure and dynamics of the layer, depend on the geometry of the flow and on other parameters ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). The hot gas flows in at about the sound speed of the cold gas, $v_\mathrm{mix} \sim c_\mathrm{s,cold}$. The inflow scales as $\dot m \propto t_\mathrm{cool}^{-1/2}$ when cooling is slow ([Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) 2019; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021), and as $\dot m \propto t_\mathrm{cool}^{-1/4}$ when it is fast ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) 2020a; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021; [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) 2020). Slow and fast refer to the time of mixing, $t_\mathrm{mix} \sim \ell/u'$ with the local turbulent velocity $u'$, and the Damköhler number $\mathrm{Da} \equiv t_\mathrm{mix}/t_\mathrm{cool}$ separates the two regimes at unity. [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) (2021) drew the analogy with the fronts of turbulent combustion, in which gas burns on a time of reaction where in a mixing layer it cools on $t_\mathrm{cool}$. Fig. 4 shows a schematic of a mixing layer after [Slavin, Shull and Begelman](#slavin-et-al-1993) (1993), a surface of constant temperature in a simulation of [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) (2020), and the two regimes of [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) (2021), which differ in morphology and in the energy that they radiate.

The interface of a mixing layer is fractal ([Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) 2020; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021). [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) (2020) showed this in simulations and built an analytic model on the fractal shape ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). Its scalings differ slightly from those of the model of [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) (2021), which draws on combustion, but the simulations show that mixing, followed by cooling, is the process that matters under approximate pressure equilibrium ([Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) 2020; [Chen, Fielding and Bryan](#chen-et-al-2022) 2022).

The turnover time of an eddy grows with its size, as $t_\mathrm{eddy} \propto \ell^{2/3}$ in a Kolmogorov cascade, so the largest scales are the bottleneck of mixing and cooling ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). The morphology of the layers is therefore generally not converged, while the rates of mass transfer are ([Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021). Since turbulent diffusion dominates, thermal conduction matters little for the transfer of mass and for the total luminosity of the layer ([Esquivel et al.](#esquivel-et-al-2006) 2006; [Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2021) 2021). It matters much for the full distribution of temperature and for the emission of the single ions of intermediate temperature ([Tan and Oh](#tan-oh-2021) 2021). 2.6.3 draws the consequences for resolution.

#### 2.2.4 Higher Mach numbers and magnetic fields

Most of these studies treat subsonic and transonic shear ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). [Yang and Ji](#yang-ji-2022) (2022) extended them to higher Mach numbers, for slow cooling, $\mathrm{Da} < 1$. The turbulent velocity saturates at $\mathcal{M} \gtrsim 1$, and the flux of mass reverses, so that cold gas evaporates. How more efficient cooling changes this has not yet been explored. Streams, which share much with mixing layers, become unstable above the critical Mach number of eq. 1 through body modes (2.3.6). [Ji, Oh and Masterson](#ji-et-al-2019) (2019), also for $\mathrm{Da} < 1$, and more recently [Das and Gronke](#das-gronke-2023) (2023) studied magnetic fields in radiative mixing layers. The fields grow to equipartition and, depending on their orientation, can strongly suppress the flux of mass from the hot to the cold medium. They do so above all for fast cooling, $\mathrm{Da} > 1$, where mixing is the bottleneck.

### 2.3 The survival and growth of cold gas

#### 2.3.1 Survival with radiative cooling

Radiative cooling is a particularly important addition to the crushing of clouds ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). [Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994, §2.3.1) already alluded to it, and later work studied it further ([Mellema, Kurk and Röttgering](#mellema-et-al-2002) 2002). Detailed simulations with strong cooling ([Scannapieco and Brüggen](#scannapieco-bruggen-2015) 2015; [Brüggen and Scannapieco](#bruggen-scannapieco-2016) 2016; [Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2017) 2017) still concluded that all cold gas is eventually mixed into the wind. The review traces this to simulations that did not follow the cloud long enough, had too small a domain, did not track the cloud to capture its tail, or treated only clouds small enough to be destroyed. The two-dimensional simulations of [Marinacci et al.](#marinacci-et-al-2010) (2010), and later those of [Armillotta, Fraternali and Marinacci](#armillotta-et-al-2016) (2016), [Armillotta et al.](#armillotta-et-al-2017) (2017) and [Gritton, Shelton and Galyardt](#gritton-et-al-2017) (2017), partly in three dimensions, showed that cooling can halt the disruption of some clouds and even make their cold mass grow.

[Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2018) (2018) formulated a quantitative criterion: a cloud survives to be entrained if $t_\mathrm{cool,mix}/t_\mathrm{cc} < 1$ ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, eq. 7). Here $t_\mathrm{cool,mix}$ is the cooling time of the mixed gas at the geometric means of the temperatures and densities of the two phases, in the spirit of [Begelman and Fabian](#begelman-fabian-1990) (1990) (2.2.2). Since $t_\mathrm{cc} \propto r_\mathrm{cl}$ while the cooling time depends only on the gas, the criterion becomes a threshold of size, $r_\mathrm{cl} > r_\mathrm{crit}$: only clouds larger than a critical radius survive. For typical conditions of winds and the ISM, $r_\mathrm{crit}$ is of order a parsec, $r_\mathrm{crit} \approx 2\,\mathrm{pc}\;T_\mathrm{cl,4}^{5/2}\,\mathcal{M}_\mathrm{wind}\,P_3^{-1}\,\Lambda_\mathrm{mix,-21.4}^{-1}\,(\chi/100)$ (footnote 12). Here $T_\mathrm{cl,4} = T_\mathrm{cl}/10^4\,\mathrm{K}$, $P_3 = nT/(10^3\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}\,K})$, $\Lambda_\mathrm{mix,-21.4} = \Lambda(T_\mathrm{mix})/(10^{-21.4}\,\mathrm{erg\,cm^3\,s^{-1}})$, and $\mathcal{M}_\mathrm{wind}$ is the Mach number of the wind with respect to its own sound speed. Since $r_\mathrm{crit} \propto 1/n$, the threshold can also be written as a fixed column density of the cloud (footnote 13). **[GK]** The scalings follow from eq. 7 with $T_\mathrm{mix} = \chi^{1/2} T_\mathrm{cl}$, $n_\mathrm{mix} = P/(k_\mathrm{B} T_\mathrm{mix})$ and $v_\mathrm{wind} = \mathcal{M}_\mathrm{wind}\,c_\mathrm{s,hot}$, where $c_\mathrm{s,hot} \propto (\chi T_\mathrm{cl})^{1/2}$.

#### 2.3.2 The debate on the criterion

The exact form of the criterion is still debated ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). [Li et al.](#li-et-al-2020) (2020) proposed $t_\mathrm{cool,hot}/t_\mathrm{life} < 1$, with the cooling time $t_\mathrm{cool,hot}$ of the hot medium and an empirical lifetime $t_\mathrm{life}$ of the cloud, which depends on several parameters but is generally about $10\,t_\mathrm{cc}$. [Sparre, Pfrommer and Ehlert](#sparre-et-al-2020) (2020) compared the two criteria and supported that of Li et al., while [Kanjilal, Dutta and Sharma](#kanjilal-et-al-2020) (2020) reached the reverse conclusion. Groups define survival differently, which matters because the cold mass can drop substantially in an initial phase, $t \sim \chi^{1/4} t_\mathrm{cc}$, before it grows again. A fixed cutoff, such as survival while the cold mass stays above 10 per cent of its initial value, avoids misreading an under-resolved later evolution, but may flag clouds as destroyed that would eventually grow. Fig. 5, adapted from [Li et al.](#li-et-al-2020) (2020), shows the mechanisms at work in the regime of destruction, without cooling or with weak cooling.

Several studies have since argued that both criteria are somewhat unphysical ([Abruzzo, Bryan and Fielding](#abruzzo-bryan-fielding-2022) 2022; [Abruzzo, Fielding and Bryan](#abruzzo-fielding-bryan-2023) 2023; [Farber and Gronke](#farber-gronke-2021) 2021). The mixed gas spans a range of temperatures, so that the picture of Begelman and Fabian is too simple (2.2.2). Near the temperature of the hot medium, moreover, mixing and not radiative cooling lowers the temperature, as simulations show in which clouds still grow when cooling is switched off above some temperature. The effective cooling time in eq. 7 should therefore be a weighted mean of $t_\mathrm{cool}$ between $T_\mathrm{cold}$ and $T_\mathrm{hot}$ ([Farber and Gronke](#farber-gronke-2021) 2021; [Abruzzo, Fielding and Bryan](#abruzzo-fielding-bryan-2022) 2022). These studies and the simulations of mixing layers show that the weighting peaks near the minimum of the cooling time, but its form is unknown. A criterion of survival that is better founded in theory and generally valid is still outstanding.

#### 2.3.3 What radiative cloud crushing has shown

The review draws five lessons from recent simulations of radiative cloud crushing ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2):

- Cold gas survives the acceleration by ram pressure if it cools efficiently enough, when some cooling time is shorter than some time of destruction. As the geometric criterion $r_\mathrm{cl} > r_\mathrm{crit}$, this lets clouds larger than several parsecs survive at the high pressures of the ISM and of winds, with a threshold that depends strongly on the environment ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2018) 2018).
- Clouds that survive keep growing, also while they are entrained, and may gain much mass late in their evolution. Pulsations of the cold gas, whose pressure overshoots, drive the growth ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) 2020a; [Abruzzo, Fielding and Bryan](#abruzzo-fielding-bryan-2022) 2022). The transfer of mass follows the scaling of mixing layers, $\dot m \propto t_\mathrm{cool}^{-1/4}$ (2.2.3).
- The cold gas grows mainly in the wake of the cloud, in tails that reach $0.2$ to $0.5\,\chi r_\mathrm{cl}$, so that the domain must be long and the frame must follow the cloud (3.1).
- The transfer of mass also transfers momentum, which speeds up the acceleration and entrainment ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) 2020a; [Tonnesen and Bryan](#tonnesen-bryan-2021) 2021), even for molecular clouds of $\chi \sim 10^4$ ([Chen and Oh](#chen-oh-2024) 2024).
- Early studies, mostly in two dimensions, with short boxes, without cooling and over short times, could not see these results. Turbulent mixing and cooling need sufficient resolution, three dimensions and radiative cooling, and the long-term growth needs long boxes and long times.

#### 2.3.4 Further physics and realistic winds

Recent work connects several of these effects ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). [Hidalgo-Pineda, Farber and Gronke](#hidalgo-pineda-et-al-2023) (2023) confirmed that magnetic fields alone cannot entrain clouds of $\chi \gtrsim 100$. Together with cooling, however, they shift the criterion of survival by orders of magnitude, to $t_\mathrm{cool,mix}/t_\mathrm{cc} < 100$ for a plasma beta of about one in the wind. [Brüggen, Scannapieco and Grete](#bruggen-et-al-2023) (2023) found that anisotropic conduction together with cooling helps clouds survive in fields parallel to the flow, but has little effect in transverse fields, as the large scan of parameters of [Li et al.](#li-et-al-2020) (2020) also shows. Gas below $10^4$ K, which some earlier simulations included ([Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2017) 2017), has drawn new interest, to extend the criteria to colder gas and to add dust and molecules. These help to tell cold gas formed in the halo from gas carried there from the ISM.

Clouds of non-spherical, turbulent shape change the longer-term evolution little when they fall within the criterion of survival ([Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2017) 2017; [Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) 2020a; among others). Studies of more complex cold gas bridge the gap towards simulations of the full ISM ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.6). The criterion for single spherical clouds still needs generalising, for instance to clouds that shield each other, or that change the mixed gas because the wind engulfs many of them at different places.

The wind itself has drawn less attention ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). A constant, homogeneous wind is unrealistic for galactic winds, above all for the evolution beyond $t_\mathrm{drag}$, since an adiabatically expanding wind drops in pressure and density while its Mach number rises ([Chevalier and Clegg](#chevalier-clegg-1985) 1985). [Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020a) (2020a) built this into their boundary conditions (see also [Dutta and Sharma](#dutta-sharma-2019) 2019). They found that the lower density of the wind slows the transfer of mass, but that the larger surface of the cold gas at lower pressure largely compensates. [Fielding and Bryan](#fielding-bryan-2022) (2022) built the terms of transfer between the phases into a semi-analytic model of a wind of two fluids, which [Nikolis and Gronke](#nikolis-gronke-2024) (2024) compared with simulations of cloud crushing. They showed that the changing background alters the clouds, and that the back-reaction of the cold gas on the hot medium can change the effect of the wind on larger scales considerably.

#### 2.3.5 Infalling clouds

A similar problem of entrainment applies to infalling cold gas, such as coronal rain, gas falling in the ICM or the high-velocity clouds in the halo of the Milky Way ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). The frames of a moving cloud and of a moving medium can be exchanged at some stages, but with an important difference. An outflowing cloud is gradually entrained, so that its shear decreases, while an infalling cloud reaches a terminal velocity and keeps a constant shear, so that it is never safe from destruction. A global box can follow the fall, but is generally too expensive for the details of mixing, and a domain that moves with the cloud, with modified boundary conditions, serves better ([Heitsch and Putman](#heitsch-putman-2009) 2009; 3.1). [Heitsch et al.](#heitsch-et-al-2021) (2021) showed in this way that the mixing and accretion of halo gas can dominate the mass of a high-velocity cloud. They leave gradients of metallicity and velocity along the stretched cloud, which may reveal its history of accretion.

[Tan, Oh and Gronke](#tan-et-al-2023) (2023) found that the nearly constant shear at late times makes the criterion of survival stricter: the time to double the mass, $t_\mathrm{grow} \equiv m/\dot m$, must not exceed $t_\mathrm{cc}$ ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). The accretion of halo gas also sets a terminal velocity $v_\mathrm{t,grow} \sim g\,t_\mathrm{grow}$, with the gravitational acceleration $g$, which can lie well below the usual terminal velocity set by drag. The effect of magnetic fields on such clouds, and on their criterion of survival, is open ([Kaul et al.](#kaul-et-al-2025) 2025).

In sum, simulations of clouds in winds have shown how turbulent mixing, radiative cooling, magnetic fields and geometry shape the evolution and survival of cold gas in hot flows ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). They led to the criteria of survival, uncovered how cold gas grows and is entrained, and underpin the modern models of multiphase gas below the scale of the grid (§3.2). Open questions remain on realistic winds, on the interplay of several processes and on infalling clouds.

#### 2.3.6 Streams and jets

Cold streams that penetrate hot haloes are thought to feed galaxies around cosmic noon ([Birnboim and Dekel](#birnboim-dekel-2003) 2003; [Dekel and Birnboim](#dekel-birnboim-2006) 2006) ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.3). Some cosmological simulations show them, but their existence is debated, partly because these simulations resolve haloes too coarsely to capture the disruption of streams by hydrodynamic instabilities. Dedicated simulations of streams and jets share a set-up. A semi-infinite cylinder of radius $r_\mathrm{c}$ and density $\rho_\mathrm{c}$ lies in a background of density $\rho_\mathrm{b}$ that moves along it with the relative velocity $v$, usually periodic along the cylinder. A jet has $\rho_\mathrm{c} < \rho_\mathrm{b}$, a stream $\rho_\mathrm{c} > \rho_\mathrm{b}$, and the review treats streams, whose dynamics are the more multiphase.

Adiabatic streams overlap much with mixing layers (2.2), with the important difference that above the critical Mach number of eq. 1 they develop unstable body, or reflected, modes, which mix and destroy the stream in a similar way ([Padnos et al.](#padnos-et-al-2018) 2018, among others). However its destruction is defined, it takes about $t_\mathrm{disrupt} \sim \alpha\,r_\mathrm{c}/v$ (eq. 8), with a factor $\alpha$ found by experiment that depends on $v/(c_\mathrm{s,hot} + c_\mathrm{s,cold})$ ([Dimotakis](#dimotakis-1991) 1991). Since $\alpha \sim 10$ for $\chi \sim 100$, $t_\mathrm{disrupt} \approx t_\mathrm{cc}$ for the most common overdensity, and the criterion of eq. 7 and its critical radius hold (footnote 15). For $\chi \sim 1$, however, [Padnos et al.](#padnos-et-al-2018) (2018) found $\alpha \sim 4$ to 10, well above $\chi^{1/2}$. Magnetic fields, thermal conduction or self-gravity can slow the non-linear disruption.

With radiative cooling, streams can develop a multiphase structure, with cold cores inside warmer, turbulent layers ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.3). As for clouds, cooling can be efficient enough to prevent the disruption of the cold gas. Fig. 6, after [Mandelker et al.](#mandelker-et-al-2020) (2020), contrasts a stream that stays intact with cooling with one whose mixed gas soon dominates without it. Mandelker et al. found the criterion $t_\mathrm{cool,mix} < t_\mathrm{disrupt}$ (eq. 9), equivalent to eq. 7 but for the time of destruction. It too becomes a critical radius, below which cold gas is mixed and destroyed faster than it cools, so that only segments of a stream with $r \gtrsim r_\mathrm{crit}$ persist and possibly grow. If galaxy-scale and cosmological simulations do not resolve $r_\mathrm{crit}$, their streams may disrupt artificially or survive too easily, and their predictions of cold inflow, of the fuelling of galaxies and of the structure of the CGM become qualitatively wrong. Over longer times, the inflow onto the stream scales as $t_\mathrm{cool}^{-1/4}$, as in mixing layers (2.2.3). Where jets meet an ambient medium such as the ISM, shocks and contact discontinuities form a complex interface of turbulent mixing and secondary instabilities, which matters for the efficiency of the feedback of AGN in the ICM and the CGM.

### 2.4 The characteristic scale of cooling

#### 2.4.1 Shattering and splattering

Beyond whether and where thermal instability acts (2.7.1), simulations of multiphase media have studied the longer-term, non-linear evolution of the condensing gas and its morphology ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.4). Early work pointed to fragmentation aided by cooling, which makes small cold clouds in a hot medium ([Hennebelle and Pérault](#hennebelle-perault-1999) 1999; [Burkert and Lin](#burkert-lin-2000) 2000). [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) (2018) showed that clouds smaller than the shattering length $l_\mathrm{shatter} \equiv \min(c_\mathrm{s} t_\mathrm{cool})$ (eq. 11) stay in sonic contact with their surroundings and cool isobarically. Larger clouds, $r \gg l_\mathrm{shatter}$, cannot cool as a whole in pressure balance: between $t \sim t_\mathrm{cool}$ and the sound-crossing time $t \sim r/c_\mathrm{s}$ they are strongly underpressurised. McCourt et al. suggested that such clouds fragment as they cool, eventually into clumps of size $l_\mathrm{shatter}$ that stay in sonic contact. They showed the resulting morphology of the cold gas in large two-dimensional simulations and related it to observations of small fragments of cold gas. At about the same time, [Cornuault et al.](#cornuault-et-al-2018) (2018) described a similar fragmentation in streams, but evaluated the cooling length $c_\mathrm{s} t_\mathrm{cool}$ at the conditions behind a shock rather than at its minimum, and did not test their theory with simulations (footnote 18). The simulations of McCourt et al. show a range of sizes of clumps, which may reflect imperfect fragmentation or rapid coagulation (footnote 19).

The nature of fragmentation driven by cooling has since been debated ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.4). [Waters and Proga](#waters-proga-2019b) (2019b) gave a counterpoint to shattering, summarised again in [Waters and Proga](#waters-proga-2023) (2023). From the linear theory of thermal instability and simulations in one dimension, they showed that large clouds can oscillate as they cool. They suspected that the fragmentation of [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) (2018) is numerical, since similar artefacts appeared in their own one-dimensional simulations, and conjectured that a rebound is followed by fragmentation. Three-dimensional simulations show such a process ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2020b) 2020b; [Yao et al.](#yao-et-al-2024) 2024) and extend the criterion of fragmentation, $r \gg l_\mathrm{shatter}$, by a dependence on the final overdensity. The cold clumps then form at the end of a violent pulsation, which supports the picture that [Waters and Proga](#waters-proga-2019b) (2019b) called splattering. Fig. 7 contrasts the two pathways: a cloud far out of pressure balance that pulsates violently and sheds its outer layers, and a continuous fragmentation in pressure balance into ever smaller clouds of size $\sim c_\mathrm{s} t_\mathrm{cool}$, which McCourt et al. envisaged as shattering.

#### 2.4.2 Open questions on fragmentation

The review lists the open questions ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.4):

- Dimensionality. Some studies, especially earlier ones, ran in one dimension, while the fragmentation of a surface aided by the KH instability, or fragmentation in a turbulent medium ([Gronke et al.](#gronke-et-al-2022) 2022, among others), occur only in several dimensions. [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) (2018) state that shattering cannot occur in one dimension, yet [Das, Choudhury and Sharma](#das-et-al-2021) (2021) support and [Waters and Proga](#waters-proga-2019b) (2019b) oppose shattering with one-dimensional simulations. [Farber and Gronke](#farber-gronke-2023) (2023) found that the rotation of clumps continues their fragmentation after the expansion, which they call splintering (footnote 20).
- Further physics, chiefly thermal conduction. The Field length might set the characteristic scale after all, but conclusions differ widely, mainly because the effect of tangled magnetic fields is uncertain ([Burkert and Lin](#burkert-lin-2000) 2000; [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) 2018; [Waters and Proga](#waters-proga-2019b) 2019b; among others).
- The coagulation of droplets, during the cooling and later, and its relation to the criterion of fragmentation. Coagulation in multiphase media has long been known, but only recently have studies turned to coagulation driven by cooling, in which clumps are carried along by the hot gas that accretes onto them ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2023) 2023, among others). It is slow, but depends strongly on the distance between droplets, and may matter in the early stages of fragmentation.
- Initial conditions. [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2018) (2018) used a truncated Gaussian random field, more recent studies simpler top-hat or solenoidal perturbations, which may change the evolution and its outcome.

The latest literature is inconclusive, which terms that differ between studies do not help. Open are whether a continuous fragmentation like that of Jeans occurs, whether multiphase gas has a characteristic scale, and how far turbulent motions alone fragment clouds, without shattering driven by cooling.

### 2.5 Measures of mixing

#### 2.5.1 Thermal state, kinematics and morphology

Most simulations of multiphase gas ask, in some form, for the thermal state of the medium ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.3). The full distribution of the gas in the plane of temperature and density captures it (Fig. 2, right). In practice it is often binned into ranges of temperature, which give the mass and the volume of each phase. The ranges differ between studies and between fields, which makes their results harder to compare. For the evolution in time, the fluxes between the phases, of mass, $\dot m$, or of momentum, $\dot p$, are of particular interest, and at the coarsest level whether a phase exists or vanishes.

The kinematics of the phases, how they move with respect to each other, is measured by the mean difference of velocity between two phases, $\Delta v$, weighted by mass or taken at given places, or by the distribution of the gas in temperature and velocity ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.3). The turbulent motions are analysed through velocity structure functions (VSF) and spectra of kinetic power. The spatial distribution and morphology of the phases link simulations to observations, through the filling fractions of area and volume, the distributions of the sizes of clumps and the coherence of the phases, which also show how gas fragments and mixes. Codes of radiative transfer, such as SKIRT ([Camps and Baes](#camps-baes-2015) 2015) and Trident ([Hummels, Smith and Silvia](#hummels-et-al-2017) 2017), turn simulations into synthetic spectra, to be compared with the lines of sight towards quasars and with other spectral diagnostics.

The review counts the variety of diagnostics among the challenges common to the field ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3.3). It differs between communities that analyse their results differently, and still more between simulations of different kinds, so that it remains open how set-ups of different scales can inform each other. Models below the grid (2.6.5), adaptive refinement and so-called handshake simulations may bridge the scales. Since observations remain the benchmark, models of radiative transfer and tools for synthetic observations are essential for quantitative comparisons.

#### 2.5.2 Global quantities of a shocked cloud

[Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994, §3) compress each simulation into global quantities, integrals over the cloud material of mass $m_\mathrm{c}$. From the moments $\langle r^2 \rangle$, $\langle z \rangle$ and $\langle z^2 \rangle$, weighted by the density $\rho_\mathrm{c}$ of cloud material (eqs. 3.4–3.6), they define the effective radius across the axis, $a = [\frac{5}{2}\langle r^2 \rangle]^{1/2}$, and along it, $c = [5(\langle z^2 \rangle - \langle z \rangle^2)]^{1/2}$ (eqs. 3.7, 3.8), whose factors make them the true radii of a uniform ellipsoid, and the aspect ratio $c/a$. Further quantities are the mean density $\langle \rho_\mathrm{c} \rangle = m_\mathrm{c}/V_\mathrm{c}$, with the volume $V_\mathrm{c}$ occupied by cloud material, the mean velocity of the cloud relative to the shocked intercloud gas, its dispersions along and across the axis, and the circulation $\Gamma = \int \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\boldsymbol{A}$. The mean density is hard to compute for $\chi > 10$, since at late times much of the cloud mass lies in zones that also hold intercloud gas, whose share of the volume the two-fluid code knows less well than their mass. Four times describe the evolution. The drag time $t_\mathrm{drag}$ is the time by which the relative velocity has fallen by a factor $e$. The destruction time $t_\mathrm{dest}$ is the time by which the mass of the core, the gas connected to the main fragment on the axis, has fallen to $1/e$ of the initial mass. The time $t_\mathrm{m}$ is the time at which the radius across the axis first comes within 10 per cent of its largest value. The mixing time $t_\mathrm{mix}$ is the time at which the mix fraction, the fraction of the cloud mass in zones that hold both cloud and intercloud material, reaches one half (§8). The destruction time can jump when a few zones decide whether the core splits, while the mixing time, although its definition depends on the resolution, converges (2.6.6).

### 2.6 Numerical resolution and convergence

#### 2.6.1 The difficulty of multiphase media

The contrasts of temperature in astrophysics, and the contrasts of density that come with them, range from about $10^2$ to $\gtrsim 10^{10}$, which strains numerical methods and makes adaptive techniques such as smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) problematic ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.2). The phases have different length scales, so the problem is multi-scale. In the CGM and the ICM, cold gas can moreover lie in very small structures spread over a large volume. Adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), which adapts the size of the cells to the local density, then becomes inadequate or too expensive. Observations find cold structures in the CGM as small as tens of parsecs. With at least eight cells across a structure (footnote 5), a halo of the mass of the Milky Way would need $(8 \times 100\,\mathrm{kpc}/10\,\mathrm{pc})^3 \approx 5 \times 10^{14}$ resolution elements, of order $10^{15}$, against about $10^{11}$ in the largest simulations of fixed resolution to date (footnote 6, §3.1). Multiphase quantities such as the mass of cold gas are therefore not converged in typical cosmological simulations ([Faucher-Giguère et al.](#faucher-giguere-et-al-2016) 2016), as simulations that refine the CGM systematically have shown (§2.8.3).

The physics adds to the difficulty ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.2). Turbulence mixes and destroys the phases, and radiative cooling and heating exchange mass and momentum between them. Thermal conduction and viscosity shape the temperature and the turbulence, metals, molecules and their chemistry set the cooling, and magnetic fields and cosmic rays add pressure and act on the mixing directly. The nature of the heating and of the drivers of turbulence matters as well. Since no simulation can yet treat all the scales and processes together, most address particular questions or systems.

#### 2.6.2 No universal criterion

For gravitational collapse, the criterion of [Truelove et al.](#truelove-et-al-1997) (1997) sets a clear and widely adopted demand on resolution, but no single criterion holds for multiphase flows ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §1.3). The scale to resolve depends on the quantity of interest. Converged rates of mass transfer, morphologies or kinematics generally need the characteristic scale of the process resolved by at least a few cells. §2 of the review names these scales together with the diagnostics that they make converge, and the subjects of this synthesis take them up in turn. **[GK]** The criterion of [Truelove et al.](#truelove-et-al-1997) (1997) asks that the Jeans length span at least four cells.

#### 2.6.3 Mixing layers

In mixing layers, the largest eddies limit mixing and cooling (2.2.3), so that larger simulations need only the driving scale of a layer resolved for its rate of mass transfer to converge ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.1). A comparison with observations of ions such as O VI needs in addition the Field length, after [Field](#field-1965) (1965), to be resolved, or its effect to be added in post-processing. With the conduction of [Spitzer](#spitzer-1962) (1962), the Field length shrinks steeply towards low temperatures, as $T^{5/2}$ by the review, so a simulation may leave it unresolved below a cutoff temperature. [Tan and Oh](#tan-oh-2021) (2021) showed that the distribution of temperature converges only above this cutoff (footnote 10). [Fielding et al.](#fielding-et-al-2020) (2020) showed that a layer that does not resolve the smallest value of $c_\mathrm{s} t_\mathrm{cool}$, the shattering length that §2.4 of the review treats, develops numerical dips of pressure across it (footnote 9). Such simulations must also reach a steady state in a box wide enough across the flow (2.2.2). **[GK]** The Field length is the scale on which conduction balances cooling, $\kappa T/\lambda_\mathrm{F}^2 \sim n^2 \Lambda(T)$, with the conductivity $\kappa$ and the cooling function $\Lambda$. With $\kappa \propto T^{5/2}$ and $n \propto 1/T$ at fixed pressure, $\lambda_\mathrm{F} \propto T^{11/4}\,\Lambda(T)^{-1/2}$. The power $5/2$ that the review gives holds where $\Lambda \propto T^{1/2}$, as for bremsstrahlung.

#### 2.6.4 Clouds in winds

What convergence means for the crushing of clouds, and when it is reached, has long been debated ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). [Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994) found that their two-dimensional, adiabatic simulations needed about 100 cells per cloud radius for accurate results, by the review 100 to 200 (2.6.6). [Nakamura et al.](#nakamura-et-al-2006) (2006, fig. 1) showed that the shape and displacement of the cloud converge to within 10 per cent of their finest simulation for $\gtrsim 100$ cells per radius. Experiments with the Nova laser offered a benchmark beyond comparisons with finer simulations ([Klein et al.](#klein-et-al-2000) 2000, [2003](#klein-et-al-2003); footnote 14). For radiatively cooling clouds, [Yirak, Frank and Cunningham](#yirak-et-al-2010) (2010) found no clear convergence of the same global quantities up to 1536 cells per radius, which they ascribed to changes in the structure of the radiative shock.

In three-dimensional radiative simulations, scans of resolution typically span 8 to 128 cells per radius ([Cooper et al.](#cooper-et-al-2009) 2009; [Scannapieco and Brüggen](#scannapieco-bruggen-2015) 2015; [Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2017) 2017). The detailed morphology, such as the number of fragments ([Cooper et al.](#cooper-et-al-2009) 2009), stays unconverged, but the overall rate at which the phases mix converges, although its evolution can be stochastic and non-monotonic in the resolution. Studies that find growth of the cold mass accordingly report modest demands: about 8 cells per radius appear to capture the growth ([Gronke and Oh](#gronke-oh-2018) 2018; [Abruzzo, Fielding and Bryan](#abruzzo-fielding-bryan-2022) 2022). These tests were mostly run for transonic flows, $\mathcal{M} \sim 1.5$, and overdensities $\chi \sim 100$. [Gronke et al.](#gronke-et-al-2022) (2022) showed that the growth converges less readily at higher Mach numbers, but no systematic study exists. As for mixing layers (2.6.3), the distribution of temperature converges much less readily, and likely needs the Field length to be resolved ([Abruzzo, Fielding and Bryan](#abruzzo-fielding-bryan-2022) 2022).

The review asks how these demands map onto the critical radius $r_\mathrm{crit}$ (2.3.1), below which cold gas is quickly mixed away and above which it can grow ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). If $r_\mathrm{crit}$ sets the minimum size of long-lived structures, a simulation that does not resolve it may evolve qualitatively wrongly in the long term, so that $r_\mathrm{crit}$ may offer a criterion of convergence founded in physics. The same holds for the critical radius of streams (2.3.6).

#### 2.6.5 The gap to galaxies and models below the grid

Even with growing computing power, about four orders of magnitude separate the resolutions feasible today from those needed to resolve the multiphase gas of a single halo like that of the Milky Way ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3.1; 2.6.1). To resolve both the Field length, which governs the distribution of gas at intermediate temperatures (2.6.3) and can lie below a parsec, and the inflows of cold gas through haloes of hundreds of kiloparsecs takes more than six orders of magnitude in length, or 18 in mass (§3.2). Large simulations therefore need models below the grid, as they do for feedback and star formation. Classical models of the multiphase ISM were early examples. Newer ones treat the phases more explicitly, often as several fluids, and reach into the CGM and the ICM: two-phase schemes in smoothed particle hydrodynamics, a cold phase decoupled from the hot medium, cold particles embedded in cells of hot gas, and two interpenetrating fluids. Particles can represent several bulk flows in a cell but struggle with the dispersion of velocities. Two fluids represent the dispersion and any filling fraction, but only one mean velocity per phase.

The review names four challenges for such models ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3.2). A model must give converged results whether or not the colder phase is resolved, which calls for comparisons with fully resolved simulations of idealised set-ups and for coupling terms founded in physics with few free parameters. Its coupling terms rest on small-scale simulations whose uncertainties, such as the effects of viscosity and conduction on mixing, carry over, and current models omit magnetic fields and mostly treat two phases. It must work together with the models of feedback and star formation of cosmological simulations and stay affordable without much tuning. Since it does not follow the unresolved morphology of the cold gas or the gas at intermediate temperatures, tracers such as O VI must be painted on in post-processing.

#### 2.6.6 Resolution in the shock–cloud problem

[Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994) measure resolution by $R_n$, $n$ zones of the finest level per initial cloud radius (§3, Table 1). Convergence studies, announced for a second paper, showed that about 100 zones per radius are needed to represent the hydrodynamics adequately, about three times more than earlier calculations used, and that $R_{120}$ gives accurate results (abstract, §1, §3). Radiative losses need still more, since they compress the cloud more. Their runs span $R_{30}$ to $R_{240}$, most at $R_{60}$ or $R_{120}$. Some quantities converge and others do not. The circulation converges, since numerical viscosity does not affect it significantly, while the peak vorticity, set by the thickness of the shocks and shear layers, scales as $(\Delta x)^{-1}$ from $R_{30}$ to $R_{240}$ (§7.2). The mixing time converges although its definition rests on the grid, because the large-scale growth of the instabilities sets the time of fragmentation (§8). The Richtmyer–Meshkov instability, seeded by the mesh, loses importance as the resolution grows (§5.1). **[GK]** The second paper of the series that appeared, [Nakamura et al.](#nakamura-et-al-2006) (2006), treats smooth cloud boundaries (2.6.4) rather than this study of convergence.

### 2.7 Fountain clouds and the condensation of the corona

#### 2.7.1 Thermal instability in haloes

Thermal instability makes a multiphase medium directly: local overdensities cool slightly faster than the thermally stable background, grow denser and cool faster still ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.4; [Field](#field-1965) 1965). The details are intricate. In a stratified hot atmosphere that is stable against convection, condensation was long thought impossible, since large-scale inflows advect the perturbations before they can grow ([Balbus and Soker](#balbus-soker-1989) 1989, among others). [Sharma et al.](#sharma-et-al-2012) (2012) and [McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2012) (2012) showed that thermal instability is possible when heating suppresses these inflows and the ratio of the cooling to the free-fall time lies below a constant of order unity, $t_\mathrm{cool}/t_\mathrm{ff} \lesssim O(1)$ (eq. 10). The reviews of [Donahue and Voit](#donahue-voit-2022) (2022) and [Faucher-Giguère and Oh](#faucher-giguere-oh-2023) (2023) treat thermal instability in full, and Gronke and Schneider focus on recent simulations, which modify the criterion in several ways:

- Fast uplift of a parcel of gas keeps its cooling time nearly constant while its free-fall time grows strongly, which can satisfy eq. 10 and trigger the instability. Early work discussed this ([Shapiro and Field](#shapiro-field-1976) 1976; [Bregman](#bregman-1980) 1980), and simulations on the scale of haloes have since shown it ([Li and Bryan](#li-bryan-2014) 2014, among others).
- In a rotating halo the effective free-fall time is longer, which makes the halo more prone to the instability ([Sobacchi and Sormani](#sobacchi-sormani-2019) 2019).
- Local overdensities shorten the cooling time and raise the probability of precipitation ([Choudhury, Sharma and Quataert](#choudhury-et-al-2019) 2019). Eq. 10 then holds locally, with the overdensity in the cooling time, while the global criterion at a given radius has a lower threshold.
- Turbulence forms such overdensities. [Gaspari et al.](#gaspari-et-al-2018) (2018) proposed $t_\mathrm{cool}/t_\mathrm{eddy} \lesssim 1$, with the turnover time $t_\mathrm{eddy}$ of eddies on a relevant scale in the ICM, and analogous criteria hold in the ISM. [Mohapatra et al.](#mohapatra-et-al-2023) (2023) combined it with eq. 10 into a new empirical relation. This criterion of precipitation differs from the criterion of survival in a turbulent medium, which relates to the time of destruction of cold gas and resembles eq. 7 ([Gronke et al.](#gronke-et-al-2022) 2022; footnote 16).
- Even weak magnetic fields, down to $\beta \sim 100$, make precipitation easier, since they suppress buoyant oscillations on scales below about $v_\mathrm{A} t_\mathrm{cool}$, and the cold gas that forms is more filamentary ([Ji, Oh and McCourt](#ji-et-al-2017) 2017).
- Cosmic rays that heat the gas change the linear criterion ([Kempski and Quataert](#kempski-quataert-2020) 2020), and in the non-linear stage the morphology and dynamics of the multiphase gas. Their non-thermal pressure can lower the overdensity of the cold gas considerably. Methods of two moments for the transport of cosmic rays have recently made more realistic transport possible in multiphase simulations (footnote 17).

The theory stands on solid ground, but its application to cosmological simulations is less certain ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.4). In the FIRE and Illustris-TNG50 simulations, the ratio $t_\mathrm{cool}/t_\mathrm{ff}$ of a parcel of gas in a halo predicts its thermal evolution poorly ([Esmerian et al.](#esmerian-et-al-2020) 2020; [Nelson et al.](#nelson-et-al-2020) 2020), perhaps because the hot medium in FIRE flows in as a cooling flow. Observations remain the final benchmark.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture records the codes and set-ups of the simulations, as far as the papers describe them. §1 and §2.1 of the review name no code of the simulations that they discuss. 2.2.1 describes the set-up of the shear layer, and 2.5 the codes of radiative transfer that §1.3 names.

### 3.1 Wind tunnels

In a wind tunnel, a hot wind blows over a cold cloud, usually spherical ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §2.2). Since the cold gas grows mainly in the wake, in tails of up to $0.5\,\chi r_\mathrm{cl}$ (2.3.3), the domain must be long. Techniques of cloud tracking change the frame of reference continuously to keep the cloud inside it ([McCourt et al.](#mccourt-et-al-2015) 2015; [Brüggen and Scannapieco](#bruggen-scannapieco-2016) 2016, among others), and [Dutta and Sharma](#dutta-sharma-2019) (2019) describe them in detail. Boundary conditions can impose an expanding wind (2.3.4). Infalling clouds are followed in a domain that moves with the cloud, whose boundary conditions are modified accordingly (2.3.5).

### 3.2 Codes and hardware

The review traces the growth of the field to that of computing ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3.1). For many multiphase problems, such as mixing layers, turbulent boxes and outflows, refinement by mass fails, since instabilities must be resolved in gas that may be thinner than the median, and adaptive refinement makes robust studies of convergence hard or impossible. Apart from devices for particular set-ups, such as cloud tracking or refinement on the cooling length in the CGM, these simulations need expensive domains of fixed resolution or of targeted static refinement, a main reason why many early models ran in one or two dimensions. Their physics, however, is mostly local. MHD, optically thin cooling and many models of radiative transfer and of the transport of cosmic rays need only a local stencil, which suits massive parallelism.

Clock speeds stopped growing in the mid-2000s, so that power came from more cores, served by a first generation of parallel codes, among them FLASH, RAMSES, GADGET, Athena, PIERNIK, AREPO and Enzo ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3.1). Graphics processing units (GPUs), with thousands of slow cores on a chip and most of their transistors spent on arithmetic rather than control, then opened a wide gap in peak performance over CPUs (Fig. 12). High-level programming models such as CUDA brought them into science from the early 2010s. The codes GAMER and Cholla ([Schneider and Robertson](#schneider-robertson-2015) 2015) showed large speed-ups early, provided that the data were managed carefully and the GPUs had enough work. Newer codes for modern machines include DISPATCH, Phantom, QUOKKA, Parthenon, SWIFT and AthenaK. They make simulations of more than $10^{10}$ resolution elements feasible, and parameter studies at lower resolution cheap.

Challenges remain ([Gronke and Schneider](#gronke-schneider-2026) 2026, §3.1). Adaptive time-stepping, which lets elements take different steps instead of all following the fastest signal, saves much time, but is hard to balance across nodes and to port to GPUs, since the elements then follow different paths of control. Languages for GPUs are not standardised: CUDA is proprietary and runs only on NVIDIA hardware, other vendors offer HIP or SYCL, and frameworks such as Kokkos, RAJA, OpenACC and OpenMP make code portable across CPUs and GPUs, although with fewer features. Data are a last challenge, since a snapshot of tens of billions of elements fills terabytes. Analysis must therefore move into the running simulation, through tools that are often specific to one code, through modular libraries that plug into several, or through public archives.

### 3.3 The code of Klein, McKee and Colella

The code of [Klein, McKee and Colella](#klein-et-al-1994) (1994, §3) is two-dimensional and axisymmetric, a second-order Godunov scheme on a local adaptive mesh ([Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) 1984; [Berger and Colella](#berger-colella-1989) 1989). A rectangular grid covers the domain, and nested rectangular meshes refine it, in three levels with a factor of 4 in each direction, so that a cell of level 1 holds 256 cells of level 3. Estimates of the local truncation error by Richardson extrapolation decide where to refine, and the maximum level is also set directly: every cell that holds cloud material is refined to level 3, while intercloud gas away from the cloud stays at lower levels. The code carries two fluids and maintains the interface between cloud and intercloud gas throughout, with a no-slip condition at the interface, and the refinement acts on both fluids. A planar shock runs along the axis of symmetry. The calculation runs in the frame of the shocked intercloud gas, in which the cloud eventually comes to rest, so that initially the shock moves at $v'_\mathrm{b} = (\frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4M^2})\,v_\mathrm{b}$ and the cloud at $-\frac{3}{4}(1 - M^{-2})\,v_\mathrm{b}$, for $\gamma = 5/3$ (eqs. 3.1, 3.2). The domain is $16a_0$ long and $3a_0$ in radius, and at $R_{120}$ the cloud radius spans 7.5 cells of level 1. The runs, listed in Table 1, cover contrasts of 3 to 400 and Mach numbers of 10, 100 and 1000, and mostly last about four crushing times, the standard case $9.66\,t_\mathrm{cc}$. They ran on a Cray Y-MP 8/128 at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (acknowledgements).
