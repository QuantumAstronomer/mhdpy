# Synthesis of Ulula

This synthesis digests Ulula, a hydrodynamics code for two dimensions written in Python, in its release 1.0.2, kept with its documentation as `reference-works/Ulula/Code`. The code paper completes the sources, kept as `reference-works/Ulula/Docs/2505.20399v1.pdf`: the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025) ([Diemer](#diemer-2025) 2025, [arXiv:2505.20399v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20399v1)). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in these sources or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. Code is cited by its path in the repository and the name of a function or class, as in (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `Simulation.riemannSolverHLLC`), and a page of the documentation by its path. A section sign without a named source refers to the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025).

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin, version and licence

Ulula is a hydrodynamics code meant for teaching and for numerical experiments, by Benedikt Diemer of the University of Maryland (`README.md`). It was developed alongside ASTR 670, a graduate course on hydrodynamics taught there, whose students tried it out in class (acknowledgements of the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025)). The name plays on that of Euler, which contains Eule, the German word for owl. Ulula is Latin for owl, and Latin is the language of most of Euler's papers (`docs/source/index.rst`).

Version 0.1.0 of 24 March 2021 held the hydrodynamic solver and its examples (`docs/source/versions.rst`). Version 0.1.1 reorganised the code and added the Sedov–Taylor explosion and plots of radial averages in one dimension. Version 0.2.0 saved simulations to files in HDF5, the Hierarchical Data Format, which serve both as snapshots and for restarts, and kept the primitive and conserved fluid quantities in dictionaries. Version 0.2.1 reworked the runtime interface, so that files, plots and movies can follow evenly spaced times, all at once and without altering the run, and 0.2.2 checks that a restart runs to a later time than that of its file. Nearly four years later, 0.3.0 of January 2025 brought gravity, by a fixed acceleration or a fixed potential that the user sets, reflecting walls as boundaries, code units with plots in any system of units, and the handling of violations of the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition. It also added automatic checks of conservation and setups for cloud crushing, free fall, the Rayleigh–Taylor instability and tidal disruption. Version 0.4.0 made simulations in one dimension proper ones and added the isothermal equation of state, updates and boundary conditions that the user defines for the run, and setups for an atmosphere and a sound wave. It replaced global index variables by indexing that adapts to the variables in use. Version 0.4.1 added the HLLC Riemann solver, the Gresho vortex, advection in one dimension and options for plots, among them temperatures in kelvin and radial and azimuthal velocities. Version 0.5.0 of 7 May 2025 brought self-gravity from the Poisson equation for periodic boundaries, setups for the Jeans instability and a Keplerian disk, an atmosphere of varying temperature, and much of the documentation. Version 1.0.0 of 13 May 2025 made its u/v notation consistent across code and documentation. Version 1.0.1 of 26 May added the merger setup and plots in one dimension with panels stacked vertically, and 1.0.2 of 19 November 2025 moved the build to `pyproject.toml`, a change by Miha Cernetic.

The code is kept at [bitbucket.org/bdiemer/ulula](https://bitbucket.org/bdiemer/ulula), and its documentation is published at [bdiemer.bitbucket.io/ulula](https://bdiemer.bitbucket.io/ulula) (`README.md`; §1). **[GK]** PyPI, the Python Package Index, holds every release from 0.1.0 to 1.0.2, among them 0.3.1 and 0.3.2 of 8 January 2025, which the history does not list, and its dates fall on the day that the history gives or the day after. The repository has no tags. The head of its branch master is commit 946dfb1 of 19 November 2025, which carries the version 1.0.2. Between 1.0.1 and 1.0.2 its commits also dropped the support of Python 2, with Python 3.6 as the lowest version, and updated the instructions for a developer installation. The local copy agrees in content with the source distribution of 1.0.2 on PyPI in its 30 Python files, `pyproject.toml`, `README.rst` and `LICENSE.txt`, and with commit 946dfb1 in the eleven pages of `docs/source/`. Its other files, `README.md`, the makefile of the documentation and the images, agree with that commit in size, and it lacks only the file `.gitignore` of the repository. The published documentation states no version.

Ulula is distributed under the MIT licence, with the copyright for 2020 to 2025 held by Diemer (`LICENSE.txt`). The README presents the code as being tested and offers no warranty. The version history credits Calvin Osinga with the code for movies in the GIF format and Philip Mansfield with the computation of overlaps between annuli and squares in radial plots. It names Gandalf, the code of [Hubber et al.](#hubber-et-al-2018) (2018, [doi:10.1093/mnras/stx2405](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stx2405)), as the origin of the exact solution of the Sedov–Taylor explosion. The [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025), by Diemer alone, adds that the exact solution of the shock tube follows lecture notes by Frank [van den Bosch](#van-den-bosch) and Susanne [Höfner](#hofner), that Philip Mocz commented on a draft, and that the National Science Foundation, under grant 2338388, and the Sloan Foundation supported the work (acknowledgements). **[GK]** arXiv holds only the first version of the paper, of 26 May 2025, without a reference to a journal.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

The [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025) explains the need for such a code (§1). Gravity and hydrodynamics are arguably the dominant physical processes in the Universe, and hydrodynamics governs systems from gas planets and stars to the interstellar and intergalactic media, clusters of galaxies and the early Universe, yet physics curricula for undergraduates often leave it out. Few of its problems have analytical solutions, sound waves and hydrostatic atmospheres among them, so that research depends on numerical solutions, which teaching on paper does not convey. Simple methods for partial differential equations fail. Finite differences are quickly taught, but they neither keep the solution positive nor conserve mass, and the discontinuities of the non-linear Euler equations resisted a long search for solvers, until finite-volume schemes brought the first real success ([Godunov](#godunov-1959) 1959). How such solvers work is hard to convey without experiments. Writing one from the start takes too long for a course, while production codes bury the equations in code for adaptive grids, parallelisation, input and output, and further physics. The need to compile them from C or Fortran can also rule them out for homework.

Ulula is the paper's answer: a small code for hydrodynamics and gravity in two dimensions, written to be brief and legible, which still offers fast algorithms of the kind used in research (§1). The whole solver fits into one Python file of fewer than 800 active lines. It holds a MUSCL–Hancock scheme whose Riemann solvers, reconstruction schemes, boundary conditions and equations of state can be exchanged (abstract). Two dimensions balance simplicity against performance. One dimension soon becomes dull, since many problems of hydrodynamics are multi-dimensional by nature, while problems in three dimensions take too long without parallelisation. For simplicity Ulula uses only Python and NumPy, with no further library to install or compile, JAX and Numba for instance. Extensive online documentation and heavy comments serve its teaching. Two lines of code suffice to run a prepared setup, to try other solvers and to make plots and movies. The documentation names the components that the brevity makes easy to find, exchange and modify: spatial interpolation, slope limiter, Riemann solver and time stepping (`docs/source/index.rst`). **[GK]** Counted without blank lines, comments and docstrings, `ulula/core/simulation.py` has 783 lines at release 1.0.2.

Ulula is not meant for scientific production runs, as it runs on one processor and on a grid of fixed size (`docs/source/index.rst`). Since NumPy carries out all its operations on arrays, it is still fairly fast on one processor. Many of its test problems take under a minute on a current laptop, and the abstract speaks of a few minutes.

Ulula is not the first code in Python for teaching hydrodynamics (§1). pyro ([Zingale](#zingale-2014) 2014; [Harpole et al.](#harpole-et-al-2019) 2019), digested in `pyro-synthesis.md`, has a similar aim and comes with an open-source text on numerical hydrodynamics ([Zingale](#zingale-2021) 2021), digested in `computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md`. The two codes are built differently. Ulula is one framework of interchangeable components, while pyro holds many solvers for particular problems, such as advection, relaxation by multigrid, diffusion and incompressible flow, so that its code is many times larger. pyro also speeds up some routines by compiling them just in time, where Ulula relies on NumPy alone. Shorter and simpler still are single scripts for one problem, such as the "Create your own" series of [Mocz](#mocz), which solves problems like the Rayleigh–Taylor instability in as few lines as possible. At the other end of the range, compiled libraries have made Python fast enough for production codes such as PySPH ([Ramachandran et al.](#ramachandran-et-al-2019) 2019) and Dedalus ([Burns et al.](#burns-et-al-2020) 2020), which serve research and far exceed Ulula in size and complexity. **[GK]** The synthesis of Pyro bears out both differences. Since version 3.0, pyro compiles its kernels with Numba at run time, and its Python without the tests comes to about 20,000 lines in the sizes that `plan.md` gives for its steps, three times the 6,700 lines of Ulula.

The paper names the limits of the code (§5). Ulula is of second order, knows only Cartesian grids in one and two dimensions, runs on one processor, and has neither viscosity nor radiative cooling. Physics that behaves differently in three dimensions is left out on purpose. Most interesting problems of magnetohydrodynamics are three-dimensional, and turbulence differs in kind in three dimensions, so that no setup of turbulence is included. The paper concludes that a framework of fewer than 800 active lines need not give up the fidelity of its algorithms or their speed, which the operations of NumPy on one processor limit. It sees Ulula as a platform for quick experiments with a low threshold, rather than as a monolithic solver.

## 2. Numerical methods

Eight subjects group the numerical methods by component. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

Ulula solves its equations with a Godunov scheme whose components can be exchanged (§3; Table 1). Two Riemann solvers compute the fluxes between cells: HLL, which ignores contact discontinuities, and HLLC, which restores them (2.4). The states on either side of an interface come either from constant data in each cell, which gives first order in space, or from a linear reconstruction of second order (2.5). Its slope is limited by the minmod limiter, the most cautious, by the limiter of van Leer, or by the monotonised central (MC) limiter, the most aggressive. In time, the states are either taken as they are, which the paper calls the Euler integrator, of first order and unstable, or advanced by half a time step in the Hancock step, of second order (2.6). The Hancock step works in conserved or in primitive variables, and the latter is faster. The class `HydroScheme` names these choices by identifiers: `const` or `linear` for the reconstruction, `none`, `minmod`, `vanleer` or `mc` for the limiter, `hll` or `hllc` for the Riemann solver, and `euler`, `hancock` or `hancock_cons` for the time integration (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `HydroScheme`; `docs/source/simulation.rst`). Its defaults, linear reconstruction with the MC limiter, the HLLC solver and the Hancock step in primitive variables, together form the MUSCL–Hancock scheme. The limiter `none` takes the central slope without limiting and gives a highly unstable scheme, while constant reconstruction is highly diffusive, and both serve only as demonstrations. HLLC cannot be combined with an isothermal gas, which has no contact discontinuities. Otherwise the code accepts any combination, unstable ones such as constant states with the Hancock step included, since experiments with failing algorithms are part of its purpose.

### 2.1 The equations and equations of state

#### 2.1.1 The Euler equations with gravity

Ulula solves the Euler equations of a compressible, inviscid fluid under gravity, written as conservation laws of mass, momentum and total energy (§2, eq. 1). At a fixed point, the density of each conserved quantity falls where its flux diverges and rises where it converges, and sources add to it (`docs/source/simulation.rst`). With the density $\rho$, the velocity $\boldsymbol{v} = (v_x, v_y)$, the pressure $P$, the internal energy per unit mass $\varepsilon$ and the gravitational potential $\Phi$, the equations read $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{v}) = 0$, $\partial(\rho\boldsymbol{v})/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v} + \mathbf{I}P) = -\rho\nabla\Phi$ and $\partial E/\partial t + \nabla\cdot[(E + P)\boldsymbol{v}] = \rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t$. The total energy per unit volume, $E = \rho(v^2/2 + \varepsilon + \Phi)$, includes the potential energy. As one vector equation, $\partial\boldsymbol{U}/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}(\boldsymbol{U}) = \boldsymbol{S}$ (eq. 2), the system has the conserved variables $\boldsymbol{U} = (\rho, \rho v_x, \rho v_y, E)$ and the sources $\boldsymbol{S} = (0, -\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial x, -\rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial y, \rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t)$. The fluxes of mass and energy are $\rho\boldsymbol{v}$ and $(E + P)\boldsymbol{v}$, and those of the two momenta $(\rho v_x^2 + P, \rho v_x v_y)$ and $(\rho v_x v_y, \rho v_y^2 + P)$ (eq. 3). The documentation writes the momentum fluxes with a Kronecker delta, which adds $P$ only to the flux of $\rho v_x$ in $x$ and to that of $\rho v_y$ in $y$. Fluxes and sources need the pressure and velocity, so that the primitive variables $\boldsymbol{V} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, P)$ complete the description, and the code converts often between the two sets (2.1.3). The potential is either imposed or follows from the density by the Poisson equation $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, with the gravitational constant $G$ (eq. 5). Fixed potentials of the user can stand for a constant acceleration or for masses that are not simulated hydrodynamically (2.7).

**[GK]** The source of the energy equation follows from the potential energy in $E$. Without it, the kinetic and internal energy change by the work $-\rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Phi$, while the continuity equation gives $\partial(\rho\Phi)/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\Phi\boldsymbol{v}) = \rho\,\partial\Phi/\partial t + \rho\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\Phi$ for the potential energy. Their sum is the energy equation of eq. 1. In a potential that is constant in time, the total energy therefore has no source and is conserved, while the momentum keeps its source.

#### 2.1.2 Equations of state

An equation of state closes the system by relating pressure and internal energy (§2). For an ideal gas, $P = nk_\mathrm{B}T = \rho k_\mathrm{B}T/(\mu m_\mathrm{p}) = \rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, with the number density $n$, Boltzmann's constant $k_\mathrm{B}$, the temperature $T$ in kelvin, the mean particle mass $\mu$ in units of the proton mass $m_\mathrm{p}$ and the adiabatic index $\gamma$ (eq. 4). Its sound speed is $c_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$. An isothermal gas keeps $T$, and so $\varepsilon$, constant. It is barotropic, as its pressure depends on the density alone. The energy equation can then be dropped, since density and the fixed internal energy determine the energy, and the sound speed is $c_\mathrm{s} = \sqrt{P/\rho}$.

`Simulation.setEquationOfState` chooses the mode `ideal`, the default with $\gamma = 5/3$, or `isothermal` (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). Its docstring advises $\gamma = 5/3$ for atomic and $7/5$ for diatomic molecular gases. The mean particle mass is needed, in either mode, only to plot temperatures. The isothermal mode requires the fixed internal energy per unit mass in code units. `Setup.internalEnergyFromTemperature` computes it from a temperature as $\varepsilon = k_\mathrm{B}T/[(\gamma - 1)\mu m_\mathrm{p}]$, converted with the square of the ratio of the code units of time and length (`ulula/core/setup_base.py`). The code takes the isothermal pressure as $P = \rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, which equals $\rho k_\mathrm{B}T/(\mu m_\mathrm{p})$, so that $\gamma$ cancels, and the sound speed as $\sqrt{\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)}$, the same in every cell (`Simulation.soundSpeed`). A flag, `track_pressure`, is true for the ideal gas and false for the isothermal one, and decides whether pressure and total energy are carried as variables at all (3.2). `Simulation.soundSpeed` stops with an error where the sound speed is not a number, and advises a smaller CFL number. The background of the documentation still says that Ulula always assumes an ideal gas (`docs/source/simulation.rst`), although version 0.4.0 added the isothermal mode.

A pressure floor can be set as well. The docstring describes it as a minimum that is enforced, which can help where numerical errors make pressures negative, but which breaks the conservation of energy, as it may add thermal energy. `Simulation.checkPressure` runs wherever pressures follow from the conserved variables or are evolved otherwise. Without a floor, it stops with an error at any zero or negative pressure, and advises a smaller CFL number or a floor. With a floor, it raises every pressure below the floor to it, but only when some pressure in the array is zero or negative, so that pressures between zero and the floor otherwise remain.

#### 2.1.3 Primitive and conserved variables

`Simulation.primitiveToConserved` sets the mass density and the momenta $\rho v_x$ and $\rho v_y$, and for an ideal gas the total energy $E = \rho(v_x^2 + v_y^2)/2 + P/(\gamma - 1)$, to which it adds $\rho\Phi$ when gravity is on. `Simulation.conservedToPrimitive` divides the momenta by the density and subtracts the kinetic energy and, with gravity, the potential energy from $E$. It multiplies the remaining internal energy per unit volume by $\gamma - 1$ to obtain the pressure, which it then checks (2.1.2). The potential is kept only among the primitive variables, so that the primitive array must hold it before the conversion. Both functions take the arrays of input and output as arguments, so that they also convert states other than the main ones, such as those at the cell edges, and `primitiveToConservedRet` returns the conserved variables in a new array.

#### 2.1.4 The flux vector

`Simulation.fluxVector` computes the flux of mass, momentum and total energy from a primitive state, for one direction at a time, since the scheme sweeps the directions in turn (2.3). With $v_1$ the velocity along the sweep and $v_2$ the one across it, the flux has the components $\rho v_1$, $\rho v_1^2 + P$ for the momentum along the sweep, $\rho v_1 v_2$ for the momentum across it, and $(E + P)v_1$, where $E$ is computed from the primitive state with $\rho\Phi$ included. The momenta keep their places in the array, so that one function serves both sweeps. For an isothermal gas the pressure is $\rho\varepsilon(\gamma - 1)$, and there is no flux of energy, as the energy is no variable.

### 2.2 The grid and boundary conditions

#### 2.2.1 The domain and its cells

`Simulation.setDomain` sets up a domain of $n_x$ by $n_y$ square cells (`ulula/core/simulation.py`). The cell width is $\Delta x = (x_\mathrm{max} - x_\mathrm{min})/n_x$ in both directions, so that the upper edge in $y$ follows as $y_\mathrm{min} + n_y\Delta x$, and the defaults are $x_\mathrm{min} = 0$, $x_\mathrm{max} = 1$ and $y_\mathrm{min} = 0$. The numbers of cells must be integers, with $n_x \geq 2$ and $n_y \geq 1$, and $n_y = 1$ makes the simulation one-dimensional. Before it builds anything, the function rejects two combinations: the HLLC solver with an isothermal gas, and self-gravity from the Poisson equation with boundaries that are not periodic (2.7). Each edge carries $n_\mathrm{g}$ ghost cells, one for constant and two for linear reconstruction (3.2). In $x$, the physical cells have the indices $n_\mathrm{g}$ to $n_\mathrm{g} + n_x - 1$, and the centres of all cells, ghost cells included, lie at $x_\mathrm{min} + (i - n_\mathrm{g} + 1/2)\Delta x$ for the index $i$. The same holds in $y$ for a two-dimensional domain. A one-dimensional domain has a single row in $y$, without ghost cells, at the height $y_\mathrm{min} + \Delta x/2$. Its first index in $y$ is 0, but the code sets the last one to 1, which works because the slices of the physical domain, which run to one past the last index, are cut at the edge of the array. The time and the number of steps start at zero, and the direction of the last sweep at $-1$, before a flag records that the domain is set. `Simulation.xyGrid` returns the coordinates of all cell centres, ghost cells included, as two two-dimensional arrays.

#### 2.2.2 Boundary conditions

Ulula offers three kinds of boundary, chosen for the whole domain: periodic, outflow and wall (§3.6; `Simulation.setDomain`). `Simulation.enforceBoundaryConditions` fills the ghost cells from the physical domain, at every time step (2.3). With periodic boundaries the domain wraps around. The ghost cells at each edge take the values of the physical cells at the opposite edge, and in two dimensions the corners take those of the diagonally opposite corner. With outflow boundaries, the ghost cells copy the adjacent physical cell and the corners the corner cell, so that flows continue across the edge. With walls, the ghost cells mirror the adjacent physical cells, the corners in both directions, and the velocity normal to the wall changes sign: $v_x$ in the ghost cells on the left and right, and $v_y$ in those at the bottom and top. The mirrored counter-flow balances any motion onto the wall, so that no mass or energy crosses it.

The docstring of `setDomain` states what each kind conserves. Periodic boundaries conserve mass, momentum and energy, walls mass and energy but not momentum, and outflow boundaries none of them. Outflow boundaries can also mislead where the flow at an edge points into the domain, which then keeps gaining mass unless pressures from within stop the flow. By default, `enforceBoundaryConditions` treats the hydrodynamic variables of both arrays, `V` and `U`. Called with a list of arrays and a slice of variables, it fills only those, as the solver of gravity does for the potential and its gradients (2.7).

#### 2.2.3 Updates and boundaries of the user

Two functions of the user, each called with the simulation object, extend the boundaries (§3.6). An update function, set with `Simulation.setUserUpdateFunction`, runs before the boundary conditions are filled. It lets a setup act on the simulation at run time, while the boundaries still follow automatically. A boundary function, set with `Simulation.setUserBoundaryConditions`, runs after them, for boundaries of its own, such as a flow that enters the domain through one side. Neither runs when only given arrays are filled. The code does not check what these functions do, and they must leave the primitive and conserved variables consistent with each other. The base class of the problem setups provides both, and each becomes active when a setup overrides it.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture draws on steps 1 to 3 and 8 to 10 of `plan.md`.

### 3.1 Packaging and installation

Ulula is the Python package ulula, built with setuptools 61.0 or later from `pyproject.toml`, which takes the version from `ulula/utils/version.py`. The package holds the subpackages `core`, `examples`, `physics`, `setups` and `utils`, is marked as unsafe to run from a zip archive, and needs Python 3.6 or later. It depends on NumPy ([Harris et al.](#harris-et-al-2020) 2020), SciPy ([Virtanen et al.](#virtanen-et-al-2020) 2020), Matplotlib ([Hunter](#hunter-2007) 2007) and h5py (`pyproject.toml`; acknowledgements of the [Ulula paper](#diemer-2025)). `README.rst` serves as the description of the package, and `README.md` gives the same text in Markdown. Ulula installs from PyPI with `pip install ulula` (`docs/source/installation.rst`). For development, a fork of the repository on BitBucket is cloned and installed with `pip install -e .`, so that changes to the source take effect at once. Updates from the main repository arrive by synchronising the fork on BitBucket and pulling. An older way, adding the folder to `PYTHONPATH`, is still described but no longer recommended, as it is less robust with virtual environments. **[GK]** The wheel of 1.0.2 on PyPI is pure Python, for any version of Python 3.

### 3.2 The simulation object and its data

The solver of Ulula is one class, `Simulation`, and a second class, `HydroScheme`, holds the choice of algorithm (`docs/source/simulation.rst`). A simulation proceeds in a fixed order. A `Simulation` is created, and its hydro scheme, equation of state, code units and gravity may be set, as well as functions of the user that change the domain or the boundaries at run time. `Simulation.setDomain` then sets the domain in cells and in coordinates and creates the arrays of variables. The initial conditions are written in primitive variables into the array `V`, as each problem setup does, and a setup with gravity calls `Simulation.solveGravity` with `initial_setup = True`. `Simulation.timestep` then advances the state until the simulation ends, with plots as desired. The runtime function `run` carries out this workflow, which a user may also write out and modify. The setters of the scheme, the equation of state, the code units and the gravity raise an error once the domain is set, as `setDomain` builds the arrays from these settings, while the functions of the user may also be set later. The constructor applies the defaults by calling every setter without arguments (`ulula/core/simulation.py`, `Simulation`).

`HydroScheme` holds the four identifiers of the components that part 2 introduces, and four parameters of the time step (2.3). They are the CFL number `cfl`, 0.8 by default, the largest CFL number tolerated in the second sweep, `cfl_max`, 0.95, the factor `cfl_reduce_factor`, 1.2, by which the time step shrinks when that limit is exceeded, and the number of attempts, `cfl_max_attempts`, 3. Its constructor rejects a CFL number or a maximum of 1 or more, a maximum that does not exceed the CFL number, and a factor that does not exceed 1. `Simulation.setHydroScheme` turns the identifiers into function pointers for the reconstruction, the limiter and the Riemann solver. It sets one ghost cell for constant and two for linear reconstruction, since the slopes reach a second neighbour, and rejects unknown identifiers, those of the time integration included.

The docstring of `Simulation` lists its fields in seven groups. The domain is described by whether it has two dimensions, by the cell width, the same in $x$ and $y$, by the numbers of cells in each direction and of ghost cells at each edge, by the first and last indices of the physical cells and the coordinates of the cell centres, by the type of boundary, and by a flag that the domain is set. The fluid variables comprise whether pressure is tracked, the numbers of hydrodynamic and of all variables, dictionaries of the indices of the primitive and of the conserved variables, the arrays `U` and `V`, and the states at the left and right cell edges. The scheme and the time step comprise the `HydroScheme`, whether source terms are active, the time, the number of steps and the direction of the last sweep. Further groups hold the equation of state, with its mode, $\gamma$, $\gamma - 1$ and its inverse, $\mu$, the fixed internal energy and the pressure floor, then the code units in centimetres, seconds and grams, the gravity, with its mode, the direction of a fixed acceleration, $g$ and $G$, and the functions of the user for updates and boundaries. The arrays of variables have one index for the variable and one for each direction, over physical cells and ghost cells alike.

The variables carry abbreviations of two letters, which give their positions in the arrays, select them in plots and name them in initial conditions (`docs/source/simulation.rst`). The primitive variables are `DN`, `VX`, `VY` and `PR`, for density, the two velocities and pressure, and the conserved ones `MS`, `MX`, `MY` and `ET`, for the densities of mass, momentum and total energy. With gravity, the primitive array also holds the potential `GP`, its gradients `GX` and `GY`, and a fixed potential of the user, `GU`, which have no conserved counterparts. In terms of mass $m$, length $l$ and time $t$, densities have the unit $m/l^3$, velocities $l/t$, momentum densities $m/(l^2 t)$, pressure and energy density $m/(l t^2)$, potentials $l^2/t^2$ and their gradients $l/t^2$. As the equations keep their form under changes of these units, the code units can take any values. They leave the outcome unchanged and appear only in plots, unless these are drawn in code units.

`Simulation.setDomain` also lays out the data (2.2.1). It numbers the variables: density and mass density share the index 0, the velocities and momenta 1 and 2, and pressure and total energy 3 for an ideal gas, while an isothermal gas has no fourth variable. The indices become attributes, such as `DN` and `MX`, and enter the dictionaries of the primitive and of the conserved variables. With gravity, the primitive variables gain the potential, then its two gradients unless the acceleration is fixed, and finally the fixed potential of the user in the mode that adds it to self-gravity (2.7). Three counts follow. The hydrodynamic variables are those with an equation of their own, whose fluxes are computed. The state variables add the potential, which the conversions and fluxes need although it has no equation, and all variables add its gradients and the potential of the user. The array `V` holds all variables and `U` the hydrodynamic ones. Each has a copy of the same shape that keeps the state at the start of a time step, so that a failed step can be repeated (2.3). For linear reconstruction, the states at the left and right cell edges have arrays of their own over the state variables. For constant reconstruction they are the array `V` itself, since the states at the edges then equal those at the centres. Slices prepared once select the variables, whether hydrodynamic, state or all, the physical cells in each direction and in the whole domain, the cells shifted by one or two places, and the cells to the left and to the right of every interface of the domain, which the solver reuses.
