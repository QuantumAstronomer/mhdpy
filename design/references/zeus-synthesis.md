# Synthesis of ZEUS-2D

This synthesis digests the three papers on ZEUS-2D, a code for radiation magnetohydrodynamics in two space dimensions. They are kept in `References/Zeus/Docs` and present, in turn, its hydrodynamic, magnetohydrodynamic and radiation hydrodynamic algorithms and tests: [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a, [doi:10.1086/191680](https://doi.org/10.1086/191680)), [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b, [doi:10.1086/191681](https://doi.org/10.1086/191681)) and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) (1992, [doi:10.1086/191682](https://doi.org/10.1086/191682)). The code itself is not among the sources. Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables, as in [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a, §2, eq. 3). Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

The three papers appeared together in volume 80 of the Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series, in June 1992, on pages 753–790, 791–818 and 819–845. Stone and Norman wrote the first two, and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) the third. The [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) gives James M. Stone and Michael L. Norman as members of the National Center for Supercomputing Applications and of the Department of Astronomy, both at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. It was received on 9 January 1991 and accepted on 29 October 1991. Its copyright is held by the American Astronomical Society, with all rights reserved, and the local copies are scans from the NASA Astrophysics Data System (ADS). The computations of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) ran on the Cray X-MP of the NCSA, and its authors acknowledge discussions with David Clarke, Chuck Evans, John Hawley, Dimitri Mihalas and Louis Wicker ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, acknowledgements). The [second paper](#stone-norman-1992b) gives the same affiliations and the same dates of receipt and acceptance. Its computations, too, ran on the Cray X-MP of the NCSA. Its authors thank the same colleagues, except Louis Wicker, for discussions, and Stone thanks Mihalas, the NCSA and the Department of Astronomy for financial support ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, acknowledgements).

ZEUS-2D descends from ZEUS, a code for astrophysical fluid dynamics that began as Norman's doctoral project, on how rotating interstellar clouds collapse and evolve as stars form ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §3; [Norman](#norman-1980) 1980; [Norman, Wilson and Barton](#norman-et-al-1980) 1980). Its algorithms were later improved and extended ([Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) 1986), and it served widely in models of jets in radio galaxies ([Norman et al.](#norman-et-al-1981) 1981, [1982](#norman-et-al-1982)). Last, David Clarke added an algorithm for MHD, for jets in radio galaxies held together by magnetic fields ([Clarke](#clarke-1988) 1988; [Clarke, Norman and Burns](#clarke-et-al-1986) 1986, [1989](#clarke-et-al-1989)). ZEUS-2D keeps ZEUS as its foundation but is a new code. It differences the equations in a covariant formalism that admits any orthogonal coordinate system, has a simpler and more accurate MHD algorithm than the later versions of ZEUS, and improves the management, control and portability of the code.

**[GK]** Stone's doctoral thesis at the University of Illinois, supervised by Norman and Mihalas, describes in detail how the code was developed and tested, and applies it to mass outflows from star-forming regions ([Stone](#stone-1990) 1990). The ADS holds the thesis under two records, [1990PhDT.......418S](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990PhDT.......418S/abstract), which names the supervisors, and [1990PhDT........16S](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990PhDT........16S/abstract), which gives the abstract.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

Hydrodynamic flows occur throughout astrophysics, from compact objects and stars up to interstellar and intergalactic gas ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §1). Their equations can rarely be solved analytically without restrictive assumptions, so that models rely on numerical solutions. Of the many numerical methods, finite differences are among the most developed and straightforward: robust, easy to code, efficient, and easy to modify and extend with new algorithms. A frontier of modelling is to include every physical effect that may matter for the dynamics, such as the Galactic magnetic field, which can dominate how the interstellar medium evolves, or the strong magnetic and radiation fields of the central object in accretion flows. Realistic models then need methods that treat all these effects self-consistently.

ZEUS-2D is a general-purpose code for astrophysical fluid dynamics in two space dimensions, with magnetic fields and radiation transfer ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §1). Generality drove its development, so that it can model a wide variety of systems. Its hydrodynamic algorithms were chosen as the most accurate ones that still let more complex physics be added easily and self-consistently. The MHD assumes flux freezing but is otherwise general. It is designed to treat all families of MHD waves properly, and to keep the evolved field free of divergence at all times. The radiation transfer uses a full transport algorithm. Closing the equations of radiation hydrodynamics with a solution of the transfer equation avoids the diffusion approximation, so that the method applies to optically thin and thick media alike, without flux limiters. Self-gravity enters through the Poisson equation, whose discretisation yields a sparse banded matrix that is solved iteratively (abstract).

ZEUS is an explicit Eulerian finite-difference code in two dimensions, built for simplicity, robustness and speed ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §3). Simple algorithms make for simple coding. Improvements can then be built in and tested quickly, new physics is easy to add, and a problem can be solved with different algorithms to tell the features of a solution that the numerics cause from those that are physical. More complex algorithms, such as higher-order Godunov methods like the piecewise parabolic method (PPM) of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984), give better results for purely hydrodynamic problems, but often need years of development before new physics can be added to them. The speed of the ZEUS algorithms allows large problems at high resolution, which is among the most important considerations in finding the converged solution of a physical problem. The summary of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) sharpens the argument: Godunov codes such as PPM do better on strong shocks, but new physics such as radiation MHD is very hard to add to them self-consistently, which rules them out for ZEUS-2D (§6).

In many astrophysical systems, magnetic fields can be crucial to the dynamics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §1). MHD processes matter in the solar corona, in the interstellar medium with its star-forming regions, in the outflows and jets of protostars, in accretion onto magnetised white dwarfs and neutron stars, and in extragalactic radio sources, and models of these systems must treat MHD effects self-consistently. The algorithms for the magnetic field stand on their own, independent of the hydrodynamic ones. They could be built into almost any explicit Eulerian finite-difference code, and, with some changes, into Lagrangian or implicit codes. The paper describes them in enough detail for others to implement or modify them, and reports an extensive series of tests for comparison with other schemes.

Of the physical processes that the equations of hydrodynamics leave out, the papers add magnetic fields and radiation transport, but not others, such as heat conduction ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §2). They present no applications to particular astrophysical problems, and refer to [Stone](#stone-1990) (1990) for them (§1), as the [second paper](#stone-norman-1992b) repeats ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §1). Its MHD leaves out resistivity and ambipolar diffusion, although adding them would be straightforward (§2).

### 1.3 The three papers

The algorithms of ZEUS-2D fall into three areas, and each paper treats one ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, abstract, §1). [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a) describe the hydrodynamics, which forms the foundation for the rest, [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b) the MHD, and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) (1992) the radiation hydrodynamics. The [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) calls the other two [Paper II](#stone-norman-1992b) and [Paper III](#stone-et-al-1992), and the [second paper](#stone-norman-1992b) calls the others [Paper I](#stone-norman-1992a) and [Paper III](#stone-et-al-1992). The division follows the subject matter, and lets readers who are interested only in MHD or radiation skip the rest. Each paper is meant to be detailed enough for others to implement the methods, and to serve as a basis for comparing the results of other methods.

The first paper, [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a), runs to 38 pages, with 148 numbered equations, 18 figures, 3 tables and 39 references. It writes down the equations of hydrodynamics that the code solves (§2), traces the roots of ZEUS-2D and the reasons for its choice of algorithms (§3), describes the code in detail over most of its length (§4), presents its tests (§5) and summarises (§6). Three appendices give the vector and tensor operators in covariant form, the tensor artificial viscosity, and some difference formulae.

The second paper, [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b), runs to 28 pages, with 55 numbered equations, 19 figures, 3 tables and 25 references. It writes down the equations of MHD (§2), describes the algorithms for the magnetic field and how they fit into the rest of ZEUS-2D (§3), presents the tests (§4) and summarises (§5). Its abstract states the main result. Constrained transport (CT) keeps the evolved field free of divergence at all times, but only a careful choice of how the electromotive forces are computed lets it propagate every family of MHD waves accurately, shear Alfvén waves in particular. A new way of computing these forces, by the method of characteristics (MOC), achieves this, and an extensive series of tests shows that the hybrid MOC-CT method evolves all wave families accurately.

### 2.1 The equations and the split solution procedure

#### 2.1.1 The equations of hydrodynamics

Astrophysical plasmas are dilute mixtures of electrons, ions, atoms and molecules ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §2). Their microscopic state is given at each instant by the distribution function in phase space, which evolves by the Boltzmann equation. When particles collide so often that their mean free path is much shorter than the length scales of the system, a statistical description by conserved macroscopic quantities suffices. These evolve by the velocity moments of the Boltzmann equation, and successively higher moments yield the equations of hydrodynamics. The paper does not derive them rigorously, but notes that collisions are frequent enough for this continuum description in many astrophysical problems of gas dynamics.

The equations are written in Lagrangian form, with the comoving derivative $D/Dt \equiv \partial/\partial t + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 4). The variables evolved are the mass density $\rho$, the velocity $\mathbf{v}$ and the internal energy per unit volume, $e$. They obey the continuity equation $D\rho/Dt + \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ (eq. 1), the equation of motion $\rho\, D\mathbf{v}/Dt = -\nabla p - \rho \nabla \Phi$ (eq. 2) and the internal energy equation $\rho\, D(e/\rho)/Dt = -p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$ (eq. 3). Two relations close the system: an equation of state for the gas pressure $p$ as a function of $\rho$ and $e$, or equivalently of $\rho$ and the temperature, and the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G \rho$ for the gravitational potential $\Phi$ (eq. 5). The equations carry neither viscous stresses nor a heat flux. The continuity equation states the conservation of mass, and the equation of motion that of momentum.

For the energy, the internal energy equation is solved rather than an equation that conserves the total energy, so that the internal energy, and with it the temperature, is more accurate in highly supersonic flows ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §2). **[GK]** At high Mach numbers the kinetic energy dominates. An internal energy found by subtracting it from the total energy then suffers from the truncation errors of the kinetic energy relative to the total, and can even turn negative (2.5 of `computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md`). Evolving $e$ itself avoids the subtraction. The price is that eq. 3, with its term $-p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$, is not in conservation form, so that conservation of the total energy to round-off is no longer built into the scheme.

#### 2.1.2 Operator splitting

ZEUS-2D integrates the equations explicitly in time, by finite differences and operator splitting ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4). The evolution of the state $y$, written as $\partial y/\partial t = \mathcal{L}(y)$ (eq. 6), is broken into parts by splitting the operator into terms, $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_1 + \mathcal{L}_2 + \dots$, which are applied in turn, each to the result of the one before: $(y^1 - y^0)/\Delta t = L_1(y^0)$, then $(y^2 - y^1)/\Delta t = L_2(y^1)$, and so on, where $L_i$ is a difference approximation to $\mathcal{L}_i$ (eqs. 7 to 9). The split solution only approximates that of the full nonlinear, multidimensional operator, and the order of the parts is largely free. Numerical experiments have shown, however, that such a sequence of partial updates is more accurate than a single update from the old state, and that the order matters little ([Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984b) 1984b; [Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) 1986).

The parts form two steps. The source step solves difference approximations to $\rho\, \partial\mathbf{v}/\partial t = -\nabla p - \rho \nabla \Phi - \nabla \cdot \mathbf{Q}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 10) and $\partial e/\partial t = -p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{Q} : \nabla \mathbf{v}$ (eq. 11), where $\mathbf{Q}$ is the tensor of an artificial viscosity. The transport step accounts for advection. It solves difference approximations to integral equations for a zone of volume $V$ and surface $\partial V$: $d/dt \int_V \rho\, dV = -\int_{\partial V} \rho(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_g) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ for the mass (eq. 12), and the same with $\rho\mathbf{v}$ for the momentum and with $e$ for the internal energy (eqs. 13 and 14). Here $\mathbf{v}_g$ is the velocity of the grid, which may move. The integral equations follow from the continuity equation and the divergence theorem. **[GK]** With eq. 1, eqs. 2 and 3 take the forms $\partial(\rho\mathbf{v})/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}) = -\nabla p - \rho\nabla\Phi$, in Cartesian coordinates, and $\partial e/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (e\mathbf{v}) = -p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$. Their flux terms make up the transport step, and their remaining terms, with the viscous ones, the source step. For a zone whose surface moves with $\mathbf{v}_g$, the rate of change of $\int_V q\, dV$ gains the flux $q\mathbf{v}_g \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ through the moving surface, by Reynolds' transport theorem, so that only the flux relative to the grid remains.

The source step thus contains terms that the Euler equations lack: the stresses and the heating of an artificial viscosity ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4). Like the viscosity of a real fluid, it smooths discontinuities, where the difference equations would otherwise fail. A real shock is a few mean free paths wide, but the artificial viscosity is made strong enough to spread a shock over several zones. A nonlinear form, large in shocks and negligible elsewhere, keeps its effect on smooth flow small. With it, the difference equations give the correct Rankine–Hugoniot jump conditions and the correct shock speed ([von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) 1950). Since a shock is then several zones wide, its internal structure cannot be studied, unless an adaptive mesh resolves it with very small zones.

Solving the integral forms allows conservative differencing, which keeps the total amount of each advected quantity on the grid to round-off ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4). The fluxes are measured relative to a grid that moves with $\mathbf{v}_g$ against a fixed background, so that the grid can follow global features of a flow, such as collapse or expansion, and resolve them better. In curvilinear coordinates, the scalar divergence theorem misses the terms that the gradient operator produces on the unit vectors of the velocity components. These terms act as inertial forces, and ZEUS-2D adds them in the source step.

### 2.2 The staggered mesh and the covariant formalism

#### 2.2.1 The staggered mesh

All dependent variables are functions of two spatial coordinates, $x_1$ and $x_2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). In Cartesian coordinates this means slab symmetry, and in cylindrical or spherical coordinates axial symmetry. The restriction saves memory and computing time. The methods extend readily to three dimensions, and ZEUS-3D already does so ([Clarke, Stone and Norman](#clarke-et-al-1990) 1990). Vectors and tensors keep all their components, each a function of the two coordinates, so that the velocity has a third component $v_3$ as well. This 2.5-dimensional dynamics is essential for self-consistent rotating flows and for the full set of MHD wave modes.

The domain is divided into zones, numbered by $i$ from $ii$ to $io$ in $x_1$, for inner and outer, and by $j$ from $ji$ to $jo$ in $x_2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, fig. 1). Two rows of ghost zones lie beyond each boundary. Their values come from the boundary conditions of §4.5, not from the evolution equations. Two rows are needed to give derivatives at a boundary, as inflow conditions and the higher-order interpolation of the transport step require.

Positions are given by coordinate arrays ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1, fig. 2). The a-mesh holds the zone boundaries, $x1a_i$ being the left boundary of zone $i$ and $x2a_j$ the lower boundary of zone $j$, and the b-mesh holds the zone centres $(x1b_i, x2b_j)$. The spacings $dx1a_i = x1a_{i+1} - x1a_i$ and $dx1b_i = x1b_i - x1b_{i-1}$, and likewise $dx2a_j$ and $dx2b_j$, can be chosen freely, which allows nonuniform grids. As the grid must be orthogonal, a spacing chosen in $x_1$ holds across the whole range of $x_2$, and vice versa.

The mesh is staggered ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). Scalars, and the diagonal components of even-rank tensors, sit at the zone centres. Vector components sit on the faces normal to them, and the off-diagonal components of even-rank tensors at the corners. Of the hydrodynamic variables, the density $d_{i,j}$, the internal energy density $e_{i,j}$, the rotational velocity $v3_{i,j}$ and the potential $\Phi_{i,j}$ lie at the zone centre $(x1b_i, x2b_j)$, while $v1_{i,j}$ lies at $(x1a_i, x2b_j)$ and $v2_{i,j}$ at $(x1b_i, x2a_j)$ (fig. 3). Scalars and vectors share indices instead of using half indices such as $v1_{i+1/2,j}$. The face with the same index as a zone centre always lies at the smaller coordinate, as $x1a_i < x1b_i$ and $x2a_j < x2b_j$. With the usual multidimensional form of the artificial viscosity (§4.3), the hydrodynamics needs no tensors of rank two. They appear in radiation hydrodynamics and in the tensor artificial viscosity of appendix B.

The staggering has two advantages ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). Most spatial differences are centred, as a vector formed from differences of scalars lies midway between them, and centred differences are formally of second order, against first order for one-sided differences. The transport step also needs fewer interpolations, since velocities on the faces give the flux into or out of a zone directly. On the other hand, other parts of the calculation need extra averages, such as forming the momentum from density and velocity, which can limit the overall accuracy and the rate of convergence, the rate at which the error falls as the number of grid points grows ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b). Some boundary conditions also become harder to apply. The authors judge the advantages to outweigh these drawbacks.

#### 2.2.2 Domains and grid motion

A domain need not be globally rectangular ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). For an accretion disc around a central object in cylindrical coordinates, the origin $r = z = 0$ is better left out, to avoid numerical trouble near the surface of the central object, and a staircase in the boundary near the origin achieves this. ZEUS-2D allows it by making the first and last index in each direction an array over the other direction: $ii(j)$, $io(j)$, $ji(i)$ and $jo(i)$.

The mesh can also move against a stationary background, with grid velocities on the zone faces, where the fluid velocities lie ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). Once a time step is complete, the zone boundaries of the a-mesh and the centres of the b-mesh are moved by the grid velocities. They can be prescribed freely, and independently in the two directions. Setting them equal to the fluid velocity along a strip of zones makes the code semi-Lagrangian, and in a collapse problem the grid can follow an approximation to the collapse. Several such choices are built in. The motion must keep the grid orthogonal, so the grid velocity in the 1-direction may vary only with $x_1$, and that in the 2-direction only with $x_2$. This is less general than truly two-dimensional adaptive meshes, such as those of [Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) (1984) and [Fiedler](#fiedler-1990) (1990, private communication), and limits how well a moving grid can follow two-dimensional flows. The authors see a more sophisticated algorithm as a very profitable addition.

#### 2.2.3 The covariant formalism

Equations written and differenced in one chosen coordinate system tie the code to that system, and moving it to another is usually lengthy ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.2). ZEUS-2D instead differences the equations in a covariant form. Every vector and tensor operator is written without reference to particular coordinates, through the scale factors of the metric, which is diagonal for an orthogonal coordinate system (appendix A), and these covariant equations are differenced with the scale factors in place. The difference equations then hold in any orthogonal coordinate system, and only the scale factors depend on it. For a mesh that does not move, they are computed once, at the start.

Three coordinate systems are used, in each of which $x_3$ is the ignorable coordinate: Cartesian, with $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (x, y, z)$ and scale factors $(h_1, h_2, h_3) = (1, 1, 1)$; cylindrical, with $(z, r, \phi)$ and $(1, 1, r)$; and spherical polar, with $(r, \theta, \phi)$ and $(1, r, r\sin\theta)$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 15 to 17). In all three, $h_1 = 1$, $h_2$ is a function of $x_1$, and $h_3$ is a function of $x_1$ times a function of $x_2$. The code stores them in that form, as $h_1 = g_1 = 1$, $h_2 = g_2(x_1)$ and $h_3 = g_{31}(x_1)\,g_{32}(x_2)$, so that $g_1$ can be dropped (eqs. 18 to 20). Each of $g_2$, $g_{31}$ and $g_{32}$ has a value on the a-mesh and one on the b-mesh (fig. 4), and so do their derivatives. The derivatives $\partial g_2/\partial x_1$, $\partial g_{31}/\partial x_1$ and $\partial g_{32}/\partial x_2$ vanish in Cartesian coordinates, are 0, 0 and 1 in cylindrical ones, and 1, 1 and $\cos\theta$ in spherical polar ones (eqs. 21 to 23). The factored form saves memory and arithmetic, but rules out coordinate systems whose scale factors do not fit it, such as oblate spheroidal coordinates, where $h_1 \neq 1$, although they would suit rotationally flattened stars. Extending the implementation to them would be straightforward.

Curvilinear coordinates bring the risk of differencing errors near coordinate singularities ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.2). In spherical polar coordinates, the simple radial difference $r^{-2}\,\partial(r^2F)/\partial r \approx (r_i^2F_i - r_{i-1}^2F_{i-1})/\{[(r_i + r_{i-1})/2]^2(r_i - r_{i-1})\}$ (eq. 24) does not reproduce, near the origin, the difference over the volume, $(r_i^2F_i - r_{i-1}^2F_{i-1})/(r_i^3/3 - r_{i-1}^3/3)$ (eq. 25). The two agree at large radii, but differ badly as $r_{i-1} \to 0$, and differences in the polar angle suffer likewise. Using the volume difference is an instance of a general way of reducing difference errors near coordinate singularities, which [Evans](#evans-1986) (1986) named coordinate regularisation, and [Mönchmeyer and Müller](#monchmeyer-muller-1989) (1989) give a general method for differencing conservation laws on non-Cartesian meshes. ZEUS-2D does not regularise in full generality. It uses volume differences wherever they are needed, through the volume factors $dvl1$ and $dvl2$: $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ in Cartesian coordinates, $\Delta z$ and $\Delta(r^2/2)$ in cylindrical ones, and $\Delta(r^3/3)$ and $\Delta(-\cos\theta)$ in spherical polar ones (eqs. 26 to 28). Like the scale factors, they are defined on both meshes. Terms of the form $(h_2h_3)^{-1}\,\partial F/\partial x_1$ and $h_3^{-1}\,\partial F/\partial x_2$ are then differenced as $\Delta F/(g_{32}\,dvl1)$ and $\Delta F/(g_{31}\,dvl2)$ (eq. 29).

**[GK]** The denominators of eqs. 24 and 25 differ by exactly $(r_i - r_{i-1})^3/12$. The two formulae thus agree to relative order $(\Delta r/r)^2$ at large radii, but in the innermost zone, where $r_{i-1} = 0$, they differ by a factor of 4/3. Eq. 25 is the exact average of the divergence over the shell between $r_{i-1}$ and $r_i$, by the divergence theorem. With $g_2 = g_{31} = r$ and $g_{32} = \sin\theta$ in spherical polar coordinates, and $g_{32} = r$ in cylindrical ones, $dvl1$ is in all three systems the difference of $\int g_2 g_{31}\,dx_1$, and $dvl2$ that of $\int g_{32}\,dx_2$. A zone thus has the volume $dvl1\,dvl2\,\Delta x_3$, and eq. 29 is the volume difference of the operator it replaces, since $(h_2h_3)^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_1 = g_{32}^{-1}(g_2g_{31})^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_1$, and likewise in $x_2$.

#### 2.2.4 Operators in orthogonal coordinates

Appendix A collects coordinate-free expressions for the operators the papers use, for an orthogonal metric $g_{ij} = \mathrm{diag}(h_1^2, h_2^2, h_3^2)$ with determinant $g = h_1^2h_2^2h_3^2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. A, eq. 109). They hold for any number of dimensions and any form of the scale factors, so that they also serve calculations in three dimensions and in other orthogonal coordinates. They follow from the covariant forms of the operators, with physical components related to covariant ones by $a_i = h_ia_{(i)}$ and $T_{ij} = h_ih_jT_{(i,j)}$, and to contravariant ones by $a^i = a_{(i)}/h_i$ and $T^{ij} = T_{(i,j)}/(h_ih_j)$ (eqs. 113 and 114). Texts on tensor calculus give them, as do [Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) (1984). In physical components, the gradient of a scalar is $(h_1^{-1}\,\partial f/\partial x_1,\ h_2^{-1}\,\partial f/\partial x_2,\ h_3^{-1}\,\partial f/\partial x_3)$ (eq. 115), the divergence of a vector is $(h_1h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_2h_3a_1)/\partial x_1 + \partial(h_1h_3a_2)/\partial x_2 + \partial(h_1h_2a_3)/\partial x_3]$ (eq. 116), and the Laplacian of a scalar is the divergence of its gradient (eq. 117). The curl has the component $(\nabla\times\mathbf{a})_1 = (h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_3a_3)/\partial x_2 - \partial(h_2a_2)/\partial x_3]$, and the others by cyclic permutation (eqs. 118 to 120).

A vector's gradient is a tensor of rank two, built with the Christoffel symbols of the second kind ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. A). For an orthogonal metric, only $\Gamma^i_{ii} = \tfrac12\,\partial(\ln g_{ii})/\partial x_i$, $\Gamma^i_{jj} = -\tfrac12 g_{ii}^{-1}\,\partial g_{jj}/\partial x_i$ and $\Gamma^i_{ij} = \Gamma^i_{ji} = \tfrac12\,\partial(\ln g_{ii})/\partial x_j$ are nonzero (eqs. 121 to 123). The components follow the pattern $(\nabla\mathbf{a})_{(11)} = h_1^{-1}\,\partial a_1/\partial x_1 + (h_1h_2)^{-1}a_2\,\partial h_1/\partial x_2 + (h_1h_3)^{-1}a_3\,\partial h_1/\partial x_3$ on the diagonal and $(\nabla\mathbf{a})_{(12)} = h_1^{-1}\,\partial a_2/\partial x_1 - (h_1h_2)^{-1}a_1\,\partial h_1/\partial x_2$ off it, with the others by permutation of the indices (eqs. 124 to 126). The first index thus gives the direction of differentiation, and the second the component. **[GK]** The general expression stated before them, $(h_ih_j)^{-1}a_{i;j}$, has the indices of $a_{i;j}$ the other way round. With $a_{j;i}$ it gives these components.

The Laplacian of a vector follows from $\nabla^2\mathbf{a} = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}) - \nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{a}$ and the expressions above ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 127 to 129). The divergence of a tensor of rank two, $T^{ij}_{;j} = g^{-1/2}\,\partial(g^{1/2}T^{ij})/\partial x_j + \Gamma^i_{jk}T^{kj}$, contracts its second index. Its first physical component is $(h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_2h_3T_{11}/h_1)/\partial x_1 + \partial(h_3T_{12})/\partial x_2 + \partial(h_2T_{13})/\partial x_3]$ plus terms in the derivatives of the scale factors: $-T_{22}(h_1h_2)^{-1}\,\partial h_2/\partial x_1 - T_{33}(h_1h_3)^{-1}\,\partial h_3/\partial x_1 + (T_{12} + T_{21})(h_1h_2)^{-1}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_2 + (T_{13} + T_{31})(h_1h_3)^{-1}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_3$, and one in $T_{11}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_1$. The other components follow by permutation (eqs. 130 to 132). For a symmetric tensor the mixed terms add in pairs. For an antisymmetric one they cancel, and its diagonal vanishes. **[GK]** In each component, the term in the derivative of the scale factor of its own direction carries the wrong sign. Eq. 130 has $-T_{11}h_1^{-2}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_1$, and eqs. 131 and 132 have the like terms in $T_{22}$ and $T_{33}$, where a plus is right. With only $T_{11}$ nonzero, in a stretched Cartesian coordinate, the paper's form gives $h_1^{-1}\,\partial T_{11}/\partial x_1 - 2T_{11}h_1^{-2}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_1$ instead of $h_1^{-1}\,\partial T_{11}/\partial x_1$. In the three coordinate systems of ZEUS-2D these derivatives vanish, since $h_1 = 1$, $h_2$ does not depend on $x_2$ and nothing depends on $x_3$, so that the code is not affected.

Appendix C writes out the zone-centred differences that the tensor artificial viscosity of appendix B needs ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. C). The divergence of the velocity is $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j} = (g2a_{i+1}\,g31a_{i+1}\,v1_{i+1,j} - g2a_i\,g31a_i\,v1_{i,j})/dvl1a_i + (g32a_{j+1}\,v2_{i,j+1} - g32a_j\,v2_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dvl2a_j)$ (eq. 145), a difference of the fluxes through the faces of the zone over its volume factors. The diagonal components of the velocity gradient are $(\nabla v_{(11)})_{i,j} = (v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})/dx1a_i$ (eq. 146), $(\nabla v_{(22)})_{i,j} = (v2_{i,j+1} - v2_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dx2a_j) + \bar v_1\,(\partial g2b_i/\partial x_1)/g2b_i$ (eq. 147) and $(\nabla v_{(33)})_{i,j} = \bar v_1\,(\partial g31b_i/\partial x_1)/g31b_i + \bar v_2\,(\partial g32b_j/\partial x_2)/(g2b_i\,g32b_j)$ (eq. 148). Here $\bar v_1 = (v1_{i,j} + v1_{i+1,j})/2$ and $\bar v_2 = (v2_{i,j} + v2_{i,j+1})/2$ average the velocities on opposite faces of the zone. **[GK]** These are eqs. 116 and 124 in two dimensions, differenced on the staggered mesh, with the divergence in the volume form of eq. 29.

### 2.3 The source step and artificial viscosity

#### 2.3.1 Substeps and forces

The source step solves difference forms of eqs. 10 and 11, the sources and sinks of the variables, split from the advection that the transport step adds afterwards ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3). It has three substeps of its own. The first updates the velocities by the pressure gradient, gravity and the curvature terms, the inertial forces of curvilinear coordinates. The second adds the stresses and heating of the artificial viscosity, from the velocities after the first. The third adds the compressional heating. The superscripts $n$, $n + a$, $n + b$ and $n + c$ mark the values when the source step begins and after each substep.

In the first substep, $v1$ changes at the rate $-(p_{i,j} - p_{i-1,j})/[dx1b_i\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i-1,j})/2] - (\Phi_{i,j} - \Phi_{i-1,j})/dx1b_i + \bar v_2^2\,(\partial g2a_i/\partial x_1)/g2a_i + \bar v_3^2\,(\partial g31a_i/\partial x_1)/g31a_i$, with all values at time level $n$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 30). Here $\bar v_2$ averages $v2$ over the four faces next to the face of $v1$, and $\bar v_3$ averages $v3$ over the two zones on either side of it. The second component changes at the rate $-(p_{i,j} - p_{i,j-1})/[g2b_i\,dx2b_j\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i,j-1})/2] - (\Phi_{i,j} - \Phi_{i,j-1})/(g2b_i\,dx2b_j) + \bar v_3^2\,(\partial g32a_j/\partial x_2)/(g2b_i\,g32a_j)$, with $\bar v_3$ now averaged in $x_2$ (eq. 31). The staggering thus calls for averages of the density, and of the velocity components that do not lie on the face being updated. The pressure comes from the equation of state when the source step begins, mostly the ideal gas law $p = (\gamma - 1)e$, and the potential from the Poisson equation with the density of that time (§4.7). The curvature terms appear only in curvilinear coordinates, as inertial forces that the geometry adds to the divergence of the momentum flux. **[GK]** In spherical polar coordinates, with $g_2 = g_{31} = r$ and $g_{32} = \sin\theta$, they are $(v_\theta^2 + v_\phi^2)/r$ in the radial and $v_\phi^2\cot\theta/r$ in the polar equation of motion, the centrifugal terms of these coordinates.

#### 2.3.2 Artificial viscosity

The second substep adds the artificial viscosity, whose differencing depends on its form ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3.2). The customary form is that of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) (1950), derived from planar shocks in one dimension. Their insight was that a nonlinear viscous pressure, acting only in compression, gives the correct jump in entropy and the correct shock speed while staying negligible away from shocks: $q = l^2\rho(\partial v/\partial x)^2$ where $\partial v/\partial x < 0$, and $q = 0$ elsewhere, with a constant length $l$ that sets its strength (eq. 32). In several dimensions, a separate scalar viscous pressure is defined for each direction, at the zone centres: $q1_{i,j} = C_2\,d_{i,j}(v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})^2$ where $v1_{i+1,j} < v1_{i,j}$, and zero otherwise, and $q2_{i,j}$ likewise from $v2$ (eqs. 33 and 34). The dimensionless $C_2 = l/\Delta x$ counts roughly the zones over which a shock is spread, and is typically about 3. The velocities then change at the rates $-(q1_{i,j} - q1_{i-1,j})/[dx1b_i\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i-1,j})/2]$ and $-(q2_{i,j} - q2_{i,j-1})/[g2b_i\,dx2b_j\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i,j-1})/2]$, and the internal energy at the rate $-q1_{i,j}(v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})/dx1a_i - q2_{i,j}(v2_{i,j+1} - v2_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dx2a_j)$ (eqs. 35 to 37). **[GK]** This heating is never negative, since each $q$ is nonzero only where its velocity difference is negative. With eq. 32 and $\partial v/\partial x \approx \Delta v/\Delta x$, eqs. 33 and 34 call for $C_2^2$ rather than $C_2$, given $C_2 = l/\Delta x$, and appendix B, which sets $l = C_2\,\Delta x$, keeps the square. Later copies of the code settle the question in favour of eqs. 33 and 34. Two survive on the web: release 2.0.3 of the Laboratory for Computational Astrophysics, of 2007, and Stone's own version 2.0, of 2003. In both, the quadratic viscous pressure is a constant `qcon` times $\rho(\Delta v)^2$, with `qcon` = 2 by default, so that `qcon` stands for $(l/\Delta x)^2$ and the definition $C_2 = l/\Delta x$ is the slip.

This extension to several dimensions is ad hoc ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3.2). A rigorous treatment defines an isotropic tensor of artificial viscous stress. The scalar form serves in Cartesian coordinates, but can cause serious trouble in curvilinear ones ([Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) 1979): radial inflow along converging grid lines compresses the gas even where $\partial v/\partial r = 0$, and should heat it, yet the scalar form gives $q = 0$. A tensor artificial viscosity avoids this, with all the good properties of the scalar one in covariant form ([Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) 1979; [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) 1986). ZEUS-2D offers both forms. The nonlinear viscosity only has to give the correct jump conditions and shock speed, and numerical experiments show the scalar form adequate in many problems, even in non-Cartesian geometries, so that the tensor form is kept for problems that demand it.

Some tests with strong shocks also need a linear artificial viscosity, to damp oscillations in stagnant regions of the flow. Its scalar viscous pressure is $q^{\rm lin} = C_1\rho C_a\Delta v$, with $C_1$ of order unity and the adiabatic sound speed $C_a$, $C_a^2 = \gamma p/\rho$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 38; [Lapidus](#lapidus-1967) 1967; [Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) 1986). It acts in expansion as well as in compression, separately in each direction, and enters through eqs. 35 to 37 with $q^{\rm lin}$ in place of $q$, directly after the nonlinear viscosity and before the third substep. **[GK]** The paper leaves the sign of $\Delta v$ open. In eq. 37 the linear viscosity heats only if $q^{\rm lin}$ has the sign opposite to $v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j}$, so that $\Delta v$ must be the difference taken the other way, positive in compression. Both later copies of the code take $q^{\rm lin} = -$`qlin`$\,\rho\,(v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})\sqrt{e/\rho}$, which confirms this reading, but use $\sqrt{e/\rho}$ for the sound speed, which for an ideal gas differs from $C_a$ by the factor $[\gamma(\gamma - 1)]^{1/2}$.

#### 2.3.3 Compressional heating

The third substep adds the compressional heating ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3.3). For an ideal gas, an implicit update with the time-centred pressure $p^{n+1/2} = (p^n + p^{n+1})/2$, $(e^{n+1} - e^n)/\Delta t = -p^{n+1/2}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, improves the conservation of energy (eq. 39). With $p = (\gamma - 1)e$ it can be solved for the new energy, $e^{n+c}_{i,j} = e^{n+b}_{i,j}\,[1 - (\Delta t/2)(\gamma - 1)(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}]/[1 + (\Delta t/2)(\gamma - 1)(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}]$, with the zone-centred divergence of appendix C (eq. 40). For other equations of state, a predictor with $p^n$, $e^{\rm pred} = e^{n+b} - \Delta t\,p^n(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}$ (eq. 41), is followed by a corrector with the average of $p^n$ and the pressure $p^{\rm pred}$ of $e^{\rm pred}$ (eq. 42). The partially updated variables then pass to the transport step.

#### 2.3.4 The tensor artificial viscosity

Appendix B builds the tensor artificial viscosity on the analogy of the viscous stress of a Newtonian fluid, $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mu[\nabla\mathbf{v} - \tfrac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{e}]$, with the dynamic viscosity $\mu$, the symmetrised velocity gradient $\nabla\mathbf{v}$ and the unit tensor $\mathbf{e}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. B, eq. 133; [Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) 1984, §25). The bulk viscosity is taken as zero, by Stokes's hypothesis, so that the tensor is traceless and vanishes for a fluid that expands equally in all directions. An artificial viscosity differs from a molecular one in the size of its coefficient, which must spread shocks over a few zones of the local mesh, act only in compression, and be large in shocks but negligible elsewhere. On the analogy of the nonlinear coefficient of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950), [Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) (1979) take $\mathbf{Q} = l^2\rho(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})[\nabla\mathbf{v} - \tfrac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{e}]$ where $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$, and zero elsewhere, with $l$ a few zone widths (eq. 134). Its main advantage over the scalar form is its dependence on the divergence, which is invariant, instead of the velocity gradient, a tensor that is a single number only for planar flow in one dimension.

[Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) (1979) give $\mathbf{Q}$ for one-dimensional spherical geometry, and its extension to several dimensions is direct, with two choices ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. B). The length $l$ must be a single number rather than one per direction, since the tensor would otherwise not be isotropic and would lose its zero trace. In practice it is set from the larger grid spacing, $l_{i,j} = \max(C_2\,dx1a_i, C_2\,dx2a_j)$. The off-diagonal, shear components are dropped, so that the viscosity smooths only compressive shock fronts and does not broaden large gradients of shear. The diagonal components are zone-centred, $Q11_{i,j} = l_{i,j}^2\,d_{i,j}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}[(\nabla v_{(11)})_{i,j} - \tfrac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}]$, and likewise $Q22$ and $Q33$, with the differences of appendix C (eqs. 135 to 137).

The viscous force and heating, $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q}$ and $-\mathbf{Q}:\nabla\mathbf{v}$, take the covariant forms $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q})_{(1)} = (g_2^2g_{31})^{-1}\,\partial(g_2^2g_{31}Q_{11})/\partial x_1$ and $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q})_{(2)} = (g_2g_{32}^2)^{-1}\,\partial(g_{32}^2Q_{22})/\partial x_2 + Q_{11}(g_2g_{32})^{-1}\,\partial g_{32}/\partial x_2$, in which the zero trace has eliminated $Q_{33}$, and $\mathbf{Q}:\nabla\mathbf{v} = l^2\rho(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\tfrac13[(\nabla v_{(11)} - \nabla v_{(22)})^2 + (\nabla v_{(11)} - \nabla v_{(33)})^2 + (\nabla v_{(33)} - \nabla v_{(22)})^2]$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 138 to 140). The paper states that they reduce to the formulation of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) in Cartesian geometry. [Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) stress that writing the heating as a sum of squares is essential, since in their experience every other form eventually turns unstable. The difference forms divide differences of the zone-centred components across a face by the scale factors on that face, average $Q11$ in the last term of the second component, and replace eqs. 35 to 37 when the tensor form is used (eqs. 141 to 143). The viscous time step of the tensor form, $\delta t = \Delta x^2/(4\nu)$ with $\nu = l^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, replaces the viscous limit of §4.6 (eq. 144).

**[GK]** The sum of squares makes the heating $-\mathbf{Q}:\nabla\mathbf{v}$ non-negative wherever $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$, the only place where $\mathbf{Q}$ acts. In one-dimensional planar flow, eq. 134 gives $Q_{11} = \tfrac23 l^2\rho(\partial v/\partial x)^2$, the pressure of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) with $l^2$ scaled by 2/3. The later copies of the code take $l^2$ as 1.5 `qcon` times the square of the larger zone width, the factor 1.5 making the tensor form equal to the scalar one in such flow. In several dimensions the two forms differ even in Cartesian coordinates, as $Q_{11}$ depends on the whole divergence. Four printed formulae need correcting, and the width that sets $l_{i,j}$ needs a scale factor. Eq. 138 has $g_3^2g_{31}$ in the denominator, where its numerator and eq. 141 show $g_2^2g_{31}$ to be meant. Eqs. 141 and 142 divide their first differences by the scale factors alone, without the spacings $dx1b_i$ and $dx2b_j$ between the zone centres that make them derivatives. Eq. 144 lacks an absolute value, as $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$ wherever the viscosity acts, although the sign does not matter inside eq. 74, which squares each limit (2.5.2). In spherical polar coordinates $dx2a_j$ is an angle, so that $l_{i,j}$ needs the length $g2b_i\,dx2a_j$ in the 2-direction, which the code uses. Eq. 138 also relies on $g_2$ and $g_{31}$ having the same logarithmic derivative, as in all three coordinate systems of ZEUS-2D. In general, the zero trace leaves $(g_2g_{31}^2)^{-1}\,\partial(g_2g_{31}^2Q_{11})/\partial x_1 + Q_{22}\,\partial\ln(g_{31}/g_2)/\partial x_1$, which follows from eq. 130 with the sign corrected in 2.2.4.

### 2.4 The transport step

#### 2.4.1 Conservative, directionally split advection

The transport step solves difference forms of the integral eqs. 12 to 14, by which the amount of a quantity $q$ in its control volume, the zone centred on $q$, changes by the flux of $q$ through the faces ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). The scheme, of second order in space and time, changes $q\tau$, with $\tau$ the control volume, by the differences of the time-centred fluxes $\langle(vk - vgk)\,q^*_k\,\tilde A_k\rangle$ through its faces (eqs. 43 and 44). Here $vk - vgk$ is the velocity relative to the grid, $q^*_k$ the face value of $q$ given by the interpolation, and $\tilde A_k$ the area of the face, all in direction $k$. For a zone-centred $q$ the velocities lie on the faces of the control volume, which is a main reason for the staggered mesh, while other quantities need velocities averaged to the faces. Each flux is computed once and serves both zones beside its face. The fluxes then cancel in pairs when the update is summed over the grid, so that the total amount of $q$ changes only by the fluxes through the boundaries of the domain, to round-off.

The advection is split into one-dimensional sweeps ([Strang](#strang-1968) 1968): one in the 1-direction, and then one in the 2-direction with the quantities from the first ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 45 and 46). Second order needs a symmetric sequence, such as half a sweep in $x_1$, a full one in $x_2$ and another half in $x_1$, at half as much computing time again as the plain sequence. Experiments by [Finn and Hawley](#finn-hawley-1989) (1989) showed the gain not worth the cost at moderate resolutions, up to about $10^5$ zones in two dimensions, where a higher resolution with the unsymmetric sequence is more accurate for the same time. ZEUS-2D therefore splits unsymmetrically, but alternates the order of the sweeps from one cycle to the next, which may improve the accuracy at no extra cost.

#### 2.4.2 Interpolation to the faces

The hard part is to find accurate and stable values of $q$ at the faces, averaged over the time step ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). [Godunov](#godunov-1959) (1959) first advected discontinuities stably on an Eulerian grid, by upwinding: the value at a face comes from the distribution of $q$ upstream of it. Later work raised the accuracy with polynomials of higher order within the upstream zones, and found that stability at higher order also needs monotonicity. A profile that rises or falls monotonically before advection must do so after it, so that no new extrema appear. [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984b) (1984b) and [Clarke](#clarke-1988) (1988) discuss how monotonic schemes of the kind ZEUS-2D implements suppress instabilities.

Three interpolations are implemented for the face value $q^*_i$ of a zone-centred $q_i$, each named by the order of the first term it drops from the Taylor series ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). The donor cell method, of first order, takes $q$ as constant within a zone, so that $q^*_i = q_{i-1}$ where $v_i - vg_i > 0$ and $q^*_i = q_i$ where it is negative (eq. 47). It is very diffusive and serves only in tests. The van Leer method, of second order ([van Leer](#van-leer-1977) 1977), takes $q$ as linear within a zone, with $q^*_i = q_{i-1} + [\Delta x_{i-1} - (v_i - vg_i)\Delta t]\,dq_{i-1}/2$ for flow in the positive direction and $q^*_i = q_i - [\Delta x_i + (v_i - vg_i)\Delta t]\,dq_i/2$ otherwise (eq. 48). Its slopes are monotonised as the harmonic mean, $dq_i = 2\Delta q_{i-1/2}\,\Delta q_{i+1/2}/(\Delta q_{i-1/2} + \Delta q_{i+1/2})$, of the differences $\Delta q_{i+1/2} = (q_{i+1} - q_i)/\Delta x_i$ where these have the same sign, and $dq_i = 0$ otherwise (eq. 49). The slopes allow nonuniform spacing, and where they vanish the method falls back to the donor cell. It combines lower diffusion with speed, and is the method most used in ZEUS-2D. **[GK]** Eq. 48 gives the average of the linear profile over the part of the upwind zone that crosses the face in a time step.

The piecewise parabolic advection (PPA), of third order, interpolates parabolae: $q^*_i = q_{R,i-1} + \xi(q_{i-1} - q_{R,i-1}) + \xi(1 - \xi)(2q_{i-1} - q_{R,i-1} - q_{L,i-1})$ for flow in the positive direction, and the mirror form in $q_{L,i}$ and $q_{R,i}$ otherwise ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 50). Here $\xi$ is the fraction of the upwind zone that the flow relative to the grid crosses in a time step, and $q_{L,i}$ and $q_{R,i}$ are the monotonised values at the left and right faces of zone $i$, from the algorithm of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) as [Clarke](#clarke-1988) (1988) implemented it in ZEUS. The paper refers to these sources for the details, such as the constraints for monotonicity and the steepening of contacts. A parabola needs two zones upwind of a face, so that PPA uses five zones centred on the one updated, and two ghost zones at each boundary. **[GK]** Eq. 50 is the average of the parabola of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) over the part of the upwind zone that crosses the face.

PPA keeps contact discontinuities to two zones, better than any other method, but at a high cost in computing time ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). For many dynamical problems in several dimensions, it is not clear that it does better than the van Leer method at a higher resolution. A third-order advection does not make the whole code converge at that order, as other parts of the procedure may dominate the error. [Finn and Hawley](#finn-hawley-1989) (1989) showed, moreover, that with a velocity that varies in space, the van Leer method and PPA must account for the velocity gradient across a zone to keep their orders of convergence. ZEUS-2D therefore mostly uses the van Leer method, although PPA is fully implemented. PPA is not PPM, the piecewise parabolic method, which pairs the same third-order interpolation with a Riemann solver.

#### 2.4.3 Fluxes on a moving grid and consistent transport

The mass shows how the fluxes are built ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). In the 1-direction, the flux through a face of zone $(i,j)$ is $d^*_{1,i,j}(v1_{i,j} - vg1_i)\,g2a_i^{n+1/2}\,g31a_i^{n+1/2}\,dvl2a_j^n$, with the interpolated density $d^*$ and with scale factors centred in time to follow the motion of the grid (eq. 51). Factors that depend on $x_2$ are not centred in time, since the grid stays fixed in $x_2$ during the sweep in $x_1$. The change of $d\,dvl1a\,dvl2a$ over the time step is then the difference of the fluxes through the two faces, with $dvl1a$ at the new time (eq. 52). The sweep in $x_2$ uses the flux $d^*_{2,i,j}(v2_{i,j} - vg2_j)\,g31b_i^n\,dx1a_i^n\,g32a_j^{n+1/2}$ and the volume factor $dvl2a$ at the new time, starting from the densities of the first sweep (eqs. 53 and 54). **[GK]** Per unit $x_3$, $g2a_i\,g31a_i\,dvl2a_j$ is the area of a face normal to $x_1$, and $g31b_i\,dx1a_i\,g32a_j$ that of a face normal to $x_2$, exactly when $x1b_i$ lies midway between $x1a_i$ and $x1a_{i+1}$.

The other variables follow in the same way, with two differences ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). First, they are transported consistently with the mass, a notion due to [Norman, Wilson and Barton](#norman-et-al-1980) (1980). The control-volume differencing conserves each variable globally, but the numerical diffusion of the differencing and the interpolation can spoil its local conservation, which consistent transport limits by building the flux of each variable from the flux of mass. It can much improve how well specific angular momentum is conserved locally, and with it the evolution of a system. The quantities advected are thus the density and the specific quantities: the internal energy per unit mass, $e/\rho$, and the specific momenta $s_1/\rho = v_1$, $s_2/\rho = h_2v_2$ and $s_3/\rho = h_3v_3$, whose geometric factors make $s_2$ and $s_3$ angular momenta in cylindrical and spherical geometry. Second, the transport step works with momenta and the source step with velocities, so that the variables are converted when the transport step begins and ends. As density and velocities lie at different places, the conversion uses arithmetic averages of the density. The velocities stay fixed through the transport step, and are updated from the advected densities and momenta at its end.

The mass fluxes $\dot M^1_{i,j} = d^*_{1,i,j}(v1_{i,j} - vg1_i)$ and $\dot M^2_{i,j} = d^*_{2,i,j}(v2_{i,j} - vg2_j)$ are the density fluxes without their face areas ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 55 and 56). The flux of each variable is its interpolated specific value times the mass flux, averaged where needed, times the area of the face. In the 1-direction, this gives $(e/d)^*\dot M^1$ for the energy, $v1^*\,\tfrac12(\dot M^1_{i,j} + \dot M^1_{i+1,j})$ for $s_1$, $v2^*\,g2a_i\,\tfrac12(\dot M^1_{i,j} + \dot M^1_{i,j-1})$ for $s_2$ and $v3^*\,g31a_i\,g32b_j\,\dot M^1_{i,j}$ for $s_3$ (eqs. 57 to 60), with the updates of eqs. 61 to 64, and likewise in the 2-direction (eqs. 65 to 72). The staggering shifts the control volumes. Those of the zone-centred $d$, $e/d$ and $s_3$ coincide, but those of $s_1$ and $s_2$ are centred on the faces in their own direction (fig. 5), with the volume factors $dvl1b$ and $dvl2b$ in those directions. For $d$, $e/d$ and $s_3$ the fluxes thus lie on the faces in both directions, for $s_1$ at the zone centres in the 1-direction and on the faces in the 2-direction, and for $s_2$ the other way round. With the transport step, a cycle of one time step is complete.

Eqs. 65 and 68 give the faces of the zone-centred control volume the width $dx1b_i$, where eq. 53 has $dx1a_i$, the width of the zone, for the same volume. ### 2.5 Boundary conditions and the time step

#### 2.5.1 Boundary conditions

After each update of the source or transport step, boundary conditions fill the ghost zones from the adjacent active zones by explicit formulae, without the evolution equations ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.5). Their form depends on the geometry and physics of a problem. ZEUS-2D offers five, which can be applied to each variable on each boundary separately.

- Reflecting. The zone-centred variables and the tangential velocity in the ghost zones copy the values of the active zones that mirror them. The normal velocity vanishes on the boundary and is reflected in the second ghost zone.
- Axis of symmetry. As the reflecting condition, except that the zone-centred third component of the velocity takes the negative of its mirror value, which forces it to zero on the axis.
- Inflow. The ghost zones keep prescribed values of all variables, which may vary in time, and no outflow is allowed. The condition is exact for supersonic inflow. For subsonic inflow, the boundary should let outgoing waves pass, but in practice it reflects some of them.
- Outflow. The most difficult condition, made as simple as possible: the values of the active zones are copied into the ghost zones, which extrapolates the flow along the grid lines. This is exact for supersonic outflow, but reflects waves spuriously in subsonic outflow. Where these reflections affect the dynamics, a larger domain or a more accurate condition is needed, such as extrapolation along characteristics rather than grid lines ([Thompson](#thompson-1987) 1987).
- Periodic. The zone-centred variables and the tangential velocity in the ghost zones take the values of the active zones on the far side of the grid. So does the normal velocity in the second ghost zone, while on the boundary it follows from the difference equations.

The conditions can be set for every zone of every boundary separately, so that a condition may change along a boundary, as at the inlet of a jet nozzle ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.5). The boundary conditions of each variable sit in a subroutine of their own, which any part of the code can call, so that they are easy to change. They concern the hydrodynamic variables only. [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b) and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) (1992) give those of the magnetic field and the radiation.

#### 2.5.2 Stability and the time step

Upwinding the advected fluxes is essential for stability, as it keeps short, sawtooth oscillations from growing ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.6). An explicit code must also keep its time step within the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition, which limits the distance that information travels in a time step, by waves or with the flow, to less than a zone. In one dimension, $\Delta t \le \min[\Delta x/(|u| + C_a)]$ over all zones, with the flow speed $u$ and the adiabatic sound speed $C_a$ (eq. 73), and a von Neumann analysis of the equations derives this rigorously ([Richtmyer and Morton](#richtmyer-morton-1957) 1957). In several dimensions the one-dimensional limits are combined, and ZEUS-2D takes $\Delta t = C_0/[\max(\delta t_1^{-2} + \delta t_2^{-2} + \delta t_3^{-2} + \delta t_4^{-2})]^{1/2}$, with the maximum over all zones and the Courant number $C_0 \approx 0.5$ as a safety factor (eq. 74). The limits are $\delta t_1 = \min(\Delta x_1, \Delta x_2)/C_a$ for sound, $\delta t_2 = \Delta x_1/(v_1 - vg_1)$ and $\delta t_3 = \Delta x_2/(v_2 - vg_2)$ for the flow relative to the grid (eqs. 75 and 76), and $\delta t_4$ for the artificial viscosity.

The artificial viscosity turns the equation of motion into a diffusion equation, and explicit diffusion is stable only for $\Delta t \le (\Delta x)^2/(4\nu)$, with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 77). Comparison with the Navier–Stokes equations gives $\nu = l^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$ for the artificial viscosity, so that $\delta t_4 = \min[(\Delta x_1)^2/(4l^2\Delta v_1/\Delta x_1),\ (\Delta x_2)^2/(4l^2\Delta v_2/\Delta x_2)] = \min[\Delta x_1/(4C_2\Delta v_1),\ \Delta x_2/(4C_2\Delta v_2)]$ (eq. 78). The time step follows the characteristic speeds as they change, but for accuracy it may grow by at most 30 per cent from one step to the next, while for stability it may shrink by any amount. Fully implicit differencing escapes the Courant condition, but is inefficient for flows with short dynamical time scales and is not used for the hydrodynamics. With radiation, however, the Courant condition for all characteristic speeds can become too restrictive. The radiation variables are therefore evolved fully implicitly, in a mixed explicit and implicit scheme that [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) (1992) describe.

**[GK]** As eq. 74 squares each limit, the signs of $v_1 - vg_1$, $v_2 - vg_2$ and $\Delta v$ in eqs. 76 and 78 do not matter there, nor that of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$ in eq. 144. The two forms of eq. 78 agree only if $C_2 = (l/\Delta x)^2$, the slip of the definition noted in 2.3.2. The later copies of the code take the second form, with `qcon`. In spherical polar coordinates $\Delta x_2$ is an angle, so that the widths in the 2-direction in eqs. 75, 76 and 78 need the factor $g_2$, as the length $g2b_i\,dx2a_j$ in 2.3.4 does.

### 2.6 Self-gravity

The Poisson equation is elliptic, and so easier to solve than the hyperbolic equations of hydrodynamics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). The solver must work on a nonuniform mesh in two dimensions with general boundary conditions, which rules out many of the fastest methods for special problems, such as spectral and Fourier methods, and ZEUS-2D solves the sparse matrix of the differenced equation instead. With the potential zone-centred, the covariant Laplacian of appendix A and the volume differences of 2.2.3 near the origin, the equation becomes $a_1\Phi_{i+1,j} + a_2\Phi_{i,j+1} + a_3\Phi_{i,j} + a_4\Phi_{i-1,j} + a_5\Phi_{i,j-1} = S_{i,j}$ (eq. 79). The coefficients $a_1 = g2a_{i+1}\,g31a_{i+1}\,dvl2a_j/dx1b_{i+1}$ and $a_4 = g2a_i\,g31a_i\,dvl2a_j/dx1b_i$ couple the neighbours in $x_1$, and $a_2 = g32a_{j+1}\,dvl1a_i/(g2b_i^2\,dx2b_{j+1})$ and $a_5 = g32a_j\,dvl1a_i/(g2b_i^2\,dx2b_j)$ those in $x_2$. The paper prints $a_3$ as their sum, and the source as $S_{i,j} = 4\pi G\,d_{i,j}\,dvl1a_i\,dvl2a_j$. The five-point molecule couples each zone to its four neighbours, so that an $N \times N$ grid gives $N^2$ coupled linear equations, with a sparse, banded matrix (fig. 6). The implicit moment equations of the radiation give matrices of the same structure.

The boundary conditions enter the matrix or the right-hand side without changing its structure ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). A boundary can be of Neumann type, with zero slope of the potential, at an axis or equator of symmetry, where it changes the diagonal elements. Or it can be of Dirichlet type, with the potential prescribed in the ghost zones, at an outer boundary far from most of the mass, where it changes the right-hand side. The Dirichlet values come from a multipole expansion ([Jackson](#jackson-1975) 1975), $\Phi_B = G\sum_l P_l(\mu_B)\,r_B^{-(l+1)}M_l$ with the moments $M_l = \int_V \rho\,r^lP_l(\mu)\,dV$ (eqs. 80 and 81). Here $P_l$ is the Legendre polynomial of the cosine $\mu_B$ of the angle between the position of the boundary point and the axis of symmetry, and $r_B$ is its distance from the origin, so that the method needs curvilinear coordinates with such an axis. The polynomials follow from $(n + 1)P_{n+1}(x) = (2n + 1)xP_n(x) - nP_{n-1}(x)$, with $P_0 = 1$ and $P_1 = x$ (eq. 82), and the moments are sums of $d_{i,j}\,r_{i,j}^lP_l(\mu_{i,j})\,dvl1a_i\,dvl2a_j$ over the zones (eqs. 83 and 84). When most of the mass lies far from the boundaries, a few moments suffice. When the density near the boundaries is significant the series converges slowly, and it may diverge for a mass distribution without a central concentration. ZEUS-2D adds moments until the boundary potential has converged to one part in $10^3$, up to 100 terms.

The matrix of eq. 79 is symmetric and positive definite, and several standard methods solve it ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). The alternating direction implicit method (ADI) of [Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) (1986) and [Black and Bodenheimer](#black-bodenheimer-1975) (1975) seeks the steady state of the diffusion equation $\partial\Phi/\partial g = \nabla^2\Phi - 4\pi G\rho$, in an iteration time $g$ unrelated to the physical time, by repeated one-dimensional sweeps (eq. 85). Each sweep needs only tridiagonal matrices, for which fast, vectorisable algorithms are known ([Richtmyer and Morton](#richtmyer-morton-1957) 1957). A dynamic ADI (DADI), which chooses the iteration step adaptively, converges much faster than the simple sequence of steps used here ([Larson, Hewett and Anderson](#larson-et-al-1989) 1989). The authors have also used the incomplete Cholesky conjugate gradient method (ICCG) and the generalised minimal residual method (GMRES) ([Bramley](#bramley-1989) 1989, private communication), with comparable times for grids of about $10^5$ zones, and in practice they use these rather than ADI. The implicit radiation hydrodynamics uses the same solvers and shares their storage, and as all of them rest on library routines, new ones such as DADI are easy to add. This matters, since the sparse solvers take most of the computing time of a run.

A solution is often not needed every cycle unless the density changes rapidly ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). Rearranged, eq. 79 gives a trial potential $\Phi'_{i,j} = (S_{i,j} - a_1\Phi_{i+1,j} - a_2\Phi_{i,j+1} - a_4\Phi_{i-1,j} - a_5\Phi_{i,j-1})/a_3$ from the stored one (eq. 86), and the equation is solved only where $|\Phi'_{i,j} - \Phi_{i,j}|/\Phi_{i,j} > \epsilon$, with $\epsilon \approx 10^{-5}$ (eq. 87). In practice this cuts the solutions to about one cycle in ten.

**[GK]** As printed, eq. 79 cannot hold. Its diagonal coefficient $a_3$ is the sum of the others with the same sign, so that a constant potential would not satisfy the homogeneous equation and the matrix would not be a discrete Laplacian. The diagonal must carry the sign opposite to that of the neighbours, and eq. 86 then changes accordingly. The positive boundary potential of eq. 80 belongs to a potential of the opposite sign to that of eqs. 2, 5 and 30, which satisfies $-\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$ and is positive near a mass. Its matrix, with positive diagonal and negative neighbours, is the positive definite one the paper describes. The later copies of the code use this convention: a comment declares the potential positive, the equation of motion adds its gradient, and the test of eq. 86 has the opposite signs. With the potential of eq. 5, eq. 80 needs a minus sign. Eq. 84 also lacks the factor $2\pi$ of the azimuth, since $dvl1a_i\,dvl2a_j$ is the volume of a zone per unit of $\phi$, and the code includes it.

### 2.7 Hydrodynamic tests

#### 2.7.1 Approach and advection tests

ZEUS-2D implements the algorithms of ZEUS anew and adds capabilities, such as the covariant formalism and third-order interpolation for all variables, so that it needs a full set of hydrodynamic tests ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5). Tests first catch errors in the coding, but their main task is to benchmark the algorithms. They show the strengths and limits of the methods, which helps to tell the features of a simulation that the physics causes from artefacts of the numerics. The tests isolate single terms of the difference equations first and build up to problems that use the whole code: advection in one dimension, then a shock tube in one dimension, and finally strong shocks in two dimensions.

The transport step is the most complicated part of the procedure and is tested first, with the source terms left out and a constant velocity, for which the analytic solutions are trivial ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.1). Results are shown for the density, but the other variables gave identical results, and every test in one dimension was run in each direction separately. A square pulse in Cartesian geometry, 50 zones wide and centred at $x = 30$, is advected over five times its width, so that its edges should end at $x = 255$ and 305 (fig. 8). The donor cell method loses both the shape and the amplitude of the pulse. The van Leer method keeps the amplitude and spreads each edge over about 14 zones, PPA over 6, and PPA with the steepening of discontinuities over 2. Donor cell is unfit for simulations, van Leer and PPA do well, and PPA with steepening could hardly do better. Whatever the scheme, the conservative differencing keeps the area under the pulse to within one part in $10^7$ to $10^8$. The paper gives neither the size of the grid nor the Courant number.

The rate of convergence measures the accuracy of the advection more precisely ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.1). The error is the $L_1$ norm $\epsilon = \sum_{i=1}^N |q_i - \tilde q_i|/N$ over the $N$ grid points, between the numerical solution $q_i$ and the analytic one $\tilde q_i$ (eq. 88). It should fall as $\epsilon \propto \Delta x^r$, and the rate $r$ is the slope of $\log\epsilon$ against the logarithm of the resolution, in principle 1, 2 and 3 for schemes of first, second and third order. A Gaussian pulse, advected over ten times its width, serves this test, as the discontinuities of a square pulse can never be resolved (fig. 9). PPA is the most accurate at every resolution, with an error at the finest more than a thousand times smaller than that of donor cell, and that of the van Leer method a hundred times smaller. PPA converges at the rate 3.0, the van Leer method at 2.1 and donor cell at 0.6, close to the expected values. Other waveforms mainly serve to compare advection schemes ([Clarke](#clarke-1988) 1988; [Woodward](#woodward-1986) 1986), whereas these tests check the coding, and so use few waveforms. The paper gives neither the resolutions nor the width of the pulse.

The covariant formalism is tested by advection in curvilinear geometry ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.2). In a relaxation problem, a uniform density starts with the velocity $v_r = v_0r$ and decays as $\rho = \rho_0e^{-2v_0t}$ in cylindrical and as $\rho = \rho_0e^{-3v_0t}$ in spherical geometry, so that both reach $6.14 \times 10^{-6}\rho_0$, at $v_0t = 6$ and $v_0t = 4$ respectively. ZEUS-2D gives $5.78 \times 10^{-6}\rho_0$ and $5.60 \times 10^{-6}\rho_0$, errors of 5.9 and 8.8 per cent after a fall of nearly six orders of magnitude. The profiles stay perfectly flat, which confirms the terms that depend on the geometry.

#### 2.7.2 Pressure-free collapse

The collapse of a homogeneous sphere without pressure is a last test of advection in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.3). Every mass shell follows the analytic solution of [Hunter](#hunter-1962) (1962), $r/r_0 = \cos^2\beta$ and $\rho/\rho_0 = \cos^{-6}\beta$, with $\beta + \tfrac12\sin 2\beta = t\,[8\pi G\rho(0)/3]^{1/2}$ (eqs. 89 and 90). All shells thus reach the origin together at $\beta = \pi/2$, at the free-fall time $\tau_{ff} = [3\pi/(32G\rho(0))]^{1/2}$. With $\rho = G = 1$ at the start in spherical geometry, $\tau_{ff} = 0.543$, and the run ends at $t = 0.535$, by which time the density has grown by nearly three orders of magnitude. The gravitational force of a homogeneous sphere is computed exactly, so that the test probes the radial advection. On a stationary grid, the density shows a spurious spike in the first few zones at the origin, while elsewhere the numerical and analytic solutions agree well (fig. 10). [Mönchmeyer and Müller](#monchmeyer-muller-1989) (1989) traced such an error to a staggered mesh centred on the coordinates. A mesh centred on the volumes removes it, as does coordinate regularisation in full generality, but at the cost of substantial changes to the difference equations and the transport step. A grid that collapses with the cloud, its velocities set to those of the fluid everywhere but at the boundaries, reduces the advection errors and substantially improves the solution at the origin, which shows the use of a moving mesh. The paper does not give the number of zones or the boundary conditions.

#### 2.7.3 The Sod shock tube

A shock tube tests all transport and source terms in one dimension, the artificial viscosity included ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.4). It became the standard test of hydrodynamic codes with [Sod](#sod-1978) (1978), and later [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984a) (1984a, [b](#hawley-et-al-1984b)). Two states meet at $t = 0$, hot and dense on the left and cool and rarefied on the right, and nonlinear waves run away from the discontinuity: a shock to the right and a rarefaction to the left. The exact solution of this Riemann problem shows whether a code gives the correct jump conditions and shock speed, and gives them stably. The problem is Cartesian and one-dimensional, but can be run in either direction, and along the axis in cylindrical geometry. Sod's states have $\gamma = 1.4$, pressure and density 1.0 on the left, and pressure 0.1 and density 0.125 on the right, with the discontinuity at $x = 0.5$ on a face between zones and the gas at rest. [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984a) (1984a) describe how the exact solution is obtained.

ZEUS-2D, with 100 zones and the van Leer method, follows the exact solution well, with the shock at the right, then the contact discontinuity, and the rarefaction at the left ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, fig. 11). The shock and the contact are spread over about 4 zones, as expected from the artificial viscosity and the second-order advection, and the head and foot of the rarefaction, where the slope jumps, are slightly smoothed. PPA does not reduce this smoothing much. Small overshoots appear where a slope jumps from zero. [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) (1986) showed with an adaptive code in one dimension that these are the correct solution of the viscous difference equations as $\Delta x \to 0$, a sign of low diffusion. The specific internal energy has the wrong amplitude behind the shock, probably because the shock tube is naturally posed in density and pressure, while consistent transport advects the specific internal energy. A code without consistent transport would suit this problem slightly better. [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984b) (1984b) compared the common astrophysical algorithms on this problem, those of ZEUS-2D among them, and the results agree exactly with theirs. They concluded that these algorithms gave the most accurate results of those tested. The paper does not state the time at which the solution is shown.

#### 2.7.4 Strong shocks

[Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984) compared hydrodynamic codes on three demanding problems with strong shocks, the first one-dimensional and the others two-dimensional, and ZEUS-2D was run on all three to benchmark it against those codes ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5).

In the first, two strong blast waves interact in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5.1). A gas with $\gamma = 1.4$ lies at rest between reflecting walls at $x = 0$ and 1, with density 1 throughout and pressure 1000 for $x < 0.1$, 100 for $x > 0.9$ and 0.01 in between. The two shocks reflect from the walls and collide, which produces an intricate pattern of shocks and discontinuities. [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) give no analytic solution, but a PPM calculation at very high resolution as the reference. With 1200 zones at $t = 0.038$, both the van Leer method and PPA place all shocks and contacts where the PPM solution has them (fig. 12). PPA does better, with sharper contacts and a density spike at $x = 0.76$ closer to the PPM value of about 6.5, as expected for a test built to show how the overall accuracy depends on the thickness of discontinuities. Methods that keep shocks thinner, such as PPM or adaptive meshes ([Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) 1986), do better still, but the simple algorithms of ZEUS-2D do quite well.

The second is a flow at Mach 3 through a wind tunnel with a step, in two dimensions ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5.2). The tunnel is 3 units long and 1 high, with a step 0.2 high at 0.6 from its left end. The top and bottom walls reflect, gas flows in on the left and out on the right, and the gas starts with density 1.4, pressure 1.0 and $\gamma = 1.4$, so that Mach 3 means a velocity of 3.0. As [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984) do, the flow near the corner of the step is corrected to reduce the numerical errors of this singular point. In the first four zones above the step and just right of its corner, the density and the speed, but not the direction of the velocity, are reset so that the entropy and the sum of enthalpy and kinetic energy per unit mass equal their values in the zone just left of and below the corner, and the first two zones of the row above are treated alike. On a staggered mesh this is very hard to do properly, since a zone-centred velocity averages those of the faces, so that resetting it changes the velocities of the neighbouring zones too. The solutions therefore suffer from errors generated at the corner, especially at low resolution, but they still compare the advection schemes of ZEUS-2D consistently, and qualitatively with other schemes at high resolution. The grids cover a domain four units long, of which only the left three are analysed, to avoid errors from the outflow boundary.

At $t = 4.0$, on grids of $20 \times 80$, $40 \times 160$ and $80 \times 320$ zones, higher resolution makes the discontinuities thinner and brings the shocks closer to their positions in the best PPM solution ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, figs. 13 and 14). On the coarsest grid, the Mach stem at the upper wall has not yet formed. The error from the corner shows as a spurious Mach stem at the lower reflection, where the shock meets a spurious boundary layer along the step, and it shifts the shocks and reflections, so that only the finest grids come close to the solution of [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984). PPA does better at every resolution: the shock positions and the Mach stem's length lie closer to the PPM values, and the contact from the triple point is sharper. It costs more computing time, about 2.2 times that of the van Leer method on the finest grid (table 1).

The third is the double Mach reflection, in which a Mach 10 shock reflects from a ramp ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5.3). Since a rectangular mesh cannot easily hold a sloping wall, the shock is set up oblique to the mesh and reflects from the lower boundary instead. The domain, 1 unit high and 3 long, holds gas at rest with pressure 1.0 and density 1.4, and $\gamma = 1.4$. A plane shock at 60° to the $x$-axis runs from $x = 1/6$ on the lower boundary to the top at $y = 1$, with the postshock state from the jump conditions: density 8, pressure 116.5 and a velocity of 8.25 normal to the shock. The postshock state is imposed on the left boundary and on the lower boundary between $x = 0$ and $1/6$. The lower boundary reflects from $x = 1/6$ to 3, the right boundary lets the gas out, and the upper boundary follows the exact motion of the shock. Setting the shock obliquely on the grid causes a small error, visible as stray contours from the upper right corner back to the reflected shock, which the results of [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984) also show and which is hard to remove. Again the grids cover four units, of which the left three are shown.

At $t = 0.2$, on grids of $30 \times 120$, $60 \times 240$ and $120 \times 480$ zones, resolution again matters greatly, but without the troublesome corner of the step, the solutions agree much better with the best PPM results at every resolution ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, figs. 15 and 16). On finer grids the shocks are much narrower, and the intricate structure near the reflection, with its small jet along the wall bounded by a contact discontinuity, comes out much better. PPA is again better at every resolution, and on the finest grid it differs little from the best PPM solution, whose shocks are thinner and smoother. It is 2.1 times slower than the van Leer method there (table 2).

The tables give the cost on the Cray X-MP ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, tables 1 and 2). For the wind tunnel, the van Leer method took 1149, 2310 and 4764 cycles and 14.7, 80.5 and 546 s on the three grids, and PPA 1227, 2510 and 5114 cycles and 28.1, 166 and 1190 s. For the double Mach reflection, the van Leer method took 415, 853 and 1734 cycles and 10.5, 64.2 and 456 s, and PPA 434, 888 and 1802 cycles and 19.7, 129 and 970 s. **[GK]** On the finest grids this amounts to about $2.2 \times 10^5$ zone updates per second with the van Leer method and $1.1 \times 10^5$ with PPA.

#### 2.7.5 Tests of the Poisson solver

The Poisson solver is easy to test, as many analytic potentials are known, and even without them the density can be recovered from the computed potential as a check ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.6). The tests use analytic solutions, which also check the gradient of the potential, the quantity that enters the equations. The first object, a uniform sphere of radius $R$ and density $\rho_0$, has $\Phi = 2\pi G\rho_0(R^2 - r^2/3)$ inside and $\tfrac43\pi G\rho_0R^3/r$ outside, and the gradient $-\tfrac43\pi G\rho_0r$ and $-\tfrac43\pi G\rho_0R^3/r^2$ (eqs. 91 and 92). The second is a centrally condensed sphere with $\rho = \rho_c/[1 + (r/r_c)^2]$ inside $R$, for which $\Phi = 4\pi G\rho_cr_c^2\{\arctan(r/r_c)/(r/r_c) + \tfrac12\ln[(1 + (r/r_c)^2)/(1 + (R/r_c)^2)] - 1\}$ inside and $-4\pi G\rho_cr_c^3[R/r_c - \arctan(R/r_c)]/r$ outside, with the gradient $GM(r)/r^2$ and $M(r) = 4\pi\rho_cr_c^3[r/r_c - \arctan(r/r_c)]$ within $R$ (eqs. 93 to 95). The third is a homogeneous ellipsoid of density $\rho_0$ with semi-axes $a_1$ and $a_3$, whose potential follows [Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1969) (1969, p. 42) (eqs. 96 to 108). It involves index symbols for oblate and prolate spheroids that depend on the eccentricity, and outside the body the larger root $\lambda$ of $r^2/(a_1^2 + \lambda) + z^2/(a_3^2 + \lambda) = 1$. Each object was set up in cylindrical and in spherical coordinates, with and without equatorial symmetry.

The relative errors in the potential and its gradient, with equatorial symmetry and then without, are as follows ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, table 3). In cylindrical coordinates, they are $2.23 \times 10^{-4}$ and $6.04 \times 10^{-3}$, then $1.56 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.91 \times 10^{-2}$, for the homogeneous sphere; $3.80 \times 10^{-4}$ and $2.21 \times 10^{-3}$, then $4.29 \times 10^{-3}$ and $2.22 \times 10^{-2}$, for the centrally condensed sphere; and $2.42 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.96 \times 10^{-2}$, then $8.37 \times 10^{-3}$ and $6.44 \times 10^{-2}$, for the ellipsoid. In spherical coordinates, the same sequence reads $4.02 \times 10^{-5}$ and $1.11 \times 10^{-4}$, then $1.31 \times 10^{-4}$ and $9.02 \times 10^{-4}$; $5.60 \times 10^{-4}$ and $9.98 \times 10^{-4}$, then $5.71 \times 10^{-4}$ and $9.99 \times 10^{-4}$; and $3.95 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.86 \times 10^{-2}$, then $5.83 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.74 \times 10^{-2}$. For the ellipsoid without equatorial symmetry, which gives the largest errors, most of the error lies at the surface, where the density drops abruptly to zero and the grid represents the smooth boundary only roughly, so that resolution matters there (figs. 17 and 18). The largest error in the gradient, 6.4 per cent in cylindrical coordinates, falls to 1.7 per cent in spherical ones, which describe the ellipsoid better. Even without assumed symmetry, the error contours pass smoothly and symmetrically across the equator. The paper gives neither the grids nor the sizes of the objects.

Outside the spheres, eq. 91 gives $+GM/r$ and eq. 94 $-GM/r$. Each pair is consistent within its own convention.

#### 2.7.6 Conclusions from the tests

The tests show that the algorithms are implemented correctly and how they perform, insight that helps to avoid artefacts of the numerics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §6). PPA has the lowest diffusion and the highest rate of convergence and is the clear choice for pure advection, but it typically takes twice as long as the van Leer method in two dimensions. Donor cell is too diffusive for any practical use and never serves in simulations. The shock tube reproduces exactly the published results for the same algorithms, and the tests with strong shocks compare qualitatively well with the best PPM results. The small errors in the gradient of the potential come mainly from the limited resolution of the density distribution.

### 2.8 Magnetohydrodynamics

#### 2.8.1 The equations of magnetohydrodynamics

A large-scale magnetic field that threads a plasma exerts stresses on its moving charged particles, whose motion in turn alters the strength and the distribution of the field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §2). Where the gyroradii of the particles are comparable to the scales of interest, the plasma must be described microscopically, by the Maxwell–Boltzmann equations for the distribution function of the particles. Where they are much smaller, a statistical description suffices: the equations of ideal MHD, which evolve the macroscopic conserved quantities. Like the equations of hydrodynamics, they follow from moments of the Maxwell–Boltzmann equations over velocity, here with the further assumptions of quasi-neutrality and of Ohm's law as a closure for the current density ([Spitzer](#spitzer-1962) 1962).

With these assumptions, and following [Jackson](#jackson-1975) (1975), the equations of MHD take the following form ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 1 to 4). The continuity equation, $D\rho/Dt + \rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, and the internal energy equation, $\rho\, D(e/\rho)/Dt = -p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, are those of hydrodynamics (2.1.1). The equation of motion gains the Lorentz force, $\rho\, D\mathbf{v}/Dt = -\nabla p - \rho\nabla\Phi + (1/4\pi)(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\times\mathbf{B}$, and the induction equation, $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = \nabla\times(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$, evolves the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$. An equation of state $p(\rho, e)$ and the Poisson equation for the gravitational potential $\Phi$ close the system. **[GK]** The printed eq. 2 has $p\nabla\Phi$ in place of $\rho\nabla\Phi$. This is a misprint, since only $\rho\nabla\Phi$ is a force per unit volume, and eq. 2 of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a) has it.

Further assumptions shape the terms in $\mathbf{B}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §2). The Lorentz force omits the displacement current from Ampère's law, which holds only for phenomena of low frequency, such as those the code is meant for. In Faraday's law, which yields the induction equation, the electric field is eliminated by keeping only the advective term of the generalised Ohm's law. The justification is that resistivities in astrophysics are mostly so low that the field is in effect frozen into the fluid. In many systems the gas is so highly ionised that collisions between charged and neutral particles couple the two tightly, and flux freezing holds well. Where the ionisation drops markedly, as in the densest parts of molecular clouds, the collisions no longer keep the coupling perfect, and the plasma, with its field, drifts through the neutral gas, a process called ambipolar diffusion. ZEUS-2D includes neither ambipolar diffusion nor resistivity. The authors call adding the diffusive terms to the algorithms straightforward, and a fruitful subject for future work.

Like those of hydrodynamics, the equations of MHD are coupled, nonlinear and hyperbolic partial differential equations ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §2). Many of the algorithms they require are those of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a), and §3 describes what the magnetic field adds and changes.

#### 2.8.2 The divergence constraint and the wave families

Compared with hydrodynamics, MHD poses two main numerical difficulties ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). First, Maxwell's equations require the field to stay free of divergence, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$, at all times. Unless the evolution equations build this constraint in, the numerical errors in the evolved components will in general violate it, and as the errors accumulate, the violation can grow so gross that the calculation must be stopped. [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) discuss the issue in detail. Second, MHD has entirely new families of waves. They bring a wealth of new physics, and the algorithms must propagate every MHD wave stably and accurately.

The need for algorithms that keep the field free of divergence has long been recognised ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). Until recently, the usual approach was to evolve the vector potential $\mathbf{A}$, with $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$, instead of the field, and to obtain the field by differencing the potential wherever it is needed. If the difference forms of divergence and curl are built consistently, the discrete counterpart of the identity $\nabla\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{A} = 0$ holds exactly. In two dimensions, the evolution of the potential reduces to the advection of a single scalar. The original ZEUS evolved the potential in this way to simulate axisymmetric jets with MHD ([Clarke](#clarke-1988) 1988).

The trouble with the potential lies in the Lorentz force, which needs its second derivatives ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). [Clarke, Norman and Burns](#clarke-et-al-1989) (1989) argue that a Lorentz force of first-order accuracy requires a scheme of third order for the potential, such as PPA. [Choptuik](#choptuik-1986) (1986), on the other hand, stresses that numerical derivatives of a smooth function need not lose order of accuracy compared with the analytic derivative. Still, [Clarke](#clarke-1988) (1988) showed beyond doubt, by numerical tests, that many advection problems need a scheme of higher order to give an accurate current, the first derivative of the field. Even then, the current and the force reverse anomalously near shocks, contact discontinuities and other sharp features of the field. These reversals act directly on the flow, and accelerate and heat it spuriously.

[Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) developed constrained transport (CT), which builds the constraint directly into the difference equations for the field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). As the components of the field are evolved themselves, no numerical second derivatives are needed, and the current comes out accurate and smooth, without anomalous reversals. At the same time, the method keeps $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ to machine round-off at all times. For these reasons ZEUS-2D adopts CT.

The second difficulty calls for a scheme that represents all MHD waves accurately ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). The upwind schemes that evolve the hydrodynamic variables might seem suitable for the field as well. MHD, however, has a family of incompressible transverse waves without a hydrodynamic analogue, the Alfvén waves, which couple the velocity and the field tightly. Applied to the MHD equations, the hydrodynamic upwind schemes were found to give these waves a large dispersion error. The error shows most clearly where sharp or discontinuous features evolve, or where smooth features meet a boundary, and there it creates oscillations of large amplitude and short wavelength that are stable, but neither damped nor diffused away. Of the methods tried, only schemes that are upwind in the characteristics of the Alfvén waves removed the error. A large intrinsic diffusion removes it as well, but the authors consider that unsuitable.

One method of this kind is the method of characteristics, MOC ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). Codes built entirely on it exist ([Lou, Rosner and Ulmschneider](#lou-et-al-1987) 1987) and are upwind in every family of MHD waves by construction, but they give up the conservative form, the divergence constraint and the simple difference forms of the primitive equations. ZEUS-2D therefore solves by the MOC only the subset of the equations that describes how Alfvén waves propagate, as an intermediate step that yields time-advanced quantities for the update of the field components.

In axisymmetric calculations in two dimensions, the symmetry keeps the toroidal component free of divergence, as $\partial B_\phi/\partial\phi \equiv 0$, so that only the poloidal components need a formalism that preserves the constraint, such as CT ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). The poloidal components are those in the plane of the mesh, and the toroidal component is the one normal to it. The toroidal field is evolved by a simple algorithm built on the MOC (§3.3), and the poloidal field by a form of the MOC within CT, called MOC-CT (§3.2). **[GK]** The argument holds in all three coordinate systems of ZEUS-2D, not only in the axisymmetric ones: with no dependence on $x_3$, the component $B_3$ drops out of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$ (2.2.4).

#### 2.8.3 The centring of the field

Before the difference equations for the field can be written, its components must be placed on the staggered mesh of 2.2.1, with the 3-direction normal to the computational plane of $x_1$ and $x_2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.1). The paper counts the right centring among the key aspects of any numerical scheme. An Alfvén wave couples the velocity and field components perpendicular to its direction of propagation, $v_1$ with $B_1$ for waves along the 2-direction and $v_2$ with $B_2$ for waves along the 1-direction. The field is therefore centred like the velocity: $B_1$ on the faces normal to the 1-direction, $B_2$ on those normal to the 2-direction, and $B_3$ at the zone centres (fig. 1). With this centring, each poloidal component is perpendicular to a face of the zone, like the momenta. This differs from the centring that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) originally described.

The discrete components are thus $B1_{i,j} = B_1(x1a_i, x2b_j)$, $B2_{i,j} = B_2(x1b_i, x2a_j)$ and $B3_{i,j} = B_3(x1b_i, x2b_j)$, at the positions of $v1_{i,j}$, $v2_{i,j}$ and $v3_{i,j}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.1). An important derived quantity is the current density, which the paper defines as $\mathbf{j} = \nabla\times\mathbf{B}$. With this centring, its poloidal components come to lie where the orthogonal field components are, $j_1$ with $B_2$ and $j_2$ with $B_1$, while $j_3$ lies at the zone corners (fig. 1). The figure places the 3-component of the electromotive force (EMF) of the two-dimensional CT scheme at the corners as well.

**[GK]** In Gaussian units, Ampère's law without the displacement current gives the current density $(c/4\pi)\nabla\times\mathbf{B}$, and the Lorentz force per unit volume is $\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B}/c$, which yields the term $(1/4\pi)(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\times\mathbf{B}$ of eq. 2 of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b). The $\mathbf{j}$ of its §3.1 drops the factor $c/4\pi$. Its centring follows from the curl of 2.2.4 when nothing depends on $x_3$. Then $j_1$ is a difference in $x_2$ of the zone-centred $h_3B_3$ and so lies on the faces normal to $x_2$, and $j_2$, a difference of the same in $x_1$, lies on the faces normal to $x_1$. The component $j_3$ combines differences of $h_2B_2$ in $x_1$ and of $h_1B_1$ in $x_2$, which both fall on the corners.

#### 2.8.4 Constrained transport

The poloidal field is evolved in three parts ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2): the CT formalism, which preserves the constraint (§3.2.1), the MOC-CT scheme, by which every poloidal family of MHD waves evolves accurately (§3.2.2), and the differencing of the Lorentz force in the equation of motion (§3.2.3).

[Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) built CT on the insight that a scheme which evolves the magnetic flux conservatively satisfies the constraint identically ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). Stokes' theorem turns Faraday's law, eq. 4, into the integral form $\partial\Phi_S/\partial t = \oint_{\partial S}(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (eq. 5), where $\Phi_S$ is the magnetic flux through a surface $S$ with boundary $\partial S$. The flux through a surface thus changes at the rate given by the line integral, around its edges, of the electromotive force (EMF) $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$. With the centring of 2.8.3, the components of the flux lie on the faces of the control volume.

The boundary of each face consists of four edges, and the components of the EMF are centred on the midpoints of the edges ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). On the two-dimensional grid, $\epsilon_1 = v_2B_3 - v_3B_2$ then lies on the faces normal to the 2-direction, where $B_2$ lies, $\epsilon_2 = v_3B_1 - v_1B_3$ on those normal to the 1-direction, where $B_1$ lies, and $\epsilon_3 = v_1B_2 - v_2B_1$ at the zone corners. Fig. 2 extends a zone along $x_3$ to show the contours and the direction of integration, with the field components drawn against the coordinate directions. With the EMF taken as constant along each edge, the right-hand rule gives $(\Phi1^{n+1}_{i,j} - \Phi1^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = (\epsilon3_{i,j+1} - \epsilon3_{i,j})\,\Delta x3$ for the flux through a face normal to $x_1$ (eq. 6), and $(\Phi2^{n+1}_{i,j} - \Phi2^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = -(\epsilon3_{i+1,j} - \epsilon3_{i,j})\,\Delta x3$ for one normal to $x_2$ (eq. 7), where $\Delta x3$ is the length of an edge along $x_3$. The terms in $\epsilon_1$ and $\epsilon_2$, which the full form of the equations shows on the edges along $x_1$ and $x_2$, cancel by symmetry in two dimensions.

Summing eqs. 6 and 7 over the faces of a zone gives the change of the total flux through its surface ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). The EMF of each edge enters the equations of the two faces it bounds once each, with opposite signs, so that all EMF terms cancel in the sum. If the old flux satisfied the constraint, with no net flux through the closed surface by Gauss's law, so does the new: eqs. 6 and 7 guarantee that the changes of the flux are free of divergence. Global conservation follows if the EMF is computed once for the whole grid and the same values update neighbouring zones. Summed over all zones, eqs. 6 and 7 then conserve the total flux through the domain to machine round-off. As [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) point out, this is exactly analogous to the conservative differencing of hydrodynamic variables such as the density, where the flux through each zone face, computed once, is used twice: once as mass leaves a zone and once as it enters the next.

Expressing the fluxes by the face areas and the field components turns eqs. 6 and 7 into evolution equations for the components themselves ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988), with general relativistic MHD in mind, derived covariant equations in terms of the magnetic vector density, which make no reference to the metric of a particular coordinate system and so hold in any. For nonrelativistic MHD, where the components of the metric are known analytically, covariant equations in which they appear explicitly, as in the formalism of 2.2.3, are more convenient than a transformation to the magnetic vector density. The flux components and the edge length are then $\Phi_1 = h_2h_3\,dx_2\,dx_3\,B_1$, $\Phi_2 = h_1h_3\,dx_1\,dx_3\,B_2$ and $\Delta x3 = h_3\,dx_3$ (eqs. 8 to 10).

Substituting these expressions into eqs. 6 and 7 gives the difference equations by which ZEUS-2D evolves the poloidal field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 11 and 12). The 1-component is $B1^{n+1}_{i,j} = [B1^n_{i,j}\,g2a^n_i\,g31a^n_i\,g32b^n_j\,dx2a^n_j + \Delta t\,(g31a^{n+1/2}_i\,g32a^{n+1/2}_{j+1}\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j+1} - g31a^{n+1/2}_i\,g32a^{n+1/2}_j\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j})]/(g2a^{n+1}_i\,g31a^{n+1}_i\,g32b^{n+1}_j\,dx2a^{n+1}_j)$, and the 2-component is $B2^{n+1}_{i,j} = [B2^n_{i,j}\,g31b^n_i\,g32a^n_j\,dx1a^n_i - \Delta t\,(g31a^{n+1/2}_{i+1}\,g32a^{n+1/2}_j\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i+1,j} - g31a^{n+1/2}_i\,g32a^{n+1/2}_j\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j})]/(g31b^{n+1}_i\,g32a^{n+1}_j\,dx1a^{n+1}_i)$. The scale factors appear at the old and the new time, which accounts for the compression or expansion of a moving grid, and the terms in the EMF are centred in time, at $n + 1/2$. Only the EMF remains to be specified. **[GK]** The last lines of eqs. 6 and 7 factor out one length $\Delta x3$ for both edges, which is exact only where $h_3$ is the same on them, as in Cartesian coordinates. Eqs. 11 and 12 take $h_3$ at each edge, $g31a_i\,g32a_j$ for the edge through the corner $(i, j)$, so that an edge enters both faces it bounds with the same length, and the cancellation holds in every geometry.

CT preserves the constraint, but cannot create it: the initial field must itself be free of divergence ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). Setting it up from a vector potential, as [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) suggest, is a convenient way to ensure this. Perhaps more important, the boundary conditions on the field must not introduce divergence into the domain, a point that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) also stress. ZEUS-2D therefore sets boundary values for the EMF and evolves the field components of the ghost zones like the active ones, and §3.4 gives the kinds of boundary conditions for the EMF.

#### 2.8.5 The electromotive force by the method of characteristics

Within CT, any method of computing the EMF keeps the field free of divergence, but in practice the method must be stable and must describe the MHD flow accurately ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). The stability requirement comes from the transport terms of the induction equation, through which advection of the fluid produces an EMF. [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) gave a stable method for advection problems, which can be written schematically as $\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j} = v1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}\,B2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j} - v2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}\,B1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ (eq. 13), where the starred quantities are time-centred values at the zone corners, where the EMF lies. They must be interpolated in space and time from the face-centred values that ZEUS-2D evolves. [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) averaged the velocity arithmetically at the old time, without centring it in time, and interpolated the field upstream with the van Leer method of 2.4.2.

This way of computing the EMF proved entirely adequate for the advection tests of [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988), but in fully dynamical problems it fails to carry shear Alfvén waves accurately along poloidal field lines ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2), as the tests of §4.6 show. This motivated the MOC-CT scheme.

The core of MOC-CT is to compute with the MOC the time-advanced values of both the velocity and the field from which the EMF is formed ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). The MOC step is simplified by using only the characteristics of the Alfvén waves and leaving out those of the compressive, fast and slow, MHD waves. The compressive families are tightly coupled to the hydrodynamics through the thermodynamics, so that the hydrodynamic algorithms, which evolve the compressive hydrodynamic waves, that is sound, also treat the compressive MHD waves accurately. The full MHD algorithm of ZEUS-2D thus accounts for all families of MHD waves in multidimensional flows, which the tests of §4 demonstrate.

The equations of the MOC step follow from those of MHD for a moving, incompressible fluid in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). With all variables functions of $x$ alone, and the units of $B$ chosen such that $\mu_0 = 1$, they reduce to $\partial v/\partial t = (B_x/\rho)\,\partial B/\partial x - \partial(v_xv)/\partial x$ (eq. 14) and $\partial B/\partial t = B_x\,\partial v/\partial x - \partial(v_xB)/\partial x$ (eq. 15), where $v_x$ and $B_x$ are the components along $x$, and $v$ and $B$ stand for the transverse components that the waves carry. The constraint in one dimension, $\partial B_x/\partial x \equiv 0$, and incompressibility, $\partial v_x/\partial x \equiv 0$, simplify the induction equation and the Lorentz force considerably. The leading terms, coupled, describe pure transverse Alfvén waves along $x$, and the second terms the advection by the fluid. The equations thus carry information in a moving fluid only by Alfvén waves, and admit no compressive modes. **[GK]** With $\mu_0 = 1$ the factor $1/4\pi$ of eq. 2 is absorbed into $B$, which amounts to measuring the Gaussian field in units of $(4\pi)^{1/2}$, and the Alfvén speed is $|B_x|/\rho^{1/2}$.

Multiplying eq. 15 by $\rho^{-1/2}$ and adding it to eq. 14 or subtracting it gives the characteristic form $Dv/Dt \mp \rho^{-1/2}\,DB/Dt = 0$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eq. 16), with $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + (v_x \pm B_x/\rho^{1/2})\,\partial/\partial x$ (eq. 17). The upper signs hold along the forward characteristic $C^+$, and the lower ones along the backward characteristic $C^-$. Their coefficient, $v_x \pm v_A$, is the Alfvén speed carried along by the flow. Along these characteristics, which are straight lines of slope $v_x \pm v_A$ in spacetime, the changes of velocity and field in each direction are therefore not independent.

The difference equations follow from a one-dimensional spacetime diagram centred on the position of the EMF, where the partially updated values $v_i^{*,n+1/2}$ and $B_i^{*,n+1/2}$ are needed ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2, fig. 3). By construction they lie midway between the velocities and fields known at time level $n$, which on the staggered mesh places them at the zone corners, with the EMF. Traced back along $C^+$ and $C^-$ to the old time level $n$, at which all variables are known, the characteristics end at their footpoints. Upwind interpolation of the values at time level $n$, with the donor cell, van Leer or PPA method, gives the time-averaged values over the domain of dependence of each footpoint: $v_i^{-,n}$ and $B_i^{-,n}$ on $C^-$, and $v_i^{+,n}$ and $B_i^{+,n}$ on $C^+$. For the velocities and the fields alike, the upwinding uses the characteristic speeds $v_x \pm v_A$. Along the two characteristics the equations are then differenced as $(v_i^{*,n+1/2} - v_i^{+,n}) - (B_i^{*,n+1/2} - B_i^{+,n})/(\rho_i^+)^{1/2} = 0$ (eq. 18) and $(v_i^{*,n+1/2} - v_i^{-,n}) + (B_i^{*,n+1/2} - B_i^{-,n})/(\rho_i^-)^{1/2} = 0$ (eq. 19). To centre the denominators approximately in time, $\rho_i^+ = \rho_{i-1}^n$ and $\rho_i^- = \rho_i^n$. This pair of linear equations is solved directly for $v_i^{*,n+1/2}$ and $B_i^{*,n+1/2}$. **[GK]** The solution is $B_i^{*,n+1/2} = [v_i^{-,n} - v_i^{+,n} + B_i^{+,n}/(\rho_i^+)^{1/2} + B_i^{-,n}/(\rho_i^-)^{1/2}]/[(\rho_i^+)^{-1/2} + (\rho_i^-)^{-1/2}]$, with $v_i^{*,n+1/2}$ then from eq. 18. For equal densities, $v^*$ is the mean of the two footpoint velocities plus $(B^- - B^+)/(2\rho^{1/2})$, and $B^*$ the mean of the two footpoint fields plus $\rho^{1/2}(v^- - v^+)/2$.

In several dimensions the characteristic equations are solved by directional splitting, to obtain time-advanced values of all components of velocity and field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). First, at each grid point, the pair for Alfvén waves along the 2-direction is solved: $Dv_1/Dt \mp \rho^{-1/2}\,DB_1/Dt = 0$ with $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + (v_2 \pm B_2/\rho^{1/2})\,h_2^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_2$ (eqs. 20 and 21), differenced like eqs. 18 and 19 but upwinded with the characteristic speeds $v_2 \pm B_2/\rho^{1/2}$. It yields $v1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ and $B1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ at every zone corner. The pair for Alfvén waves along the 1-direction follows: $Dv_2/Dt \mp \rho^{-1/2}\,DB_2/Dt = 0$ with $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + (v_1 \pm B_1/\rho^{1/2})\,\partial/\partial x_1$ (eqs. 22 and 23), upwinded with $v_1 \pm B_1/\rho^{1/2}$, which yields $v2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ and $B2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ at every corner.

Because the velocity and field components share their positions on the mesh, the MOC applies naturally to the coupled pair in each direction, and both sweeps give their results at the zone corners ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). This is why ZEUS-2D departs from the centring of [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988). On grids where velocity and field components do not coincide, averaging either quantity to where the other lies was found to spoil how the characteristics follow Alfvén waves, and gave no suitable algorithm. With both components known at every corner at the advanced time, eq. 13 gives the EMF, and the CT update follows. The directional splitting extends naturally to three dimensions ([Clarke, Stone and Norman](#clarke-et-al-1990) 1990).

Directional splitting only approximates the true characteristic problem in several dimensions ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). Interpolating along the principal axes of the characteristic cone, rather than along the grid lines, would probably be more accurate. On a staggered mesh, however, the exact direction of the field lines, and with it that of the characteristics, is not known at any one point, and averaging the field components to a common position to find it would probably add diffusion, much as the present splitting is likely to do. Which formulation is the most accurate is left to future numerical work, and the tests of §4 show the accuracy of the present one.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture draws on steps 2, 6, 11 and 17 of `plan.md`.

### 3.1 Data layout

Each variable is stored per zone, ghost zones included, and scalars and vector components share the indices of their zone, as 2.2.1 describes ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). In the index names of fig. 1, the ghost zones are $iim2$ and $iim1$ below $ii$ and $iop1$ and $iop2$ above $io$, and likewise in $j$. The domain boundary runs along the a-mesh lines $i = ii$ and $i = iop1$, and $j = ji$ and $j = jop1$. The active zones, which the evolution equations update, run from $ii$ to $io$ and from $ji$ to $jo$ for the zone-centred quantities, from $ii + 1$ to $io$ in $i$ for $v1$, and from $ji + 1$ to $jo$ in $j$ for $v2$. For a domain with stepped boundaries, the scalars $is$, $ie$, $js$ and $je$ hold the smallest and largest indices over the whole grid. The scale factors, their derivatives and the volume factors are kept like the coordinates, as one-dimensional arrays on both meshes, such as $g2a_i$ and $g2b_i$, and are computed once at the start when the mesh does not move (§4.2).

### 3.2 Program control

Each cycle of the main loop advances the solution by one time step ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.8, fig. 7). After the initialisation and the set-up of a problem, a cycle solves the Poisson equation (eq. 79), performs the source step in its three substeps (eqs. 30, 31, 35 to 37 and 40) and the directionally split transport step (eqs. 51 to 72), computes new grid velocities if the grid moves, and finds a new time step from the Courant condition. The loop repeats until one of several criteria stops it. It also handles the output, and an interrupt checker accepts several dozen commands during a run, to dump data, set flags and variables, or halt, resume and restart the run.

The code is meant to be modular and portable ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.8). Its Fortran follows the practice that [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) (1986) set out for writing efficient code free of bugs, and keeps as closely as possible to the Fortran 77 standard. Graphics rely on widely available software: vector graphics from the NCAR graphics library, raster images for animation in postprocessing tools on workstations, such as Imagetool and Datascope, and floating-point data in the HDF format of the NCSA. All of it is public, with source code, so that ZEUS-2D and its graphics can be compiled and run on any machine with Fortran and C compilers. The full code, with MHD and radiation, exceeds 30,000 lines of source, not counting the common blocks substituted into it. The C preprocessor CPP, together with the directories and utilities of Unix, manages its editing and compilation. CPP macros include or suppress the parts of the code that serve a physical process, a geometry or an output mode, so that an executable built without the macro for MHD carries no arrays or subroutines for it. The authors judge that without a precompiler a code of the complexity of ZEUS-2D could hardly be developed and applied.
