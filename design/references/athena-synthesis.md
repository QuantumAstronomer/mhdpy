# Synthesis of Athena and Athena++

This synthesis digests Athena++, a code for astrophysical magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) on meshes with adaptive refinement, written in C++, and Athena, its predecessor written in C. The code of Athena++ is kept as `reference-works/Athena/Code`, in a copy that is cited at release v24.0 (1.1). Thirteen papers complete the sources, kept in `reference-works/Athena/Docs`: seven on Athena, whose code is not among the sources, and six on Athena++ (1.3). Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in these sources or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. Code is cited by its path in the repository and the name of a function or class, as in (`src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/hlld.cpp`, `Hydro::RiemannSolver`). A paper is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables in the local version, as in [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, [arXiv:0804.0402](https://arxiv.org/abs/0804.0402), §4.2.3, eq. 46), and its arXiv identifier is given at its first citation. Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone. Each method is described once, from the papers and the code together: the papers on Athena tell what Athena does, and the code and the papers on Athena++ what Athena++ does. Where the two codes differ, the text says so.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, §1) explain the need for Athena from the limits of ZEUS ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, [b](#stone-norman-1992b); [Clarke](#clarke-1996) 1996; [Hayes et al.](#hayes-et-al-2006) 2006), digested in `zeus-synthesis.md`. The operator-split algorithm of ZEUS, with upwind advection of higher order, centred differences for the other terms and artificial viscosity at shocks, had served in many hundreds of astrophysical studies and was easy to extend with further physics. In the fifteen years since, refining the mesh, statically (SMR) or adaptively (AMR), had become the means to resolve wide ranges of scale on grids. Conservation at the internal boundaries between coarse and fine meshes is needed to prevent spurious reflections there ([Berger and Colella](#berger-colella-1989) 1989), so a scheme that does not solve the equations in conservative form, such as that of ZEUS, does not suit refined meshes. This was the main motive for Athena. Its algorithms are unsplit Godunov methods of higher order, which suit SMR and AMR and capture shocks, contact discontinuities and rotational discontinuities better. Other MHD codes for AMR on this basis already existed: RIEMANN ([Balsara](#balsara-2000) 2000), BATS-R-US ([Powell et al.](#powell-et-al-1999) 1999; [Gombosi et al.](#gombosi-et-al-2004) 2004), AMRVAC ([Tóth](#toth-1996) 1996; [Nool and Keppens](#nool-keppens-2002) 2002), Nirvana ([Ziegler](#ziegler-2005) 2005), RAMSES ([Fromang, Hennebelle and Teyssier](#fromang-et-al-2006) 2006), PLUTO ([Mignone et al.](#mignone-et-al-2007) 2007) and AstroBEAR ([Cunningham et al.](#cunningham-et-al-2007) 2007). The authors justify another code by their experience that the details of an algorithm matter, and those of Athena differ from the others in small or substantial ways. They wanted the code to be accurate, simple to use and to adapt, and easy to maintain, and meant the paper as a reference for anyone who adopts, modifies or extends it.

Godunov methods for MHD had advanced mainly in two respects, the integrator in several dimensions and the way the magnetic field is kept free of divergence (§1). The options tried ranged from integrators split by direction that do not constrain the divergence at all to split and unsplit integrators combined with a Hodge projection of the field, with a non-conservative form of the equations that lets errors in the constraint propagate and decay, or with constrained transport (CT) after [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988). [Tóth](#toth-2000) (2000) compared many of them on a broad suite of tests. Athena discretises mass, momentum and energy as volume averages over cells and the magnetic field as area averages over the faces of cells, which CT evolves (abstract). Its paper names three new elements. The first is a consistent way to obtain the electric fields that CT needs, averaged over time and over the edges of cells, from the Godunov fluxes, which are averages over time and faces. The second extends to MHD the reconstruction schemes whose prediction in time is split by direction, such as PPM after [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984). The third extends two unsplit integrators to MHD: the corner transport upwind (CTU) method of [Colella](#colella-1990) (1990), which Athena combines with CT as CTU+CT, and a simpler predictor-corrector scheme from the appendix of [Falle](#falle-1991) (1991), close to the MUSCL–Hancock scheme ([van Leer](#van-leer-2006) 2006; [Toro](#toro-1999) 1999), as VL+CT (§1). [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, [arXiv:astro-ph/0501557](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0501557)) set out the mathematics in two dimensions and [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) (2008, [arXiv:0712.2634](https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.2634)) in three, while the paper on the code describes the implementation. CTU+CT proved less diffusive and more accurate, so that paper describes it and leaves VL+CT, which has advantages in some applications, to a companion paper, which it cites as submitted in 2008. **[GK]** That paper appeared as [Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2009) (2009), which is not among the sources, as 1.3 explains.

The summary of the paper on Athena stresses what the combination achieves (§9). With volume and area averages, mass, momentum, energy and magnetic flux stay exactly conserved on the grid, which AMR requires. The tests show that smooth flows of every family of MHD waves converge at second order in space and time, also in several dimensions. The unsplit methods keep the symmetries of a flow: the CTU+CT method in two dimensions reduces exactly to the method in one dimension for plane-parallel flows aligned with the grid, and in three dimensions to those in two or one. On this property rests a sensitive test of the stencil that keeps the field free of divergence. A planar loop of field advected by a velocity field in three dimensions must stay planar, since any field perpendicular to its plane grows in proportion to $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$. The refinement of the mesh, the main motive for the code, was left to a later paper, together with the CTU+CT integrator on refined meshes. Gravity from a fixed potential and self-gravity, the shearing box ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2006) 2006), anisotropic thermal conduction ([Parrish and Stone](#parrish-stone-2005) 2005, [2007](#parrish-stone-2007)) and the transfer of ionising radiation ([Krumholz, Stone and Gardiner](#krumholz-et-al-2007) 2007) had been added, and curvilinear coordinates, relativistic MHD and radiation MHD with full transport were under way. Athena already served to study the magnetorotational instability (MRI) in the shearing box, colliding winds in close binaries, the decay of turbulence, the magnetic Rayleigh–Taylor instability, shocks striking magnetised clouds and supersonic turbulence in molecular clouds. The code was available on the web, with documentation and further test problems.

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020, §1) place Athena++ in a trend towards ever greater complexity among the codes of astrophysical fluid dynamics. Realistic models need further physics, among it the transfer of radiation, self-gravity, networks of chemical or nuclear reactions and dynamical spacetimes, grids adopt one of several strategies of AMR, codes offer many options of coordinates, Riemann solvers and orders of accuracy, and computers grow more heterogeneous. Among the widely used grid codes they name ZEUS ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, [b](#stone-norman-1992b); [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992) 1992; [Hayes et al.](#hayes-et-al-2006) 2006), ART ([Kravtsov, Klypin and Khokhlov](#kravtsov-et-al-1997) 1997), FLASH ([Fryxell et al.](#fryxell-et-al-2000) 2000), RAMSES ([Teyssier](#teyssier-2002) 2002), HARM ([Gammie, McKinney and Tóth](#gammie-et-al-2003) 2003), PLUTO ([Mignone et al.](#mignone-et-al-2007) 2007, [2012](#mignone-et-al-2012)), Athena and Enzo ([Bryan et al.](#bryan-et-al-2014) 2014). Athena itself had grown in this way. It gained further time integrators ([Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2009) 2009), the shearing box ([Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2010) 2010, [arXiv:1006.0139](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.0139)), cylindrical coordinates ([Skinner and Ostriker](#skinner-ostriker-2010) 2010, [arXiv:1004.2487](https://arxiv.org/abs/1004.2487)), special relativity ([Beckwith and Stone](#beckwith-stone-2011) 2011, [arXiv:1101.3573](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.3573)), particles ([Bai and Stone](#bai-stone-2010) 2010, [arXiv:1005.4980](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.4980)) and sink particles ([Gong and Ostriker](#gong-ostriker-2013) 2013). It also gained a formulation of self-gravity that conserves the total energy ([Jiang et al.](#jiang-et-al-2013) 2013) and the transport of radiation ([Skinner and Ostriker](#skinner-ostriker-2013) 2013; [Davis, Stone and Jiang](#davis-et-al-2012) 2012; [Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2012) 2012, [2014a](#jiang-et-al-2014a)), among other features. Maintaining it had become untenable, and its AMR, built on overlapping patches ([Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) 1984; [Berger and Colella](#berger-colella-1989) 1989), performed poorly on highly parallel machines. Athena++ is therefore a complete redesign. Its framework separates the mesh from the physics solved on it, refines the mesh by blocks rather than patches, and schedules the steps of an algorithm dynamically through lists of tasks (3). The language C++ was chosen for the modularity and organisation that its extensions allow.

The paper on Athena++ introduces its framework for AMR, with refinement by blocks of variables at cell centres and on faces, as MHD requires, its use in various coordinate systems and its dynamic execution by task lists (§1). As examples of modules of physics, it describes solvers for MHD, both non-relativistic and relativistic, based on the methods of Athena. It confines itself to what is new in the framework, since [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, [2008](#gardiner-stone-2008)), [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008), [Beckwith and Stone](#beckwith-stone-2011) (2011) and [White, Stone and Gammie](#white-et-al-2016) (2016, [arXiv:1511.00943](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.00943)) describe the algorithms in detail. Its abstract adds that these modules support curvilinear coordinates, time integrators of higher order, a general EOS and diffusion that super time-stepping can integrate, and that they run with well over 80% parallel efficiency on more than half a million threads. Further modules were in preparation (§5). Self-gravity was solved by FFT, following Athena, in the public version, and by full multigrid (FMG), which costs $O(N)$ operations against $O(N \log N)$ for FFT. FMG was being extended to AMR ([Tomida et al.](#tomida-et-al-2023), in preparation), and was to serve implicit moment methods of radiation transfer as well ([Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2012) 2012). The time-dependent radiation transport of [Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2014a) (2014a) was implemented and used for radiation-dominated accretion discs ([Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2014b) 2014b) and massive stars ([Jiang et al.](#jiang-et-al-2018) 2018), and was being extended to general relativity. Flux-limited diffusion and the variable Eddington tensor ([Davis, Stone and Jiang](#davis-et-al-2012) 2012; [Jiang, Stone and Davis](#jiang-et-al-2012) 2012), a Monte Carlo solver for post-processing and the adaptive ray tracing of Athena ([Kim et al.](#kim-et-al-2017) 2017) were to follow. Chemical reaction networks were implemented with a public solver for sparse matrices ([Gong et al.](#gong-et-al-2023), in preparation), and nuclear networks were being built on the same basis ([Halevi et al.](#halevi-et-al), in preparation). Together with passive scalars, they make Athena++ a code for chemo-hydrodynamics. A general module for particles coupled to the gas, based on the methods of [Bai and Stone](#bai-stone-2010) (2010), was under development ([Yang et al.](#yang-et-al), in preparation). In the copy, the multigrid solver works on refined meshes (2.10), radiation transport is present with explicit and implicit integration and with groups of frequencies, and chemistry and cosmic rays have modules of their own (2.15), while particles have none. **[GK]** The works in preparation on multigrid and chemistry appeared as [Tomida and Stone](#tomida-stone-2023) (2023, [arXiv:2302.13903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13903)), one of the sources, and as [Gong et al.](#gong-et-al-2023) (2023), listed under [Gong et al.](#gong-et-al-2023) in 4.

The summary of the paper on Athena++ restates the design and adds its results (§6). The framework accepts any logically rectangular coordinates and nonuniform spacing. Different regions of a calculation may carry different task lists, so that, for instance, kinetic MHD in dense, weakly collisional regions could be combined with particle-in-cell methods in diffuse, collisionless ones, and the Poisson equation could be solved on cores other than those for the MHD. The MHD modules extend the methods of Athena by reconstruction of higher order on curvilinear and nonuniform meshes, time integrators of higher order by the method of lines, and diffusion advanced by super time-stepping of the Runge–Kutta–Legendre type, and they work with AMR. Optimised for vector instructions, the MHD module is among the fastest public astrophysical MHD codes known to the authors, in tests of several codes built with the same compilers and optimisations, matched only by DISPATCH ([Nordlund et al.](#nordlund-et-al-2018) 2018). All cores of one Intel Skylake processor together are about five times slower than the same algorithm on an NVIDIA Volta GPU ([Grete, Glines and O'Shea](#grete-et-al-2019) 2019), as the ratio of their peak performance leads one to expect, and weak scaling keeps 84% parallel efficiency with as many as 500,000 threads. A fully fourth-order algorithm for MHD ([Felker and Stone](#felker-stone-2018) 2018, [arXiv:1711.07439](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07439)) had been implemented in the framework and was being compared with the existing algorithms on astrophysical applications. The copy offers the fourth order for hydrodynamics only, since its reconstruction refuses it with MHD while the fourth-order CT is not yet merged (`src/reconstruct/reconstruction.cpp`, `Reconstruction::Reconstruction`; 2.7). A version of the whole framework built on the library Kokkos ([Edwards, Trott and Sunderland](#edwards-et-al-2014) 2014) was being prepared for release as open source. Athena++ is public on GitHub under the BSD licence, and new modules join the public version once they are thoroughly tested.

## 2. Numerical methods

Sixteen subjects group the numerical methods by component. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

Both codes solve the equations of MHD by a Godunov method. The hydrodynamic variables are averages over the volumes of cells, updated by the fluxes through their faces (2.1), while the magnetic field consists of averages over the faces, updated by the electric fields on the edges of cells so that it stays free of divergence (2.3). Each flux comes from a Riemann problem at a face (2.5), between two states that a reconstruction builds from the averages of the neighbouring cells (2.4). Athena advances in time with the CTU or the VL integrator, Athena++ with the VL2 integrator or Runge–Kutta methods by the method of lines (2.6).

### 2.1 The equations and the finite-volume discretisation

#### 2.1.1 The equations of ideal MHD

Athena solves the equations of ideal MHD in conservative form ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §2, eqs. 1 to 4). They read $\partial \rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0$ for the mass, $\partial (\rho \mathbf{v})/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v} \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{B} \mathbf{B} + \mathsf{P}^*) = 0$ for the momentum, $\partial E/\partial t + \nabla \cdot [(E + P^*) \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{B} (\mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{v})] = 0$ for the total energy and $\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t - \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) = 0$ for the field. Here $\mathsf{P}^*$ is the diagonal tensor whose components are the total pressure $P^* = P + B^2/2$, with $P$ the pressure of the gas, and the total energy density is $E = P/(\gamma - 1) + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$ (eq. 5). The units make the magnetic permeability $\mu = 1$. This energy assumes an ideal gas, $P = (\gamma - 1) e$, where $\gamma$ is the adiabatic index and $e$ the internal energy density. For a barotropic gas, $P = P(\rho)$, such as an isothermal one with $P = C^2 \rho$ and $C$ the isothermal sound speed, the energy equation is dropped, and the total energy is no longer conserved. Athena thus solves four systems, hydrodynamics or MHD with an ideal or a barotropic gas, which differ in number and form but share the numerical methods. A tabulated EOS would fit the same methods.

The conserved variables $\mathbf{U} = (\rho, M_x, M_y, M_z, E, B_x, B_y, B_z)$, with the momentum density $\mathbf{M} = \rho \mathbf{v}$, and the primitive variables $\mathbf{W} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, P, B_x, B_y, B_z)$ describe adiabatic MHD in Cartesian coordinates (eq. 6). The system becomes $\partial \mathbf{U}/\partial t + \partial \mathbf{F}/\partial x + \partial \mathbf{G}/\partial y + \partial \mathbf{H}/\partial z = 0$ (eq. 7). The flux in $x$ is $\mathbf{F} = (\rho v_x, \rho v_x^2 + P + B^2/2 - B_x^2, \rho v_x v_y - B_x B_y, \rho v_x v_z - B_x B_z, (E + P^*) v_x - (\mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{v}) B_x, 0, B_y v_x - B_x v_y, B_z v_x - B_x v_z)$ (eq. 8), and $\mathbf{G}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ follow by permuting the directions (eqs. 9, 10), each with a zero for the field component along its own direction. In hydrodynamics, or for a barotropic gas, the corresponding components are dropped. The last three components are the induction equation written for Cartesian coordinates, but the field is evolved by a quite different method (2.3). In curvilinear coordinates the fluxes need the scale factors of the metric, or the curvature of the grid enters as source terms, or both (2.8).

#### 2.1.2 Non-ideal MHD in Athena++

Athena++ solves the same equations with terms for non-ideal MHD: isotropic viscosity, thermal conduction, Ohmic resistivity and ambipolar diffusion in the limit of strong coupling ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.1, eqs. 6a to 6d). The viscous stress tensor is $\Pi_{ij} = \rho \nu (\partial v_i/\partial x_j + \partial v_j/\partial x_i - \tfrac{2}{3} \delta_{ij} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v})$ (eq. 7), with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$. The heat flux is $\mathbf{Q} = -\kappa \nabla T$, with the thermal conductivity $\kappa$ and the temperature $T$, and $\mathbf{J} = \nabla \times \mathbf{B}$ is the current density. The momentum equation subtracts $\Pi$ from the flux of momentum. The energy equation adds to the flux of energy $-\Pi \cdot \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{Q}$, the Poynting flux of resistivity, $\eta \mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B}$, and that of ambipolar diffusion, $(\eta_{\mathrm{AD}}/|\mathbf{B}|^2) \{\mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B})\} \times \mathbf{B}$. The induction equation becomes $\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t - \nabla \times [\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} - \eta \mathbf{J} - (\eta_{\mathrm{AD}}/|\mathbf{B}|^2) \mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B})] = 0$. The total energy density is $E = e + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$ for any EOS (eq. 8). The coefficients $\nu$, $\kappa$ and $\eta$ are constant by default, but may be made functions of position and of the variables. The ambipolar coefficient $\eta_{\mathrm{AD}}$ has no default, as it depends on the rates of ionisation, recombination and collisions in the plasma, and the user supplies a function for it (2.11).

#### 2.1.3 The finite-volume discretisation

Athena integrates the equations on a regular Cartesian grid in three dimensions ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §3). The domain of size $(L_x, L_y, L_z)$ is divided into $(N_x, N_y, N_z)$ cells, and the cell $(i, j, k)$ is centred at $(x_i, y_j, z_k)$. The paper describes uniform cells of size $\delta x = L_x/N_x$, $\delta y = L_y/N_y$ and $\delta z = L_z/N_z$, and notes that the methods extend easily to nonuniform grids. Time advances from $t_0$ to $t_f$ in steps $\delta t^n = t^{n+1} - t^n$ that may vary, written $\delta t$ below. Methods built on the integral form of the equations suit flows with shocks and discontinuities ([LeVeque](#leveque-2002) 2002). Integrating eq. 7 over a cell and over a step and applying the divergence theorem gives $\mathbf{U}^{n+1}_{i,j,k} = \mathbf{U}^n_{i,j,k} - (\delta t/\delta x)(\mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2,j,k} - \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2,j,k}) - (\delta t/\delta y)(\mathbf{G}^{n+1/2}_{i,j+1/2,k} - \mathbf{G}^{n+1/2}_{i,j-1/2,k}) - (\delta t/\delta z)(\mathbf{H}^{n+1/2}_{i,j,k+1/2} - \mathbf{H}^{n+1/2}_{i,j,k-1/2})$ (§3.1, eq. 11). Here $\mathbf{U}^n_{i,j,k}$ is the average of $\mathbf{U}$ over the cell at $t^n$ (eq. 12), and each flux is the average of $\mathbf{F}$, $\mathbf{G}$ or $\mathbf{H}$ over a face and over the step (eqs. 13 to 15). Half-integer subscripts mark the faces between cells, so that $x_{i-1/2}$ lies between the cells centred at $x_{i-1}$ and $x_i$. The half-integer superscript marks an average over time, not a value at $t^{n+1/2}$. The update is exact, and a method lies wholly in how it approximates the averaged fluxes. Orders above the second are possible, and were explored for MHD by [Londrillo and Del Zanna](#londrillo-del-zanna-2000) (2000) and with WENO schemes ([Balsara and Shu](#balsara-shu-2000) 2000), but they improve the accuracy mainly in smooth flow, not at shocks and discontinuities, and are harder to combine with AMR. On a set of hydrodynamic tests in one dimension, [Greenough and Rider](#greenough-rider-2003) (2003) found a Godunov scheme of second order more accurate for its cost than a WENO scheme of fifth order. Athena therefore keeps to second order in space and time.

Athena++ discretises the equations of mass, momentum and energy (eqs. 6a to 6c) by finite volumes, storing the averages of the conserved variables at the volume centres of cells ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.1). In curvilinear coordinates the volume centre differs from the geometric centre, which matters for methods above second order ([Blondin and Lufkin](#blondin-lufkin-1993) 1993; 2.8). The induction equation (eq. 6d) is discretised by the upwind CT of [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, [2008](#gardiner-stone-2008)), with the components of the field as averages over the faces of cells (2.3). An EOS supplies the pressure and the temperature from the conserved variables. Any EOS that gives $P(\rho, e)$ and the square of the sound speed $a^2(\rho, P)$, analytically or by interpolation in a table, can be used, as [Coleman](#coleman-2020) (2020) describes and tests after [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019) verify (2.2).

#### 2.1.4 The variables in the code

The class `Hydro` holds the variables of hydrodynamics on a mesh block (`src/hydro/hydro.hpp`, `Hydro`). The conserved variables `u` and the primitive variables `w` are arrays of four dimensions, indexed as `u(n,k,j,i)` by the variable $n$ and the cell, over the whole block with its ghost cells (3.2). The indices of the variables are `IDN` for the density, `IM1` to `IM3` for the momentum and `IEN` for the total energy, and in the primitive variables `IVX` to `IVZ` for the velocity and `IPR` for the pressure, which share the values 1 to 4 (`src/athena.hpp`). Their number `NHYDRO` is set when the code is configured: 5 for an adiabatic or general EOS and 4 for an isothermal one, which has no energy (`configure.py`). The states of a one-dimensional Riemann problem carry the two transverse components of the field after the hydrodynamic variables, at the indices `IBY` and `IBZ`, equal to `NHYDRO` and `NHYDRO + 1`. `NWAVE` counts the variables of such a state: 5 or 4 in hydrodynamics and 7 or 6 in MHD, for an adiabatic or isothermal gas. Further registers serve the time integrators: `u1` and `w1` always, `u2` for the integrator `ssprk5_4` or for super time-stepping, and `u0` and `fl_div` for super time-stepping of the type RKL2 (2.6, 2.11). The fluxes through the faces are kept in `flux[3]`. Its array in the direction $x_1$ has one more entry along $i$, and those in $x_2$ and $x_3$ one more along $j$ or $k$, allocated only when the mesh extends in their directions. With mesh refinement, `coarse_cons_` and `coarse_prim_` hold variables on the coarser grid that refinement uses (3.4), and the fourth-order method keeps approximations at cell centres in `u_cc` and `w_cc` (2.7). The class also holds the boundary variable of hydrodynamics, its source terms and its diffusion (`HydroBoundaryVariable`, `HydroSourceTerms`, `HydroDiffusion`), and its constructor registers the conserved variables for refinement and communication (`src/hydro/hydro.cpp`, `Hydro::Hydro`). The magnetic field is held by the class `Field`, as a structure `FaceField` of three arrays on the faces, each with one more entry along its normal, and as values at cell centres in `bcc` (`src/athena.hpp`, `FaceField`; 2.3). The integrator is chosen at run time by the parameter `integrator` of the block `time`, with VL2 as default (2.6).

### 2.2 Equations of state

#### 2.2.1 Ideal and isothermal gases

Athena++ chooses its EOS when the code is configured: adiabatic, isothermal or general, each with its own files for hydrodynamics and MHD (`configure.py`). The class `EquationOfState` converts between conserved and primitive variables, computes sound speeds and applies floors (`src/eos/eos.hpp`, `EquationOfState`). For an adiabatic gas, the adiabatic index $\gamma$ is read from the parameter `gamma` of the block `hydro`, which must be given (`src/eos/adiabatic_hydro.cpp`, `EquationOfState::EquationOfState`). The conversion to primitive variables first raises the conserved density to the floor `dfloor` where it is lower, without changing momentum or energy, then divides the momentum by the density and computes $P = (\gamma - 1)(E - \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2)$ (`EquationOfState::ConservedToPrimitive`). Where this pressure falls below the floor `pfloor`, it is set to the floor and the total energy is raised to match, so that the floors add mass and energy. In MHD the magnetic energy $B^2/2$ is subtracted as well, from the field at cell centres, which the conversion first computes from the field on the faces (`src/eos/adiabatic_mhd.cpp`, `EquationOfState::ConservedToPrimitive`; 2.3). Both floors default to $\sqrt{1024\, F}$, with $F$ the smallest normal number in single precision, which gives about $3.5 \times 10^{-18}$ in code units, and so does the floor `sfloor` of the passive scalars. The sound speed is $a = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$. The fast magnetosonic speed along $x$ is computed as $c_f^2 = [\gamma P + B^2 + \sqrt{(B^2 - \gamma P)^2 + 4 \gamma P B_\perp^2}]/(2\rho)$, with $B_\perp$ the transverse field (`EquationOfState::FastMagnetosonicSpeed`). This equals the usual form, with $(\gamma P + B^2)^2 - 4 \gamma P B_x^2$ under the root, but needs no difference of large terms. Floors are also applied to the reconstructed states at the faces, and to the primitive and conserved variables together in the fourth-order method (`EquationOfState::ApplyPrimitiveFloors`, `EquationOfState::ApplyPrimitiveConservedFloors`). For an isothermal gas the sound speed `iso_sound_speed` must be given. There is then no energy, only the density floor applies, and $c_s^2 \rho$ takes the place of $\gamma P$ in the fast speed (`src/eos/isothermal_hydro.cpp`, `src/eos/isothermal_mhd.cpp`).

#### 2.2.2 The general EOS

The ideal gas is a common assumption in astrophysical fluid dynamics but often an invalid one, and a realistic EOS matters, for instance, in supernovae, kilonovae and ionisation instabilities of accretion discs, and in models of the transients that surveys find ([Coleman](#coleman-2020) 2020, §1). A finite-difference code such as ZEUS ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, [b](#stone-norman-1992b)) takes a general EOS easily, but a Godunov code must solve its Riemann problems with the same EOS. [Colella and Glaz](#colella-glaz-1985) (1985) made this practical, and codes such as FLASH ([Fryxell et al.](#fryxell-et-al-2000) 2000), CASTRO ([Almgren et al.](#almgren-et-al-2010) 2010) and Chimera ([Bruenn et al.](#bruenn-et-al-2018) 2018) followed. Coleman extends Athena++ to non-relativistic hydrodynamics and MHD with a general EOS, not to its relativistic modules. He claims for it the first public MHD code whose general EOS has been verified by a suite of tests with a non-trivial EOS: FLASH has published no such test against a known solution, and CASTRO, which ran similar tests ([Zingale and Katz](#zingale-katz-2015) 2015), has no magnetic field. The tests use an EOS of hydrogen with an ionisation transition and a Helmholtz EOS of electrons and positrons (abstract).

The Riemann problem is recalled for the Euler equations in one dimension (§2, eqs. 1 to 3), closed by an EOS $p(\rho, e)$ or $e(\rho, p)$ with $(\partial p/\partial \rho)_e > 0$ and $(\partial p/\partial e)_\rho > 0$ in the frame where the fluid is at rest (eqs. 4, 5). These conditions suffice for a unique solution, without being necessary ([Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) 2019). Two constant states separated at $x = 0$ evolve into a fan of four constant states that depends on $x/t$ alone, separated by three waves: rarefactions or shocks on the outside and a contact discontinuity in the middle (eqs. 6, 7; Fig. 1). [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019) solve the problem exactly to arbitrary precision for a general EOS, at a cost too high for simulations, which therefore use approximate solvers. They also show that the generalisation needs the exact adiabatic sound speed, $a^2 = (\partial p/\partial \rho)_s$ at constant specific entropy (eq. 8).

With diffusion neglected, the equations are those of ideal MHD with $E = e + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$ (§3, eqs. 9 to 12). All calculations of the paper use piecewise linear reconstruction of the primitive variables and the VL2 integrator, and reconstruction in characteristic variables does not support a general EOS. An EOS fits the framework if it satisfies eqs. 4 and 5, with well-behaved derivatives, and supplies three functions: $p(\rho, e)$, $e(\rho, p)$ and $a^2(\rho, p)$ (§3.1, eqs. 13 to 15). The first converts conserved variables to primitive ones, after the kinetic and magnetic energies are subtracted from $E$, and the second converts back. The sound speed serves the CFL condition and the Riemann solvers, for their characteristic speeds and fluxes. Even a piecewise EOS will do. The generalisation replaces $p = (\gamma - 1) e$, $e = p/(\gamma - 1)$ and $a^2 = \gamma p/\rho$ by these functions (eqs. 16 to 18), and Coleman replaced every occurrence of $\gamma$ in Athena++ in this way. That the replacements keep all the characteristic speeds of the Riemann problem is not obvious, but follows from Appendix B of [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019). The one further change concerns the estimate of the wave speeds in the Riemann solvers (2.5).

#### 2.2.3 Tables and the EOS of hydrogen

The three functions may be analytic or interpolated in tables (§3.2). The table stores $\log(p/e)$ as a function of $\log \rho$ and $\log e + \eta \log \rho$, and $\log(e/p)$ and $\log \Gamma_1$ as functions of $\log \rho$ and $\log p + \eta \log \rho$, with $\eta$ a constant chosen by the user and $\Gamma_1 = (\partial \ln p/\partial \ln \rho)_s = \rho a^2/p$ the generalised adiabatic index (eqs. 19 to 22). All three quantities are dimensionless, and all depend on $\log \rho$ and on $\log \epsilon$, with $\epsilon$ either $e \rho^\eta$ or $p \rho^\eta$. A regular grid in $\log \rho$ and $\log \epsilon$ discretises them (eqs. 23, 24), with the same number of points for $e$ and $p$, which may differ by a constant factor $c_0$, one by default (eq. 25). Bilinear interpolation keeps monotonicity between the points, and off the table the values are extrapolated linearly, which floors on $p$, $e$ and $\rho$ and a large enough table should prevent. Another EOS beyond the table would be possible but is not implemented. For an ideal gas the three quantities are constants, $p/e = \gamma - 1$, $e/p = 1/(\gamma - 1)$ and $\Gamma_1 = \gamma$ (eqs. 26 to 28), so that a table of two points in each dimension reproduces the ideal gas to machine precision, as was verified.

In the code a general EOS is configured with `--eos=general/` and the name of its implementation, which sets `GENERAL_EOS` and, for a table, `EOS_TABLE_ENABLED` (`configure.py`). Each implementation supplies the three functions as `PresFromRhoEg`, `EgasFromRhoP` and `AsqFromRhoP` (`src/eos/general/`). `src/eos/general/ideal.cpp` implements the ideal gas within the framework, mainly for debugging, and `src/eos/general/noop.cpp` makes the three functions stop the code when no general EOS is configured. `src/eos/general/eos_table.cpp` interpolates in the table of the mesh, held by the class `EosTable` and read once by the mesh (`src/utils/eos_table_class.cpp`, `EosTable`; `src/mesh/mesh.cpp`, `Mesh::Mesh`). The table holds, in this order, $p/e$, $e/p$ and $\rho a^2/p$, each as a function of $\log \rho$ and of the logarithm of the energy or pressure plus `dens_pow` times $\log \rho$. With `dens_pow` = −1 by default, the second coordinate is the specific energy or pressure, the case $\eta = -1$ of eqs. 19 to 21. Ratios stored for each quantity scale the second coordinate, as $c_0$ does in eq. 25, and the parameters `eos_rho_unit` and `eos_egas_unit` convert between code units and those of the table. The table is read from a binary, HDF5 or text file named by `eos_file_name`, whose type follows from its extension unless `eos_file_type` sets it (`EosTable::EosTable`). The interpolation is bilinear, with the indices clamped to the edges of the table, so that values off the table are extrapolated linearly (`src/utils/interp_table.cpp`, `InterpTable2D::interpolate`). For a general EOS the conversion to primitive variables subtracts the kinetic energy, and in MHD the magnetic energy, from $E$, raises the internal energy to the floor `efloor` where it is lower, adjusting the total energy, and calls `PresFromRhoEg` (`src/eos/general/general_hydro.cpp`, `src/eos/general/general_mhd.cpp`, `EquationOfState::ConservedToPrimitive`). The energy floor defaults to `pfloor` divided by $\gamma - 1$, with $\gamma = 2$ unless it is given. The sound speed and the fast speed use `AsqFromRhoP`, and `GetGamma` stops the code, as $\gamma$ has no meaning for a general EOS. The code refuses a general EOS with reconstruction in characteristic variables, and warns that combined with reconstruction above second order it has documented bugs in some cases (`src/reconstruct/reconstruction.cpp`, `Reconstruction::Reconstruction`).

The simple EOS of hydrogen of the tests contains electrons, neutral hydrogen and protons in local thermal equilibrium, with the one reaction $\mathrm{H}^0 \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}^+ + e^-$ (App. B, eq. B18). The partition functions per volume factor into bound states, nuclear spin, the spin of electrons, translation and excitation (eq. B19). The bound states are set to one, a small error for neutral hydrogen in an EOS meant as a proof of concept (eq. B20). The translational factor is $(2 \pi m_i k T/h^2)^{3/2} = n_q (m_i/m_e)^{3/2} (T/T_{\mathrm{ion}})^{3/2}$, with $n_q = (2 \pi m_e k T_{\mathrm{ion}}/h^2)^{3/2} = 1.514892 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and the ionisation temperature of hydrogen $T_{\mathrm{ion}} = \alpha^2 m_e c^2/2k = 157\,888$ K (eqs. B21 to B23). The spin factors of electrons and neutral hydrogen and the nuclear spin are 2, and protons carry the excitation factor $\exp(-T_{\mathrm{ion}}/T)$ (eqs. B24 to B27). The Saha equation, with conservation of nuclei and charge neutrality (eqs. B28 to B30), $n_{\mathrm{nuc}} = \rho/m_p$ and the mass of the electrons neglected, gives the ionisation fraction $x = 2/[1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 \tilde\rho\, e^{1/\tilde T} \tilde T^{-3/2}}]$, with $\tilde\rho = \rho/(m_p n_q)$ and $\tilde T = T/T_{\mathrm{ion}}$ (eqs. B31, B32). The pressure is $p = n_q k T_{\mathrm{ion}} \tilde p$ with $\tilde p = \tilde\rho \tilde T (1 + x)$, and the specific internal energy $\epsilon = (k T_{\mathrm{ion}}/m_p)(x + \tfrac{3}{2} \tilde p/\tilde\rho)$ (eqs. B33, B34). Closed forms give $\Gamma_1$ and $\partial x/\partial \tilde T$, and with them $a^2 = \Gamma_1 p/\rho$ (eqs. B35 to B38). The code works in these dimensionless units, computing $x$, $p$, $e = \rho \epsilon$ and $a^2$ from $\tilde\rho$ and $\tilde T$, and inverts $p$ or $e$ for $\tilde T$ by the Brent–Dekker method to the precision `InversionPrecision`, $10^{-12}$ by default (`src/eos/general/hydrogen.cpp`). It writes $\Gamma_1$ in another closed form. **[GK]** The two forms of $\Gamma_1$, eq. B36 and that of the code, agree to eight digits across the transition and match the derivative $\partial \ln p/\partial \ln \rho$ at constant entropy, computed numerically from eqs. B33 and B34 with $\mathrm{d}\epsilon = (p/\rho^2)\, \mathrm{d}\rho$. Both tend to 5/3 for a fully neutral or fully ionised gas. The values of $n_q$ and $T_{\mathrm{ion}}$ in eqs. B22 and B23 follow from the physical constants, and eqs. B31 and B37 follow from eqs. B28 to B30.

#### 2.2.4 Tests of general equations of state

Few tests of hydrodynamics or MHD with a nontrivial EOS existed, most without astrophysical contexts in mind ([Coleman](#coleman-2020) 2020, §4; [Hu, Adams and Iaccarino](#hu-et-al-2009) 2009; [Serna and Marquina](#serna-marquina-2014) 2014). Only [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019) and [Zingale and Katz](#zingale-katz-2015) (2015) computed exact solutions of hydrodynamic tests with such an EOS for astrophysics, none of the works published its solutions, and some, such as [Serna and Marquina](#serna-marquina-2014) (2014), computed none. The three variants of their tests DG1 gave comparable results. The tests of [Hu, Adams and Iaccarino](#hu-et-al-2009) (2009) and of [Zingale and Katz](#zingale-katz-2015) (2015) were not run, as they would need functions of the EOS with further arguments, such as passive scalars. Since the work grew alongside that of [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019), the tests were developed independently, on an analytic EOS, which [Zingale and Katz](#zingale-katz-2015) (2015) did not use.

The hydrogen EOS of 2.2.3 tests whether the code evolves a fluid accurately through transitions of ionisation, as models of ionisation instabilities in accretion discs need (§4.1; [Hirose et al.](#hirose-et-al-2014) 2014; [Coleman et al.](#coleman-et-al-2018) 2018). An exact Riemann solver for a general EOS in arbitrary precision, based on that of [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019), gives the solutions, and tests 1 to 7 were run with the public version 19.0 of Athena++. Each call of the EOS first finds the temperature by the method of Brent and Dekker, to one part in $10^{12}$ and between bounds for complete and for no ionisation, and then computes the quantity analytically, which for tests is better than a table, whose resolution would affect the convergence. The EOS defines the units of Table 1: the mass of the proton, the temperature $T_{\mathrm{ion}}$ and the number density $n_q$ of 2.2.3, the density $m_p n_q = 0.253384\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, the pressure $n_q k T_{\mathrm{ion}}$, the speed $(kT_{\mathrm{ion}}/m_p)^{1/2}$, the length $n_q^{-1/3}$ and the field $P_u^{1/2}$, with $P_u$ the unit of pressure. Riemann problems are free of scale, so only the ratio $\Delta t/\Delta x$ of the duration to the size of the domain is given. A first, trivial test advected a sinusoidal linear wave of amplitude $10^{-6}$ at $\rho = p = 1$, where $\Gamma_1 = 1.615$, set up as an eigenmode that travels at the sound speed, and it returned to its initial position after one crossing time. Six Riemann problems on the domain $\pm\Delta x/2$, run for $\Delta t$, test the nonlinear hydrodynamics (Table 2). Their states are given by density, speed and temperature, from which eq. B33 gives the pressures, listed in App. C. Test 1 has $(\rho, v, T) = (10^{-7}, 0, 0.15)$ on the left and $(1.25 \times 10^{-8}, 0, 0.062)$ on the right with $\Delta t/\Delta x = 0.25$, and test 2 $(4 \times 10^{-6}, 0, 0.12)$ and $(4 \times 10^{-8}, 0, 0.019)$ with 0.3, two shock tubes like that of [Sod](#sod-1978) (1978) with a shock moving to the right. Tests 3 and 4, two asymmetric double shocks, have supersonic inflow from both sides, $(8 \times 10^{-7}, 1.1, 0.006)$ against $(4 \times 10^{-7}, -1.7, 0.006)$ and $(5 \times 10^{-7}, 1.5, 0.006)$ against $(4 \times 10^{-7}, -1.8, 0.006)$, both with $\Delta t/\Delta x = 1.5$. Tests 5 and 6 are double rarefactions, symmetric with $(8 \times 10^{-5}, \mp 0.8, 0.095)$ on the two sides and asymmetric with $(6 \times 10^{-5}, -0.5, 0.095)$ and $(8 \times 10^{-5}, 0.9, 0.095)$, both with $\Delta t/\Delta x = 0.25$. All six vary the ionisation fraction $x$ and $\Gamma_1$ strongly. The errors are the norms $L_1(f) = N^{-1}\sum_i |\Delta f_i|$ and $L_2(f) = (N^{-1}\sum_i \Delta f_i^2)^{1/2}$ of the difference $\Delta f$ between the solution of Athena++ and the exact one in each of the $N$ cells (eqs. 30, 31). With discontinuities the convergence should be linear, although contacts converge sublinearly in the limit ([Banks, Aslam and Rider](#banks-et-al-2008) 2008), which these resolutions do not reach. Each test runs with $N = 64$, 128, 256, 512, 1024 and 2048 cells and a Courant number of 0.4.

For MHD no published test with an exact solution and a nontrivial EOS existed, and the MHD tests of [Serna and Marquina](#serna-marquina-2014) (2014) with the EOS of van der Waals compare with no known solution (§4.2). Their three tests DG1 gave about the same density on visual comparison. Solving the MHD Riemann problem in general with a general EOS lies beyond the work, so test 7 is restricted to problems in which all seven waves are discontinuities ([Ryu and Jones](#ryu-jones-1995) 1995). The jump conditions across each discontinuity, written with its mass flux $F_i$ (eqs. 32 to 38), then give 49 equations for 49 unknowns: the seven variables of the six intermediate states and the seven mass fluxes. The outer states are those of test 2a of [Ryu and Jones](#ryu-jones-1995) (1995), $(\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, B_y, B_z, p) = (1.08, 1.2, 0.01, 0.5, 3.6/\sqrt{4\pi}, 2/\sqrt{4\pi}, 0.95)$ on the left and $(1, 0, 0, 0, 4/\sqrt{4\pi}, 2/\sqrt{4\pi}, 1)$ on the right, with $B_x = 2/\sqrt{4\pi}$ and $\Delta t/\Delta x = 0.2$, but with the hydrogen EOS and units tuned for a large variation of $\Gamma_1$: density in $10^{-7}\rho_u$, speed in $0.2^{1/2}v_u$, field in $(2 \times 10^{-8})^{1/2}B_u$ and pressure in $2 \times 10^{-8}P_u$ (Table 3). The ideal solution of [Ryu and Jones](#ryu-jones-1995) (1995) served as the first guess for solving the 49 equations numerically (Table 13).

Tests 8 and 9 are periodic linear waves in one dimension, of hydrodynamics and of MHD, run for exactly one crossing time and compared with their initial state (§4.3). The background has $\rho_0 = 10^{-7}\rho_u$, $T_0 = 0.0435\,T_u$, within 1% of the temperature at which $|\partial\Gamma_1/\partial T|$ peaks at $\rho_0$, and $\mathbf{v}_0 = 0$ (eqs. 39 to 41). Each conserved variable carries a sine wave $f(x) = f_0 + \delta f\sin(2\pi x/\Delta x)$ over the domain of size $\Delta x$, with $\delta\rho = 10^{-6}\rho_0$ (eqs. 42, 43). In test 8 the amplitudes follow the right eigenvector of a sound wave moving to $-x$, and in test 9 that of a fast wave moving to $-x$, after the eigenvectors of [Serna and Marquina](#serna-marquina-2014) (2014), in the field $\mathbf{B}_0 = p_0^{1/2}(1, \sqrt{2}, 1/2)$ with constant $B_x$ (eq. 44). Without discontinuities the convergence should be quadratic, and the resolutions and the Courant number are those above.

Tests 10 and 11 use the Helmholtz EOS of [Timmes and Swesty](#timmes-swesty-2000) (2000), common for stellar interiors and supernovae, with which only [Zingale and Katz](#zingale-katz-2015) (2015) had shown convergence to a known solution (§4.4). It is implemented in density and temperature as [Timmes and Swesty](#timmes-swesty-2000) (2000) give it, without the tables of 2.2.3 although it has a tabular part. Newton–Raphson, secant and bisection steps find the temperature in each call, starting from the last temperature found. The tests stay at non-relativistic sound speeds, since the framework cannot treat relativity, and where degeneracy does not dominate the pressure, since the pressure can then hardly be inverted for the temperature. Along adiabats in this regime $\Gamma_1$ hardly varies, which excludes double rarefactions and leaves a test like Sod's and a double shock (Table 4). In cgs units, with speeds in $10^8\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$, pressures in $10^{16}\ \mathrm{dyn\,cm^{-2}}$ and $\Delta t/\Delta x$ in $10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s\,cm^{-1}}$, test 10 has $(\rho, v, p) = (1.0, 0, 0.7)$ against $(0.125, 0, 0.015)$ with $\Delta t/\Delta x = 0.25$, and test 11 $(0.8, 1.1, 0.05)$ against $(0.4, -1.7, 0.03)$ with 0.6, both with $Z = 1$ in the notation of [Timmes and Swesty](#timmes-swesty-2000) (2000) and a Courant number of 0.4. Only two such tests were run, since the Helmholtz EOS varies $\Gamma_1$ less than the hydrogen EOS and so tests the code less.

All tests except the double shocks converge close to linearly, the best possible rate (§5.1). Between $N = 64$ and $N = 2048$, the $L_1$ error of the density falls by factors of about 21 and 27 in tests 1 and 2, 4.1 and 2.5 in tests 3 and 4, and 17 and 19 in tests 5 and 6, against 32 for linear convergence (App. C). In tests 1 and 2 the contact and the shock spread over several cells but follow the exact discontinuities well at high resolution, and $v_x$ overshoots where the rarefaction meets the state behind it, a small notch at $N = 2048$, as do density and pressure in test 1. Tests 3 and 4 converge worst, and worse at high resolution, the errors of test 3 staying nearly constant for $512 \le N \le 2048$. The density and pressure oscillate slightly about the exact solution, most visibly at $N = 64$, and at $N = 2048$ ring like the Gibbs phenomenon, above all at the shock on the right. [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) (2019) noted similar problems, and analogous tests with an ideal gas behave alike. The lack of convergence may reflect a limit of finite volumes without front tracking. A cell that straddles a shock passes averages of density and pressure to the EOS, and where $\Gamma_1$ is not monotonic this gives a $\Gamma_1$ below the values on either side, by about the same amount at $N = 64$ and $N = 2048$. The contact also diffuses visibly, which may bring in its sublinear convergence. Even at $N = 64$, however, both tests approximate the exact solution well, which for tests designed as a worst case is reassuring. The tests are sensitive to the estimate of the wave speeds, and for HLLC the PVRS performed markedly better than the Roe average (2.5.3). Tests 5 and 6 converge close to linearly with small errors. The density shows a small spike at the centre, common in numerical solutions of double rarefactions ([Toro](#toro-1999) 1999; [Chen et al.](#chen-et-al-2019) 2019), and the ionisation fraction and $\Gamma_1$ deviate strongly, especially at $N = 64$, because the test sits at the edge of ionisation, where both react sensitively to pressure and density, while the other variables stay close to the exact solution. For the shock tubes 1 and 2 and the double rarefactions 5 and 6, no estimate of the wave speeds changed the results of HLLE, nor of HLLC, but the errors of HLLE exceeded those of HLLC by some tens of per cent. In tests 3 and 4 HLLE converged to the correct solution with none of the estimates tried, although such an estimate may exist, while with the Roe average it does converge in the analogous tests with an ideal gas.

Test 7 converges nearly linearly in all variables, its density error falling by a factor of about 21 from $N = 64$ to $N = 2048$, and approximates the exact solution well (§5.2). Tests 1 to 6 run as MHD with $\mathbf{B} = 0$ and HLLD agree with those of HLLC, except tests 3 and 4, which converge slightly worse with errors up to three times larger. The cause is the estimate of the wave speeds: HLLC with the estimate of HLLD gives similar results, and the PVRS does not extend readily to MHD, so that estimates for HLLD need further work. The linear waves converge nearly quadratically, the density error falling by factors of about 1,200 and 1,050 from $N = 64$ to $N = 2048$ against 1,024 for quadratic convergence (§5.3). Tests 10 and 11 converge well and reproduce the exact solution, test 10 with no significant deviation at $N = 2048$ and close even at $N = 64$, its density error falling by a factor of about 30 (§5.4). Test 11, whose error falls by about 20, resembles tests 3 and 4, with small oscillations at $N = 64$ and ringing at both shocks at $N = 2048$, but it is more accurate and converges better than they do. The hydrogen EOS thus tests the code harder, probably because it varies $\Gamma_1$ faster and more strongly, so that discontinuities can span regions where $\Gamma_1$ is not monotonic, and the Helmholtz EOS proves well behaved for Godunov codes. The tables of App. C list the errors of all tests at all resolutions and the constant states of each exact solution, with their density, pressure, speed, temperature and bounding wave speeds, and supplementary data give 101 points across each rarefaction.

[Coleman](#coleman-2020) (2020, §6) concludes that the general EOS required changes to the calls of the EOS and to HLLC and HLLD, while no change to HLLE was found under which it converged in tests 3 and 4. The new tests with the hydrogen and Helmholtz EOS show that the code mostly recovers the exact solutions and converges as expected. The double shocks of the hydrogen EOS converge less well but deviate little, and they may show a limit of finite volumes, as the averaged states at shocks yield too low a $\Gamma_1$. The estimate of the wave speeds affects convergence and accuracy substantially, above all in tests 3 and 4, so estimates for HLLD deserve further study. The tests are freely available and the framework is part of the public version of Athena++, so as to serve research in which a realistic EOS matters.

In Athena++, the problem generator `eos_test` prints diagnostics of the EOS rather than evolving a flow (`src/pgen/eos_test.cpp`). It reports the kind of EOS and, for a table, its shape, limits, units and ratios, prints the table on request (`print_table`, optionally exponentiated with `exponentiate_table`), and with `eos_loop` reads densities and internal energies from the input stream. For each pair it returns the pressure, the squared sound speed and the relative error of the internal energy after a conversion to primitive variables and back, and it refuses this loop for an isothermal EOS. The regression test `eos_riemann` runs tests 1 to 6 with HLLC and 256 cells, with VL2, PLM, a Courant number of 0.4 and outflow boundaries on $[-0.5, 0.5]$, and compares density, pressure and velocity with the exact solution of the hydrogen EOS against thresholds for the $L_1$ difference, such as $5.1 \times 10^{-10}$, $6.5 \times 10^{-11}$ and 0.0039 for test 1 (`tst/regression/scripts/tests/eos/eos_riemann.py`; `inputs/hydro/athinput.sod_general_H`). The test `eos_mhd` runs the same six problems as MHD, test 1 again with $B_x = 10^{-5}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$, and test 7 with the hydrogen EOS, against the eight states and seven wave speeds of its exact solution. Its thresholds for tests 3 and 4 and for the last field are raised by factors of 3, 2 and 2, with a note that the test should work without them (`tst/regression/scripts/tests/eos/eos_mhd.py`). The test `eos_comparison` runs the shock tube of Sod for $\gamma = 1.1$, 1.4 and 5/3 with the adiabatic EOS as reference, which the general ideal EOS and a binary table of the ideal gas must reproduce exactly and an ASCII table to a relative difference of $10^{-6}$. ### 2.3 Constrained transport

#### 2.3.1 Keeping the field free of divergence

Godunov methods for MHD need two extensions of the hydrodynamic algorithm: a Riemann solver for MHD, and a way to keep the evolved field free of divergence, the harder of the two ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) 2005, §1). [Brackbill and Barnes](#brackbill-barnes-1980) (1980) first noted that where $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} \ne 0$ the Lorentz force gains a component along $\mathbf{B}$, which can give wrong dynamics, and [Tóth](#toth-2000) (2000) showed that MHD shocks can then have wrong jump conditions. Three approaches were in use. A Hodge projection cleans the field of its divergence after each step ([Balsara and Kim](#balsara-kim-2003) 2003; [Crockett et al.](#crockett-et-al-2005) 2005; [Tóth](#toth-2000) 2000). An evolution equation for the divergence, added to the system, keeps its errors from accumulating in one place, as in the eight-wave scheme ([Powell et al.](#powell-et-al-1999) 1999) and the GLM-MHD scheme ([Dedner et al.](#dedner-et-al-2002) 2002). Difference equations for the field can instead conserve the magnetic flux exactly, the approach that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) called constrained transport (CT). CT had served other MHD algorithms well ([Clarke](#clarke-1996) 1996; [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b), and the authors adopt it. Its most compact form uses area averages of the field on the faces of cells rather than volume averages at their centres, since a CT built on fields at cell centres ([Tóth](#toth-2000) 2000) averages over a wider stencil than the fluxes use. The staggered grid that CT needs is often seen as a drawback, but it reflects that the quantity MHD conserves is the magnetic flux, an area average. CT conserves that flux, and so keeps the divergence zero in an integral sense, on the scale of single cells.

The paper shows that finite volumes for the other variables and CT for the field can be made consistent if their fluxes obey simple relations (2.3.3; see also [Londrillo and Del Zanna](#londrillo-del-zanna-2004) 2004). These relations lead to algorithms for the electric fields at the corners of cells that reduce exactly to the solver in one dimension for plane-parallel flows aligned with the grid, unlike earlier Godunov schemes with CT ([Balsara and Spicer](#balsara-spicer-1999) 1999; [Dai and Woodward](#dai-woodward-1998) 1998; [Ryu et al.](#ryu-et-al-1998) 1998; 2.3.5). Combined with PPM ([Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) 1984) and the Roe solver for MHD ([Cargo and Gallice](#cargo-gallice-1997) 1997), the reconstruction must include multidimensional terms of the induction equation. Methods split by direction cannot keep the constraint between their updates in one dimension, so the unsplit CTU integrator of [Colella](#colella-1990) (1990) is adopted, with extensions that CT requires (2.6). The resulting algorithm takes a single step, is of second order and fully conservative, which suits refined meshes, and reduces to the algorithm in one dimension for planar flows aligned with the grid. The paper describes it in two dimensions.

#### 2.3.2 Finite areas and the update of the field

[Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, §2) write the equations of ideal MHD with the total pressure $P^* = P + B^2/2$ and the total energy density $E = \epsilon + \rho v^2/2 + B^2/2$, for an ideal gas with $\gamma = 5/3$ unless stated otherwise (eqs. 1 to 5), with $\epsilon$ the internal energy density. None of their main results depends on the EOS. The field must stay free of divergence, or else the equations for the conserved variables cease to be consistent with those for the primitive variables. On a Cartesian grid in two dimensions, the finite-volume update gives the volume-averaged field fluxes only in its transverse components (§2.1, eqs. 6 to 12). Updates split by direction therefore do not keep the divergence zero between their sweeps, which suggests that such algorithms do not suit MHD. CT instead integrates the induction equation $\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t + \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0$, with $\mathbf{E} = -\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}$, over the faces of cells and applies Stokes' theorem (§2.2, eq. 13). In two dimensions this gives $B^{n+1}_{x,i-1/2,j} = B^n_{x,i-1/2,j} - (\delta t/\delta y)(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j+1/2} - \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j-1/2})$ and $B^{n+1}_{y,i,j-1/2} = B^n_{y,i,j-1/2} + (\delta t/\delta x)(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i+1/2,j-1/2} - \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j-1/2})$ (eqs. 14, 15). The field components are averages over the faces (eqs. 16, 17), and the electric field is an average over the step at a corner of the cell (eq. 18). As with finite volumes, the update is exact, and a method lies in how it approximates $\mathcal{E}$. The magnetic flux is conserved exactly, so that a cell without net magnetic charge at $t^n$ has none at $t^{n+1}$. Averaging over faces, the smallest domains that turn the differential equation into an integral one, keeps as much pointwise information as possible and so minimises the dissipation of averaging.

In three dimensions the update of each face adds the electric fields along the other edges of that face ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §3.2, eqs. 16 to 18), for example $B^{n+1}_{x,i-1/2,j,k} = B^n_{x,i-1/2,j,k} - (\delta t/\delta y)(\mathcal{E}_{z,i-1/2,j+1/2,k} - \mathcal{E}_{z,i-1/2,j-1/2,k}) + (\delta t/\delta z)(\mathcal{E}_{y,i-1/2,j,k+1/2} - \mathcal{E}_{y,i-1/2,j,k-1/2})$. Each component of $\mathcal{E}$ is averaged along its edge and over the step (eqs. 22 to 24). The divergence formed from the face fields at $t^{n+1}$ (eq. 25) then stays zero if it was zero at $t^n$ ([Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) 1988), and the magnetic flux through each cell is conserved. The cost is a staggered grid, which complicates the algorithm and its interface to AMR. CT on faces keeps only this one of the many possible discretisations of the divergence, while [Tóth](#toth-2000) (2000) extended CT to keep several discretisations built on fields at cell centres. Which one matters most for a given integrator is hard to judge. The authors argue that the form on faces fits finite volumes best, since it conserves the magnetic flux, and with it the total of magnetic monopoles, within each cell. A field loop advected across the grid tests whether the discretisation kept is consistent with the update of the induction equation, as an inconsistent one lets net magnetic flux grow (2.16). [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) (2008, §2) use the same CT in three dimensions.

The momentum and energy fluxes need the field at cell centres, which Athena takes as the average of the two face values along each direction, $B_{x,i,j,k} = \tfrac{1}{2}(B_{x,i-1/2,j,k} + B_{x,i+1/2,j,k})$ and likewise for $B_y$ and $B_z$, accurate to second order ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, eqs. 26 to 28; [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) 2005, eqs. 19, 20; [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) 2008, eq. 6). The face fields are evolved, and the fields at cell centres are computed from them at the end of each step.

#### 2.3.3 Consistency of finite volumes and CT

The field and its fluxes must be coupled consistently in the two representations ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) 2005, §2.3). The condition is that the cell-centred field remain the average of the face fields at $t^{n+1}$ (eqs. 19, 20). Subtracting the finite-volume update of the cell-centred field from the CT update of the face fields then fixes the fluxes of the former in terms of the corner electric fields (eqs. 21 to 24). The $y$-flux of $B_x$ through a face must be the average of $\mathcal{E}_z$ at the two ends of that face, $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_x} \cdot \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{y,i,j\pm1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i-1/2,j\pm1/2} + \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i+1/2,j\pm1/2})$, and the $x$-flux of $B_y$ minus the corresponding average, $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_y} \cdot \mathbf{F}^{n+1/2}_{x,i\pm1/2,j} = -\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i\pm1/2,j-1/2} + \mathcal{E}^{n+1/2}_{z,i\pm1/2,j+1/2})$, where $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_x}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_y}$ select the components of the flux. This agrees with the finite-volume fluxes being averages in space of the electric fields that CT uses. In effect, the Godunov fluxes of the transverse components of the volume-averaged field are replaced by averages of the corner values of $\mathcal{E}_z$, whatever algorithm computes those. The relations thus act as a correction that makes the fluxes of the Riemann solver consistent with CT, and the algorithm for the corner fields then decides how accurate and stable the whole scheme is (2.3.5).

#### 2.3.4 The field in the code

In Athena++ the class `Field` holds the face-centred field `b`, a `FaceField` with one more entry along each normal (`src/field/field.hpp`, `Field`; `src/field/field.cpp`, `Field::Field`). Further registers are `b1` and, for the integrator `ssprk5_4` or super time-stepping, `b2`, with `b0` and `ct_update` for super time-stepping of the type RKL2. The class also holds the cell-centred field `bcc`, the electric fields on the edges `e`, an `EdgeField`, and the weights `wght` with which the electric fields are carried to the corners (2.3.5). It holds as well the electric fields that the Riemann solvers return on the faces (`e2_x1f`, `e3_x1f` and so on), coarse copies for mesh refinement, its boundary variable and its diffusion (`FaceCenteredBoundaryVariable`, `FieldDiffusion`). The cell-centred field is the linear interpolation of the two face values to the volume centre of the cell (`Field::CalculateCellCenteredField`). With uniform spacing this is the average of eqs. 26 to 28, and otherwise $B_{1,i} = [(x_{1,i+1/2} - x^v_{1,i}) B_{1,i-1/2} + (x^v_{1,i} - x_{1,i-1/2}) B_{1,i+1/2}]/\Delta x_{1,i}$, with $x^v_{1,i}$ the volume centre, and likewise in the other directions. The EOS calls this function before it converts the conserved variables (2.2.1). The update of CT adds the curl of the edge electric fields in its integral form, with the areas of the faces and the lengths of the edges that the coordinates supply (`src/field/ct.cpp`, `Field::CT`). $B_1$ changes by $-(w/A_1)(L_{3,j+1/2} \mathcal{E}_{3,j+1/2} - L_{3,j-1/2} \mathcal{E}_{3,j-1/2}) + (w/A_1)(L_{2,k+1/2} \mathcal{E}_{2,k+1/2} - L_{2,k-1/2} \mathcal{E}_{2,k-1/2})$, with $A_1$ the area of its face, $L_2$ and $L_3$ the lengths of the edges and $w$ the weight that the integrator passes, and likewise $B_2$ and $B_3$. On a Cartesian grid this is eqs. 16 to 18 of [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008). $B_1$ changes only in two or three dimensions, while $B_2$ and $B_3$ change in one dimension as well, through the derivatives of $\mathcal{E}_3$ and $\mathcal{E}_2$ along $x_1$. The faces of $B_2$ that lie on a polar boundary are not updated (2.8).

#### 2.3.5 The electric fields at the corners

[Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, §3.2) derive what an algorithm for the corner fields must satisfy from plane-parallel flows aligned with the grid. There $\partial B_x/\partial x = -\partial B_y/\partial y = 0$, so that area and volume averages of the field agree, and for $\partial/\partial x = 0$ symmetry gives the corner value as the mean of the two face values beside it along $x$, $\mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} = (\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1/2} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i+1,j+1/2})/2$. An algorithm that reduces to this, or to an equivalent expression, reproduces what the underlying integrator gives for the same problem in one dimension, and the paper calls it consistent with that integrator. The algorithm should also work with any approximate Riemann solver, such as those of [Cargo and Gallice](#cargo-gallice-1997) (1997), [Dai and Woodward](#dai-woodward-1997) (1997), [Einfeldt](#einfeldt-1988) (1988) and [Li](#li-2005) (2005). It may therefore use only the electric field that the flux contains, not the waves into which the Riemann problem resolves.

The simplest algorithm, and the one most often proposed, averages the four face values that the integrator returns, $\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} = \tfrac{1}{4}(\mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j+1} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1/2} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i+1,j+1/2})$ (§3.2.1, eq. 34). It is not consistent, which shows most clearly for an unsplit integrator. For $\partial/\partial x = 0$ the two faces normal to $y$ carry equal values and the faces normal to $x$ carry the values of the cell centres, so that $\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} = \tfrac{1}{4}(\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1/2}$ (eq. 35), where symmetry requires $\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1/2}$. To judge the effect, the numerical flux is written as $\mathbf{F}_{y,i,j+1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}[\mathbf{f}_y(\mathbf{q}_{i,j}) + \mathbf{f}_y(\mathbf{q}_{i,j+1}) + \mathsf{D}_{i,j+1/2}(\mathbf{q}_{i,j} - \mathbf{q}_{i,j+1})]$, with $\mathsf{D}$ the matrix of numerical viscosity (eq. 36; [Harten, Lax and van Leer](#harten-et-al-1983) 1983; [Einfeldt](#einfeldt-1988) 1988). As the $y$-flux of $B_x$ is $\mathcal{E}_z$, the solver gives the face value $\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{B_x} \cdot \mathsf{D}_{i,j+1/2}(\mathbf{q}_{i,j} - \mathbf{q}_{i,j+1})$ (eq. 37), the predictor of 2.3.3. In eq. 35 it yields a corner value with half of that viscous term (eq. 38). The corner at $i-1/2$ has the same value by symmetry, so that eq. 23 makes it the corrected face value as well. The numerical viscosity is thus halved, the scheme no longer reduces to the integrator in one dimension, and its stability is in doubt. The fault lies in the average itself, which has no directional bias. [Balsara and Spicer](#balsara-spicer-1999) (1999) built on the same average but knew that it needs a bias, which they supplied by weights driven by two switches that detect magnetosonic shocks locally in several dimensions.

Since the viscous term of the corner value is only half its proper size, doubling it should restore the bias: $\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} = 2\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} - \tfrac{1}{4}(\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i+1,j} + \mathcal{E}_{z,i+1,j+1})$, half the sum of the four face values less a quarter of the sum of the four centre values (eqs. 39, 40). This algorithm reduces to the integrator for planar flows aligned with the grid, but its ad hoc construction leaves open how it behaves when the flow is truly multidimensional.

A systematic construction (§3.2.2) regards the algorithm as inverting the consistency relations, eqs. 23 and 24, that is, as a reconstruction or an integration. The fluxes of the integrator are values at the centres of the faces, averaged over the step, and the corner value, averaged over the step as well, is obtained by integrating in space. A face value and an estimate of the gradient between the face and the corner give one corner value, such as $\mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} = \mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j} + (\delta y/2)(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1/2,j+1/4}$ (eq. 41). The four faces next to a corner give four values, in general different, and their mean is $\mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} = \bar{\mathcal{E}}_{z,i+1/2,j+1/2} + (\delta y/8)[(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1/2,j+1/4} - (\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1/2,j+3/4}] + (\delta x/8)[(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j+1/2} - (\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x)_{i+3/4,j+1/2}]$ (eq. 42). It remains to estimate the gradients on the faces. Differentiating the induction equations gives $\partial_t(\partial B_x/\partial x) + \partial_y(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x) = 0$ on the faces normal to $y$ and $\partial_t(\partial B_y/\partial y) - \partial_x(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y) = 0$ on the faces normal to $x$ (eqs. 43, 44). Both have the form of conservation laws, so that the gradients on the faces can be computed as numerical fluxes of the HLL or the Lax–Friedrichs type, from values of $\partial B_x/\partial x$, $\partial B_y/\partial y$ and the gradients of $\mathcal{E}_z$ on either side. In the single-step scheme of first order (2.6), these values are differences between a face and the centre of the cell, $(\partial B_x/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j} = (2/\delta x)(B_{x,i+1/2,j} - B_{x,i,j})$ and $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j} = (2/\delta x)(\mathcal{E}_{z,i+1/2,j} - \mathcal{E}_{z,i,j})$, with the face value that the Riemann solver supplies and the centre value computed from the state $\mathbf{q}^n_{i,j}$ (eqs. 45, 46), and likewise along $y$.

The Lax–Friedrichs flux with a maximum wave speed $\alpha$ gives $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j+1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}[(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j} + (\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j+1}] + (\alpha/2)[(\partial B_x/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j} - (\partial B_x/\partial x)_{i+1/4,j+1}]$ on a face normal to $y$, and on a face normal to $x$ the same with the difference of $\partial B_y/\partial y$ in reverse order, because of the sign in eq. 44 (eqs. 47, 48). The four gradients in eq. 42 then give $\mathcal{E}^\alpha_z$: half the sum of the four face values, less a quarter of the sum of the four centre values, plus four terms of $\alpha/8$ times a combination of two face values and two centre values of $B_x$ or $B_y$ (eq. 49). It recovers the solution in one dimension for planar flows aligned with the grid. Its limit $\alpha = 0$ is eq. 40, which is thus the integration and averaging above with the centred estimate $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1/2,j+1/4} = \tfrac{1}{2}[(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i,j+1/4} + (\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1,j+1/4}]$ (eq. 50). As this estimate is not upwinded, some oscillations are to be expected in multidimensional flows. The terms in $\alpha$, however, matter only where the flow is truly multidimensional, and leaving them out may affect the first-order scheme little, so that the limit enters the tests as $\mathcal{E}^\circ_z$.

For pure advection, the gradient $\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y$ on a face normal to $x$ should be taken from the upwind side of the contact mode, and the authors suggest that this upwinding may suffice for a stable scheme without oscillations. The algorithm $\mathcal{E}^c_z$ combines eq. 42 with $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1/2,j+1/4}$ taken as $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i,j+1/4}$ for $v_{x,i+1/2,j} > 0$, as $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i+1,j+1/4}$ for $v_{x,i+1/2,j} < 0$ and as their mean otherwise, and likewise for the other three gradients (eq. 51). Only the direction of the mass flux matters, not how the Riemann problem is solved in detail, so that any solver, exact or approximate, can supply it. By construction $\mathcal{E}^c_z$ reduces to the integrator for planar flows aligned with the grid, and it is upwinded in several dimensions for the advection of a field. The three algorithms differ only in their numerical viscosity in multidimensional problems. The construction also suits other schemes, such as the wave propagation method of [Langseth and LeVeque](#langseth-leveque-2000) (2000), and even integrators that reconstruct the state at the corners of cells ([Balsara](#balsara-2003) 2003), since the widths $\delta x$ and $\delta y$ cancel from $\mathcal{E}^\alpha_z$, $\mathcal{E}^\circ_z$ and $\mathcal{E}^c_z$. The centre value $\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j}$ is then replaced by $\mathcal{E}_z$ in the state reconstructed at the corner, $\mathbf{q}_{i+1/2,j+1/2}$, and the reduction for planar flows still holds. [Londrillo and Del Zanna](#londrillo-del-zanna-2004) (2004) present another algorithm with this property, in a general framework for joining CT to Godunov-type schemes, with two variants, for their positive schemes and for their central ones. For a Godunov scheme of first order, their eqs. 41 to 47 show that algorithm to be identical to $\mathcal{E}^\circ_z$, although their framework does not make this obvious. The more complex algorithms of the paper may fit that framework too, which the authors did not attempt. The paper tests the three algorithms with its first-order scheme on a field loop advected across the grid and on circularly polarised Alfvén waves (§3.3; 2.16).

[Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, §6) conclude that CT combines consistently with finite volumes, and that the coupling between the electric fields of the two updates, which consistency implies, bears directly on how stable and accurate the integrator is. All three algorithms of their construction recover the solution in one dimension for planar flows aligned with the grid. In the tests $\mathcal{E}^c_z$ behaves well, with stable solutions free of oscillations, and it combines readily with other unsplit integrators, for example with central schemes or with methods of wave propagation. With PPM, the terms in $\partial B_x/\partial x$ and $\partial B_y/\partial y$, present in the primitive form in the induction equation alone, must enter the interface states, or else an advected field loop develops oscillations, as two thought experiments explain (2.6). The extension of CTU to MHD adds these terms as further source terms when the interface states are advanced to the half step, and couples the corner fields to CTU so that the divergence stays zero throughout the step (2.6). Source terms of physical origin, like external gravity or the Coriolis terms of a rotating frame, fit into the integrator without loss of its properties, a matter that the paper leaves to a later one (2.9). The extension to three dimensions mainly changes the source terms in the computation of the interface states and in their update by the transverse flux gradients ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) 2008; 2.6). The remaining conclusions, on the integrator as a whole, are those of 2.3.1.

[Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) (2008, §4) describe the algorithm for the corner fields as a predictor and corrector, with the Godunov fluxes as predictors and the fields for CT as correctors. They adopt $\mathcal{E}^c$, which [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005) found to have the best properties, as do [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, §5.3), who find that it balances accuracy and simplicity best. In three dimensions the value of $\mathcal{E}_z$ on the edge at $(i+1/2, j+1/2, k)$ is eq. 42 with the index $k$ added, with gradients chosen as in eq. 51 ([Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2008) 2008, eqs. 27, 28). The gradients come from the face values and a reference value at the cell centre, as in $(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i,j+1/4,k} = 2(\mathcal{E}_{z,i,j+1/2,k} - \mathcal{E}^r_{z,i,j,k})/\delta y$ (eq. 29). The reference field is computed from the state at the centre of the cell at a suitable time: from $\mathbf{q}^n$ for the first fluxes of a step, and from states advanced in time for the later ones (2.6). [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008) recall that CT needs averages of the electric field along the edges, whereas the Riemann solver returns averages over the faces, and that [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005) tie the two together through the averages that convert the face fields to cell-centred ones (Figure 5). They state the same algorithm for the corner at $(i-1/2, j-1/2)$ (eqs. 70 to 72), with the reference field at $t^n$ in the first and at $t^{n+1/2}$ in the second computation of the corner fields of a step in two dimensions (2.6). The components $\mathcal{E}_x$ and $\mathcal{E}_y$ in three dimensions follow by a cyclic permutation of $(x, y, z)$ and $(i, j, k)$. **[GK]** Both brackets of eq. 70 of [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008) carry the wrong sign. Moved to the corner at $(i-1/2, j-1/2)$, eq. 42 adds the gradient on the half of a face below or to the left of the corner and subtracts the one above or to the right, as in $(\delta y/8)[(\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i-1/2,j-3/4} - (\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial y)_{i-1/2,j-1/4}]$. With the printed signs, the gradient terms double the error of the plain average for a field that varies quadratically across the corner, instead of removing it. The code of Athena++ has the correct signs.

[White, Stone and Gammie](#white-et-al-2016) (2016, §3.5) extend the algorithm of [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005) to general relativity. CT itself was first developed in that setting ([Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) 1988), and their version departs from the original in details, such as velocities at the centres of cells rather than on the faces. For $\mathcal{E}^3$ on the edge at $(i-1/2, j-1/2)$ the algorithm takes five steps (Figure 2). The four face values come from the Riemann solver, the four values at the cell centres are computed, eight gradients are formed between centres and faces, these are upwinded to four gradients on the faces, and face values and gradients combine into the value on the edge. The electric fields along $x^3$ form the components $(1, 2)$ of the dual field tensor and are thus fluxes of the solver along $x^1$ and $x^2$, once transformed back to the global coordinates: $\mathcal{E}^3$ is the negative flux of $B^2$ on faces normal to $x^1$ and the flux of $B^1$ on faces normal to $x^2$. The values at the centres follow from the velocities there and the fields interpolated to the centres, through the four-vectors $u^\mu$ and $b^\mu$ (2.14). Each gradient needs a width. Between the centre $(i, j)$ and the face $(i-1/2, j)$, $\Delta x^1_{i-1/4,j}$ is the integral of $\sqrt{g_{11}}\,dx^1$ along the curve of constant $x^0$, $x^2$ and $x^3$ that joins them, and $(\partial_1 \mathcal{E}^3)_{i-1/4,j} = (\mathcal{E}^3_{i,j} - \mathcal{E}^3_{i-1/2,j})/\Delta x^1_{i-1/4,j}$ (eqs. 29, 30). The upwind side is chosen by the direction of the mass flux from the solver: $(\partial_2 \mathcal{E}^3)_{i-1/2,j-3/4}$ is taken from cell $i-1$ for $u^1_{i-1/2,j-1} > 0$ and from cell $i$ for $u^1_{i-1/2,j-1} < 0$ (eq. 31), which the authors identify with eq. 51 of [Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005). The value on the edge is eq. 42, moved to $(i-1/2, j-1/2)$ and with widths $\Delta x^1_{i-1/2,j-1/2}$ and $\Delta x^2_{i-1/2,j-1/2}$ of half a cell, defined like $\Delta x^1_{i-1/4,j}$, in place of $\delta x/2$ and $\delta y/2$, as in $(\Delta x^1_{i-1/2,j-1/2}/4)[(\partial_1 \mathcal{E}^3)_{i-3/4,j-1/2} - (\partial_1 \mathcal{E}^3)_{i-1/4,j-1/2}]$ (eq. 32). If the metric and the spacing of the grid vary smoothly, these widths cancel approximately against those of eq. 30.

In Athena++, `Field::ComputeCornerE` computes the fields on the edges in each main stage of the integrator, from the current primitive variables and cell-centred field (`src/field/calculate_corner_e.cpp`; `src/task_list/time_integrator.cpp`, `TimeIntegratorTaskList::CalculateEMF`; 2.6). The Riemann solvers store $\mathcal{E}_3$, the negative flux of $B_2$, and $\mathcal{E}_2$, the flux of $B_3$, on the faces normal to $x_1$ in `e3_x1f` and `e2_x1f`, and likewise with the indices permuted cyclically on the other faces (`src/hydro/calculate_fluxes.cpp`, `Hydro::CalculateFluxes`; `src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/hlld.cpp`, `Hydro::RiemannSolver`; 2.5). The reference fields at the cell centres are $\mathcal{E}^r_3 = v_2 B_1 - v_1 B_2$ and its cyclic permutations in Newtonian dynamics, $(u^2 B^1 - u^1 B^2)/u^0$ with the four-velocity $u^\mu$ in special relativity, and $b^1 u^2 - b^2 u^1$ in general relativity, where the metric of the cell yields $u^\mu$ and the four-vector $b^\mu$ of the field (2.14). In one dimension the face values along $x_1$ are copied to the edges. In two dimensions $\mathcal{E}_1$ and $\mathcal{E}_2$ are copied from the faces normal to $x_2$ and $x_1$, and only $\mathcal{E}_3$ is integrated to the corners, while in three dimensions all three components are. The value on an edge is $\mathcal{E}^c$ with the widths cancelled: a quarter of the sum of the four face values and of four differences between face values and reference values. For $\mathcal{E}_3$ at $(i-1/2, j-1/2)$, the difference that carries the face value at $(i-1/2, j-1)$ to the corner is $(1 - w)(\mathcal{E}_{3,i,j-1/2} - \mathcal{E}^r_{3,i,j-1}) + w(\mathcal{E}_{3,i-1,j-1/2} - \mathcal{E}^r_{3,i-1,j-1})$, with $w$ the weight on that face, and the other three are formed alike. On a Cartesian grid each difference is half a cell width times a one-sided gradient as in eq. 46, so that the sum reproduces eq. 42.

The Riemann solvers compute the weights from the mass flux $F_\rho$ through each face (`Hydro::GetWeightForCT`). With $\rho_L$ and $\rho_R$ the densities of the two states and $\Delta x$ the physical width of the cell to the right of the face, $w = 1/2 + \max\{-1/2, \min[1/2, 1024\,\Delta t\,F_\rho/(\Delta x(\rho_L + \rho_R))]\}$. The weight thus rises linearly from 0 to 1 while the velocity $F_\rho/\bar{\rho}$, with $\bar{\rho} = (\rho_L + \rho_R)/2$, grows from $-\Delta x/(1024\,\Delta t)$ to $\Delta x/(1024\,\Delta t)$, and it selects the upwind side alone beyond that range. This ramp replaces the switch of eq. 51, which the papers on Athena and eq. 31 of [White, Stone and Gammie](#white-et-al-2016) (2016) describe. The two differ only where the flow through a face crosses less than $1/1024$ of a cell in a step, and both take the mean for a vanishing mass flux. If field diffusion is enabled and super time-stepping is not, the electric fields of the diffusion are added to the edges at the end (`FieldDiffusion::AddEMF`; 2.11). Under super time-stepping, `Field::ComputeCornerE_STS` adds them alone, in the stages of the diffusion (2.11).

### 2.4 Reconstruction

#### 2.4.1 Interface states

The first step of the algorithm in one dimension computes the left and right states $\mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2}$ of the Riemann problem at the face $x_{i-1/2}$ ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.2). In this notation the left state lies to the right of the centre of cell $i-1$, and the right state to the left of the centre of cell $i$ (Figure 2). The reconstruction works in one dimension, on the one-dimensional vector of conserved variables (eq. 30), which contains the field through its transverse components alone. In several dimensions these are the cell-centred averages of the face fields, such as eqs. 27 and 28 for a reconstruction along $x$, while the longitudinal component is taken from the face where it is stored. That this component needs no reconstruction from cell centres is a further advantage of CT on a staggered grid, since a reconstruction with limited slopes would make it discontinuous at the face. With the CTU integrator, the reconstructions of second and third order in Athena limit the slopes in the characteristic variables and evolve the linearised system in the primitive variables by characteristic tracing. Both steps make the states less oscillatory, but they need the eigensystem of the linearised equations in primitive variables (2.4.4), which for more complex physics, such as relativistic MHD, can be hard to obtain. The VL+CT integrator of [Stone and Gardiner](#stone-gardiner-2009) (2009) needs no characteristic evolution and may then be the better choice (2.6).

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020, §3.2) name the three components of a Godunov method: a reconstruction without oscillations that gives the states at the faces, a Riemann solver for the upwind fluxes and electric fields on the faces, and an integrator in time. To keep the field free of divergence at every substep, the integrator must be unsplit. CTU, the most accurate unsplit integrator of Athena ([Colella](#colella-1990) 1990; [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008), projects the interface states onto characteristics during the reconstruction, which is very complex for relativistic MHD. Athena++ therefore uses simpler unsplit integrators (2.6), although CTU could still be added for non-relativistic MHD alone. Like Athena, it offers three reconstructions: donor cell of first order, PLM of second order and PPM of fourth order (§3.2.1). They act on the primitive variables $\mathbf{W} = (\rho, \mathbf{v}, P, \mathbf{B})$ or, for non-relativistic problems with an ideal EOS, on the characteristic variables $\mathbf{C} = \mathsf{L} \cdot \mathbf{W}$, with $\mathsf{L}$ the matrix of left eigenvectors (2.4.4). The characteristic variables can reduce oscillations, in MHD in particular (2.16), but their projection differs from that of Athena, and [Felker and Stone](#felker-stone-2018) (2018, §2.2.2) describe it (2.7). Athena++ needs no characteristic tracing, as it has no CTU integrator, and its stencils and limiters keep the reconstruction TVD on nonuniform and curvilinear meshes (2.4.3, 2.4.5).

In Athena++ the parameter `xorder` in the block `<time>` of the input file selects the reconstruction (`src/reconstruct/reconstruction.cpp`, `Reconstruction::Reconstruction`). The value `1` gives donor cell, `2` PLM, the default, `3` PPM, `3f` a fast variant of PPM, `wenoz` and `wenomz` two WENO schemes (2.4.5, 2.4.6), and `4` the method of fourth order (2.7). A suffix `c`, as in `2c`, `3c` and `4c`, reconstructs in characteristic variables, and a suffix `m`, as in `2m` and `2cm`, replaces the van Leer limiter of PLM by minmod. Characteristic variables cannot be combined with a general EOS, which with orders above two prints a warning of documented bugs. PPM needs three ghost cells, and the fourth order needs four, a uniform Cartesian mesh, with a warning where its cells are not square, and neither mesh refinement nor a shearing box. WENO accepts uniform Cartesian grids only. The constructor marks the directions of nonuniform spacing and the radial and polar directions of cylindrical and spherical-polar coordinates, along which PLM and PPM use their general forms (2.8). Further parameters of the block `<time>` concern PPM (2.4.5) and the fourth order (2.7). The routine for the fluxes calls the reconstruction that `xorder` selects for each line of cells (2.6).

#### 2.4.2 First order

The simplest reconstruction takes the primitive variables, and with them the conserved ones, as constant in each cell, $\mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2} = \mathbf{q}_{i-1}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2} = \mathbf{q}_i$ ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.2.1, eq. 35). It diffuses far too much for applications, but serves for tests and where extra diffusion is wanted. In Athena++, `Reconstruction::DonorCellX1` and its counterparts along $x_2$ and $x_3$ copy the primitive variables of each cell to the right state at its left face and to the left state at its right face, with the transverse field from the cell-centred field (`src/reconstruct/dc.cpp`).

#### 2.4.3 Second order

Athena reconstructs to second order with linear profiles of the primitive variables, so that the conserved variables may vary more steeply than linearly within a cell ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.2.2). With the CTU integrator the reconstruction takes eight steps. The eigensystem of the linearised equations is computed from the cell-centred primitive variables $\mathbf{w}_i$, which lack the longitudinal field (2.4.4). The differences $\delta\mathbf{w}_{L,i} = \mathbf{w}_i - \mathbf{w}_{i-1}$, $\delta\mathbf{w}_{R,i} = \mathbf{w}_{i+1} - \mathbf{w}_i$ and $\delta\mathbf{w}_{C,i} = (\mathbf{w}_{i+1} - \mathbf{w}_{i-1})/2$, named by their position relative to the cell centre (eq. 36), are projected onto the characteristic variables with the matrix $\mathsf{L}(\mathbf{w}_i)$, whose rows are the left eigenvectors, giving $\delta\mathbf{a}_{L,i}$, $\delta\mathbf{a}_{R,i}$ and $\delta\mathbf{a}_{C,i}$ (eq. 37). Monotonicity constraints make the characteristic reconstruction TVD ([LeVeque](#leveque-2002) 2002), $\delta\mathbf{a}^m_i = \mathrm{sign}(\delta\mathbf{a}_{C,i})\min(2|\delta\mathbf{a}_{L,i}|, 2|\delta\mathbf{a}_{R,i}|, |\delta\mathbf{a}_{C,i}|)$ for each component (eq. 38), and the matrix $\mathsf{R}(\mathbf{w}_i)$ of right eigenvectors, in its columns, returns $\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i = \delta\mathbf{a}^m_i \cdot \mathsf{R}(\mathbf{w}_i)$ (eq. 39). The face values are $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{L,i+1/2} = \mathbf{w}_i + [\tfrac{1}{2} - \max(\lambda^M_i, 0)\,\delta t/(2\delta x)]\,\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{R,i-1/2} = \mathbf{w}_i - [\tfrac{1}{2} + \min(\lambda^0_i, 0)\,\delta t/(2\delta x)]\,\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i$, with $\lambda^M_i$ and $\lambda^0_i$ the largest and smallest eigenvalues of the cell (eqs. 40, 41). Each equals the value that the fastest wave moving towards the face carries to it, averaged over the step. The characteristic tracing of [Colella](#colella-1990) (1990) and [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) then removes what each wave family cannot carry to the face in that time, $\mathbf{w}_{L,i+1/2} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{L,i+1/2} + (\delta t/2\delta x)\sum_{\lambda^\alpha > 0}(\lambda^M_i - \lambda^\alpha_i)(\mathbf{l}^\alpha \cdot \delta\mathbf{w}^m_i)\,\mathbf{r}^\alpha$, and likewise $\mathbf{w}_{R,i-1/2}$ with $\lambda^0_i$ and a sum over $\lambda^\alpha < 0$ (eqs. 42, 43). The sums run over the waves that move towards the face, and $\mathbf{l}^\alpha$ and $\mathbf{r}^\alpha$ are the left and right eigenvectors of the wave $\alpha$. Solvers that average over the states between their waves, such as those of the HLL family, also use the state from which a wave moves away, which without its predictor would leave state and flux of first order. With them the waves moving away enter as well, through $-(\delta t/2\delta x)\sum_{\lambda^\alpha < 0}(\lambda^\alpha_i - \lambda^M_i)(\mathbf{l}^\alpha \cdot \delta\mathbf{w}^m_i)\,\mathbf{r}^\alpha$ in $\mathbf{w}_{L,i+1/2}$ and $-(\delta t/2\delta x)\sum_{\lambda^\alpha > 0}(\lambda^\alpha_i - \lambda^0_i)(\mathbf{l}^\alpha \cdot \delta\mathbf{w}^m_i)\,\mathbf{r}^\alpha$ in $\mathbf{w}_{R,i-1/2}$ (eqs. 44, 45), terms left out with the Roe and the exact solvers. Last, the states are converted to conserved variables. **[GK]** Eq. 41 prints $\tfrac{1}{2} - \min(\lambda^0_i, 0)\,\delta t/(2\delta x)$ in the bracket. For a wave moving to the left this factor would exceed one half and reach beyond the face, whereas the value that the wave carries requires the plus sign, which eqs. 43 and 45 also presuppose.

Athena++ limits the differences of PLM with the original limiter of [van Leer](#van-leer-1974) (1974) where the spacing is uniform and no geometric factor enters, as on uniform Cartesian grids and in uniform azimuthal directions, and with the modified van Leer limiter of [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014) on nonuniform or curvilinear meshes ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.2.1). The weights of the smooth stencil adapt to such grids by themselves if the backward and forward differences are divided by the distances to the centroids of volume. In the code, the left and right differences of the primitive variables, with the transverse field from the cell-centred field, are projected onto characteristic variables if asked (`src/reconstruct/plm.cpp`, `Reconstruction::PiecewiseLinearX1`; 2.4.4). Along a uniform Cartesian direction the limited difference is the harmonic mean $\delta w^m_i = 2\,\delta w_{L,i}\,\delta w_{R,i}/(\delta w_{L,i} + \delta w_{R,i})$ where the two differences agree in sign, and zero otherwise, while the option of minmod takes the smaller of the two in magnitude. Elsewhere the differences become $\delta q_B = \delta w_{L,i}\,\Delta x_i/\Delta x^v_{i-1}$ and $\delta q_F = \delta w_{R,i}\,\Delta x_i/\Delta x^v_i$, with $\Delta x^v$ the distance between neighbouring volume centres, and the limiter of [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014, eqs. 33, 37) gives $\delta w^m_i = \delta q_F\,\delta q_B\,(c_f\,\delta q_B + c_b\,\delta q_F)/[\delta q_B^2 + \delta q_F^2 + \delta q_F\,\delta q_B\,(c_f + c_b - 2)]$, with $c_f = \Delta x^v_i/(x_{i+1/2} - x^v_i)$ and $c_b = \Delta x^v_{i-1}/(x^v_i - x_{i-1/2})$, again zero where the signs differ. On a uniform Cartesian grid $c_f = c_b = 2$, and the harmonic mean returns. The limited difference is projected back if needed, and the states lie on the line through the volume centre, $w_{L,i+1/2} = w_i + [(x_{i+1/2} - x^v_i)/\Delta x_i]\,\delta w^m_i$ and $w_{R,i-1/2} = w_i - [(x^v_i - x_{i-1/2})/\Delta x_i]\,\delta w^m_i$. After a characteristic projection the floors of the EOS are applied to both states again (2.2.1). Athena++ thus limits with the harmonic mean of van Leer where Athena uses the limiter of eq. 38.

#### 2.4.4 Characteristic variables

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, App. A) give the eigensystem of the matrix $\mathsf{A}(\mathbf{W})$ of the equations in primitive variables in one dimension, $\partial \mathbf{W}/\partial t + \mathsf{A}(\mathbf{W})\,\partial \mathbf{W}/\partial x = 0$, which convert between primitive and characteristic variables. For adiabatic hydrodynamics $\mathbf{W} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, P)$, and the eigenvalues in ascending order are $(v_x - a, v_x, v_x, v_x, v_x + a)$, with $a^2 = \gamma P/\rho$ (eqs. A1, A2). The right eigenvectors of the two sound waves are $(1, \mp a/\rho, 0, 0, a^2)$, the columns of $\mathsf{R}$ (eq. A3), and their left eigenvectors $(0, \mp\rho/(2a), 0, 0, 1/(2a^2))$, rows of $\mathsf{L}$ (eq. A4). The entropy wave has the right eigenvector $(1, 0, 0, 0, 0)$ and the left eigenvector $(1, 0, 0, 0, -1/a^2)$, and the two shear waves have unit vectors along $v_y$ and $v_z$. For isothermal hydrodynamics $\mathbf{W} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z)$ and the eigenvalues are $(v_x - C, v_x, v_x, v_x + C)$, with $C$ the isothermal sound speed. The right eigenvectors are those of eq. A3 without the entropy wave and the row of the pressure, and the left eigenvectors of the sound waves become $(1/2, \mp\rho/(2C), 0, 0)$ (eqs. A5 to A7).

For adiabatic MHD $\mathbf{W} = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, P, b_y, b_z)$, with $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{B}/\sqrt{4\pi}$, and the seven eigenvalues are $v_x \mp C_f$, $v_x \mp C_{Ax}$, $v_x \mp C_s$ and $v_x$ (eqs. A8, A9). The fast and slow speeds are $C^2_{f,s} = \tfrac{1}{2}\{a^2 + C_A^2 \pm [(a^2 + C_A^2)^2 - 4a^2 C_{Ax}^2]^{1/2}\}$, with the Alfvén speeds $C_A^2 = (b_x^2 + b_y^2 + b_z^2)/\rho$ and $C_{Ax}^2 = b_x^2/\rho$ (eqs. A10, A11). The eigenvectors (eqs. A12, A18) normalise the fast and slow waves with $\alpha_f^2 = (a^2 - C_s^2)/(C_f^2 - C_s^2)$ and $\alpha_s^2 = (C_f^2 - a^2)/(C_f^2 - C_s^2)$, and they use the direction of the transverse field, $\beta_y = b_y/(b_y^2 + b_z^2)^{1/2}$ and $\beta_z = b_z/(b_y^2 + b_z^2)^{1/2}$, the sign $S = \mathrm{sign}(b_x)$, and the combinations $C_{ff} = C_f\alpha_f$, $C_{ss} = C_s\alpha_s$, $Q_f = C_f\alpha_f S$, $Q_s = C_s\alpha_s S$, $A_f = a\alpha_f\sqrt{\rho}$ and $A_s = a\alpha_s\sqrt{\rho}$ (eqs. A13 to A17). The fast wave moving to the left, for instance, has the right eigenvector $(\rho\alpha_f, -C_{ff}, Q_s\beta_y, Q_s\beta_z, \rho a^2\alpha_f, A_s\beta_y, A_s\beta_z)$, and the left eigenvectors of the fast and slow waves carry the factors $N_f = N_s = 1/(2a^2)$ (eq. A19). In the degenerate case $C_A = C_{Ax} = a$, where $C_f = C_s$, the normalisation becomes $\alpha_f = 1$ and $\alpha_s = 0$. Isothermal MHD drops the pressure and the entropy wave, with $C$ in place of $a$ throughout and $N_f = N_s = 1/(2C^2)$ (eqs. A20 to A23).

Athena++ applies these matrices without forming them (`src/reconstruct/characteristic.cpp`, `Reconstruction::LeftEigenmatrixDotVector`, `Reconstruction::RightEigenmatrixDotVector`). The two functions multiply a vector of differences by the rows of eqs. A4, A7, A18 or A22, or by the columns of eqs. A3 or A12, after permuting the components of the velocity so that the first lies along the direction of the reconstruction. The fast speed comes from $\rho C_f^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\{b_x^2 + b_t^2 + \gamma P + [(b_x^2 + b_t^2 - \gamma P)^2 + 4\gamma P b_t^2]^{1/2}\}$, with $b_t^2 = b_y^2 + b_z^2$, which is equivalent to eq. A10, and the slow speed from $C_s^2 = a^2 C_{Ax}^2/C_f^2$. Where the transverse field vanishes, $\beta_y = 1$ and $\beta_z = 0$. The two may be chosen freely as long as they do not both vanish, which keeps the eigenvectors orthonormal ([Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) 1988, eq. 45; [Roe and Balsara](#roe-balsara-1996) 1996). The normalisation takes $\alpha_f = 1$ and $\alpha_s = 0$ where $C_f^2 \le C_s^2$, the triple umbilic of [Roe and Balsara](#roe-balsara-1996) (1996), or where $C_f^2 \le a^2$, when the fast waves are acoustic at high plasma beta, and $\alpha_f = 0$ and $\alpha_s = 1$ where $a^2 \le C_s^2$, when the slow waves are acoustic at low beta.

#### 2.4.5 The piecewise parabolic method

Athena reconstructs to third order with the PPM of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984), because the smaller truncation error improves the solutions although its algorithms remain formally of second order ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.2.3; 2.16). The first five steps are those of PLM, up to the limited differences $\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i$ (2.4.3). Parabolic interpolation then gives the values on both sides of the cell, $\mathbf{w}_{L,i} = (\mathbf{w}_i + \mathbf{w}_{i-1})/2 - (\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i - \delta\mathbf{w}^m_{i-1})/6$ and $\mathbf{w}_{R,i} = (\mathbf{w}_{i+1} + \mathbf{w}_i)/2 - (\delta\mathbf{w}^m_{i+1} - \delta\mathbf{w}^m_i)/6$ (eq. 46). Further constraints keep both between the neighbouring cell averages, for each component of $\mathbf{w}$ ([Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) 1984, eq. 1.10). Where $(\mathbf{w}_{R,i} - \mathbf{w}_i)(\mathbf{w}_i - \mathbf{w}_{L,i}) \le 0$, both are set to $\mathbf{w}_i$. Where $6(\mathbf{w}_{R,i} - \mathbf{w}_{L,i})[\mathbf{w}_i - (\mathbf{w}_{L,i} + \mathbf{w}_{R,i})/2]$ exceeds $(\mathbf{w}_{R,i} - \mathbf{w}_{L,i})^2$, $\mathbf{w}_{L,i} = 3\mathbf{w}_i - 2\mathbf{w}_{R,i}$, and where it falls below $-(\mathbf{w}_{R,i} - \mathbf{w}_{L,i})^2$, $\mathbf{w}_{R,i} = 3\mathbf{w}_i - 2\mathbf{w}_{L,i}$. With $\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i = \mathbf{w}_{R,i} - \mathbf{w}_{L,i}$ and $\mathbf{w}_{6,i} = 6[\mathbf{w}_i - (\mathbf{w}_{L,i} + \mathbf{w}_{R,i})/2]$ (eq. 47), the parabola is averaged over the region from which the fastest wave reaches each face ([Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) 1984, eq. 1.12): $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{L,i+1/2} = \mathbf{w}_{R,i} - \lambda^{\max}(\delta t/2\delta x)[\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i - (1 - \lambda^{\max}\,2\delta t/3\delta x)\,\mathbf{w}_{6,i}]$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{R,i-1/2} = \mathbf{w}_{L,i} - \lambda^{\min}(\delta t/2\delta x)[\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i + (1 + \lambda^{\min}\,2\delta t/3\delta x)\,\mathbf{w}_{6,i}]$, with $\lambda^{\max} = \max(\lambda^M_i, 0)$ and $\lambda^{\min} = \min(\lambda^0_i, 0)$ (eqs. 48, 49). Characteristic tracing corrects each wave family that moves towards a face to its own speed, $\mathbf{w}_{L,i+1/2} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{L,i+1/2} + \sum_{\lambda^\alpha > 0}\mathbf{l}^\alpha \cdot [A(\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i - \mathbf{w}_{6,i}) + B\,\mathbf{w}_{6,i}]\,\mathbf{r}^\alpha$, with $A = (\delta t/2\delta x)(\lambda^M - \lambda^\alpha)$ and $B = \tfrac{1}{3}(\delta t/\delta x)^2[(\lambda^M)^2 - (\lambda^\alpha)^2]$. The state $\mathbf{w}_{R,i-1/2}$ follows likewise from a sum over $\lambda^\alpha < 0$, with $\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i + \mathbf{w}_{6,i}$ in place of $\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i - \mathbf{w}_{6,i}$ and $\lambda^0$ in place of $\lambda^M$ (eqs. 50, 51). For solvers of the HLL family, the waves that move away from a face enter through the terms of eqs. 44 and 45, accurate to second order, and the states are again converted to conserved variables. The limiters of steps 4 and 7 clip extrema. Athena therefore also offers the limiters of [Colella and Sekora](#colella-sekora-2008) (2008), which are designed against this and improve some tests markedly. The lesson that the authors draw is that a faster convergence of the reconstruction is not always the best route to a more accurate solution. **[GK]** Both lines of eq. 46 put a plus sign between the differences, as in $(\delta\mathbf{w}^m_i + \delta\mathbf{w}^m_{i-1})/6$. With it a linear profile would receive wrong face values, and smooth data would be interpolated to first instead of fourth order; the minus sign of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984, eq. 1.9) is right. Eq. 49 prints $+\lambda^{\min}$ before the bracket and $1 - \lambda^{\min}\,2\delta t/3\delta x$ within it. As $\lambda^{\min} \le 0$, both signs must be reversed, as eq. 1.12 of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) gives for the distance $-\lambda^{\min}\delta t$ and as the coefficients $C$ and $D$ of eq. 51 presuppose.

Athena++ modifies PPM substantially, so that it stays accurate on nonuniform and curvilinear meshes ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.2.1). [Felker and Stone](#felker-stone-2018) (2018, §2.2.2) review five formulations of its limiter that the developers weighed, with the errata of their original papers (2.7). The main limiter, used on all Cartesian meshes, is the limiter of [McCorquodale et al.](#mccorquodale-et-al-2015) (2015) that preserves smooth extrema, an extension of the work of [Colella and Sekora](#colella-sekora-2008) (2008). On curvilinear grids the limiter of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) runs through steps modified by [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014, §3.3), which account for the difference between the geometric and volumetric centres of cells. Along nonuniform directions of Cartesian type, eq. 1.6 of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) gives the stencil for face values of fourth order, which on uniform grids reduces to their eq. 1.9, $Q_{i-1/2} = \tfrac{7}{12}(Q_{i-1} + Q_i) - \tfrac{1}{12}(Q_{i-2} + Q_{i+1})$ ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, eq. 10). Along the radial direction of cylindrical and spherical-polar coordinates and the polar direction of the latter, the curvilinear counterparts of these weights follow [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014, §2.2; 2.8). As the CT of Athena++ is of second order by default, its MHD stays formally second-order accurate with reconstructions of higher order, although these often improve the solutions markedly (2.16). The paper adds that a scheme of fourth order throughout has been implemented, as published by [Felker and Stone](#felker-stone-2018) (2018), which the copy offers for hydrodynamics only (1.2; 2.7).

In Athena++, `Reconstruction::PiecewiseParabolicX1` and its counterparts along $x_2$ and $x_3$ build the states in five steps, after [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984), [Colella and Sekora](#colella-sekora-2008) (2008), [McCorquodale and Colella](#mccorquodale-colella-2011) (2011), [Colella et al.](#colella-et-al-2011) (2011) and [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014), whom the file cites (`src/reconstruct/ppm.cpp`). The cell averages of the primitive variables, with the transverse field from the cell-centred field, are projected onto characteristic variables if asked (2.4.4). First, eq. 1.6 of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) gives the face values from unlimited average slopes, $a_{i-1/2} = c_3 q_{i-1} + c_4 q_i + c_5\,\delta q_{i-1} + c_6\,\delta q_i$ with $\delta q_i = c_1 (q_{i+1} - q_i) + c_2 (q_i - q_{i-1})$. On uniform grids $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = c_4 = 1/2$, $c_5 = 1/6$ and $c_6 = -1/6$, which is eq. 10, and along curvilinear radial directions a stencil of four averages with the weights of [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014) takes its place (2.8). Second, along a uniform Cartesian direction, a face value outside the range of its two neighbouring averages becomes $\tfrac{1}{2}(q_{i-1} + q_i) - \tilde{d}/6$ ([Colella et al.](#colella-et-al-2011) 2011, §4.3.1). The limited second derivative $\tilde{d}$ is $\mathrm{sign}(d_f)\min(C_2|d_{i-1}|, C_2|d_i|, |d_f|)$ where $d_f = 3(q_{i-1} + q_i - 2a_{i-1/2})$ and the second differences $d_{i-1}$ and $d_i$ of the neighbouring cells agree in sign, and zero otherwise, with the constant $C_2 = 1.25$ of [Colella and Sekora](#colella-sekora-2008) (2008). Along other directions the face values are clipped to the range of their neighbours ([Mignone](#mignone-2014) 2014, eq. 45). Third, the differences $\delta a_- = q_i - a_{i-1/2}$ and $\delta a_+ = a_{i+1/2} - q_i$ describe the parabola of the cell. Fourth, along a uniform Cartesian direction and with the parameter `ext_preserving` of the block `<time>` true, the default, the limiter of [Colella and Sekora](#colella-sekora-2008) (2008) acts on the parabola. The second difference of the parabola, $d_P = 6(a_{i-1/2} + a_{i+1/2} - 2q_i)$, and those of the cells $i-1$, $i$ and $i+1$ give $\tilde{d} = \mathrm{sign}(d_P)\min(C_2|d_{i-1}|, C_2|d_i|, C_2|d_{i+1}|, |d_P|)$ where all four agree in sign, at a smooth extremum, and zero otherwise. The ratio $\rho = \tilde{d}/d_P$ is set to zero where $|d_P|$ is below $10^{-12}$ times the size of the neighbouring averages ([McCorquodale and Colella](#mccorquodale-colella-2011) 2011, eq. 27). At an extremum of the parabola or of the cell averages, the face values become $q_i - \rho\,\delta a_-$ and $q_i + \rho\,\delta a_+$, unless $\rho$ lies within $10^{-12}$ of one ([Colella and Sekora](#colella-sekora-2008) 2008, eq. 23). Elsewhere a parabola that would overshoot is corrected as eq. 1.10 of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) does: where $|\delta a_-| \ge 2|\delta a_+|$ the left value becomes $q_i - 2\delta a_+$, and where $|\delta a_+| \ge 2|\delta a_-|$ the right value becomes $q_i + 2\delta a_-$. Along other directions, or with `ext_preserving` false, the limiter of [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014) flattens the parabola where $\delta a_- \delta a_+ \le 0$ and corrects overshoots with ratios that replace the factor 2 along curvilinear directions (2.8). Fifth, the values become the states $w_{R,i-1/2}$ and $w_{L,i+1/2}$, are projected back if needed, and receive the floors of the EOS (2.2.1).

Unlike Athena, Athena++ thus interpolates the face values with unlimited slopes and limits them afterwards. It neither averages the parabola over a domain of dependence nor traces characteristics, which [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020, §3.2.1) explain by the absence of CTU (2.4.1). [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008) call the method third order and [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020) fourth order, after the interpolation of the face values, while the code selects it with `xorder` set to `3`, to signal an overall order between two and four (`Reconstruction::Reconstruction`). The variant `3f` is a simplified PPM without characteristic projection and without the limiter that preserves extrema (`src/reconstruct/ppm_fast.cpp`, `Reconstruction::PiecewiseParabolicFastX1`). It computes and limits the face values as above, but always applies the limiter of [Mignone](#mignone-2014) (2014) to the parabola, works cell by cell, and applies the floors of the EOS only if the parameter `floor_ppm_fast` of the block `<time>` is true, which it is not by default.

#### 2.4.6 WENO

The values `wenoz` and `wenomz` of `xorder` select reconstructions of the WENO type, of fifth order, on uniform Cartesian grids (`src/reconstruct/weno.cpp`). They use the five averages $q_{i-2}$ to $q_{i+2}$, the stencil of PPM, which is why the code counts them under `xorder` 3. Three parabolic candidates give the value at the right face of cell $i$ as $(2q_{i-2} - 7q_{i-1} + 11q_i)/6$, $(-q_{i-1} + 5q_i + 2q_{i+1})/6$ and $(2q_i + 5q_{i+1} - q_{i+2})/6$, and the value at the left face by reflection. Their indicators of smoothness are $\beta_0 = \tfrac{13}{12}(q_{i-2} - 2q_{i-1} + q_i)^2 + \tfrac{1}{4}(q_{i-2} - 4q_{i-1} + 3q_i)^2$, $\beta_1 = \tfrac{13}{12}(q_{i-1} - 2q_i + q_{i+1})^2 + \tfrac{1}{4}(q_{i-1} - q_{i+1})^2$ and $\beta_2$ by reflection of $\beta_0$. WENO-Z after [Borges et al.](#borges-et-al-2008) (2008) weights the candidates in proportion to $\alpha_k = d_k[1 + (\tau_5/(\beta_k + \epsilon))^2]$, with the linear weights $d_k = (0.1, 0.6, 0.3)$, $\tau_5 = |\beta_0 - \beta_2|$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-42}$ (`Reconstruction::WENOZ`). WENO-MZ after [Wang, Zhao and Yuan](#wang-et-al-2024) (2024) adds $b_4 = \tfrac{1}{12}(q_{i-1} - 2q_i + q_{i+1})^2$ and the ratio $r = (|\beta_2 - \beta_1| + \epsilon)/(|\beta_1 - \beta_0| + \epsilon)$, and weights in proportion to $\alpha_k = d_k[1 + \eta\,\tau_5/(\beta_k + \epsilon) + (1 - \eta)\,\tau_5/(t_k b_4 + \epsilon)]$, with $t = (1 + r, 2, 1 + 1/r)$, $\eta = [\tau_5/(\max(\beta_0, \beta_2) + \epsilon)]^4$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-40}$ (`Reconstruction::WENOMZ`). Both act on the primitive variables and the transverse field, never on characteristic variables, and the floors of the EOS are applied to the states. The files `src/reconstruct/weno.cpp` and `src/reconstruct/ppm_fast.cpp` are among those that the copy holds beyond release v24.0 (1.1).

### 2.5 Riemann solvers

#### 2.5.1 Approximate solvers

The second step of the algorithm in one dimension computes fluxes averaged over the step with a Riemann solver ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.3). Exact solvers for MHD ([Ryu and Jones](#ryu-jones-1995) 1995) generally cost too much, and since only the integral in time of the solution along the face is needed, which gives the flux, approximate solvers that estimate the flux accurately suffice. Nor need every face use the same solver: simple solvers may serve in smooth regions and robust, more expensive ones only where simple ones fail, as in strong rarefactions, which occupy few faces. Possible solvers include the nonlinear flux of HLL ([Harten, Lax and van Leer](#harten-et-al-1983) 1983), the HLLD flux ([Miyoshi and Kusano](#miyoshi-kusano-2005) 2005), the FORCE flux of [Toro](#toro-1999) (1999), the linear solver of [Roe](#roe-1981) (1981) extended to MHD ([Cargo and Gallice](#cargo-gallice-1997) 1997), and MHD solvers built on other approximations ([Dai and Woodward](#dai-woodward-1994) 1994; [Zachary et al.](#zachary-et-al-1994) 1994). Athena implements a range of them, with exact solvers in the simplest case, isothermal hydrodynamics. Godunov methods need no expensive solvers based on characteristic decompositions, and the local Lax–Friedrichs (LLF) or HLL fluxes of other methods serve as well. More complex solvers are chosen because they dissipate less, above all near discontinuities in the intermediate waves.

Athena++ implements, like Athena, the solvers HLLE, HLLC and HLLD and the linearised solver of Roe, but no exact solver, since exact solvers improve the accuracy of most problems little, although they may make the algorithm more robust in strong rarefactions ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) 2020, §3.2.2). HLLE, HLLC and HLLD are extended to a general EOS, which then supplies the sound speed analytically or by interpolation in a table ([Coleman](#coleman-2020) 2020; 2.2.2). With a general EOS, however, `configure.py` of the copy accepts HLLC and HLLD alone, although `src/hydro/rsolvers/hydro/hlle.cpp` contains a branch for it, in line with the finding of [Coleman](#coleman-2020) (2020, §5.1.4, §6) that no change to HLLE let it converge in the double shocks of 2.2.4. The option `--flux` of `configure.py` compiles one solver into the code as `Hydro::RiemannSolver`: `hlle`, `hllc`, `lhllc`, `hlld`, `lhlld`, `roe` or `llf`, with the two solvers of low dissipation of 2.5.5. The default is HLLD for MHD, HLLC for adiabatic and HLLE for isothermal hydrodynamics, and HLLE in general relativity. HLLC and its low-dissipation variant work only for adiabatic hydrodynamics, HLLD only for MHD and LHLLD only for adiabatic MHD. Each solver returns the fluxes of the conserved variables on the faces of one line of cells and, in MHD, the electric fields and weights of 2.3.5 (`src/hydro/calculate_fluxes.cpp`, `Hydro::CalculateFluxes`).

#### 2.5.2 HLLE and LLF

The simplest solver of Athena takes the HLL fluxes in the form of [Einfeldt et al.](#einfeldt-et-al-1991) (1991), the HLLE solver ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.3.1). Its flux is $\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{HLLE}}_{i-1/2} = [b^+\mathbf{f}_{L,i-1/2} - b^-\mathbf{f}_{R,i-1/2} + b^+ b^-(\mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2} - \mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2})]/(b^+ - b^-)$ (eq. 52), with the fluxes $\mathbf{f}_{L,i-1/2} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2})$ and $\mathbf{f}_{R,i-1/2} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2})$ of the two states from eq. 30 and the speeds $b^+ = \max[\max(\lambda^M, v_{x,R} + c_R), 0]$ and $b^- = \min[\min(\lambda^0, v_{x,L} - c_L), 0]$ (eqs. 53, 54). Here $\lambda^M$ and $\lambda^0$ denote the extreme eigenvalues of the matrix of Roe (2.5.4), which explicit formulae give without a characteristic decomposition, and $c_L$ and $c_R$ are the fastest speeds of the two states, the fast speed in MHD and the sound speed in hydrodynamics. If all waves move one way, the flux becomes that of the upwind state. The solver approximates the solution by a single constant intermediate state, a conservative average bounded by the estimates of the extreme speeds. It thus neglects the contact wave of hydrodynamics and the contact, slow and Alfvén waves of MHD, which it diffuses strongly, contacts even at $v_x = 0$, and its use in applications is limited. Its intermediate state, however, keeps density and pressure positive, so that in one dimension it yields a positive integrator ([Einfeldt et al.](#einfeldt-et-al-1991) 1991), unlike linearised solvers such as that of Roe, whose intermediate states can turn negative. HLLE therefore replaces a more accurate solver in the rare cases where that fails, although in several dimensions and at higher order positivity depends on the integrator. **[GK]** Eq. 52 multiplies $b^+ b^-/(b^+ - b^-)$ by $\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_{i-1}$, the jump between the cell averages. The HLL flux follows from the integral of the conservation laws over the fan of the Riemann problem between the two states, so the jump must be $\mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2} - \mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2}$, which equals the jump between the averages at first order only; the code uses the states.

The HLLE solver of Athena++ forms Roe averages of the two states, with the enthalpy $H = (E + P)/\rho$ averaged for adiabatic flows after [Roe](#roe-1981) (1981), and the sound speed $a = [(\gamma - 1)(\bar{H} - \bar{v}^2/2)]^{1/2}$ of the average, set to zero where the bracket is negative (`src/hydro/rsolvers/hydro/hlle.cpp`). The speeds $b^- = \min(\bar{v}_x - a, v_{x,L} - c_L, 0)$ and $b^+ = \max(\bar{v}_x + a, v_{x,R} + c_R, 0)$ bound the fan, as in eqs. 53 and 54. With the fluxes along the lines of the outer waves, $\mathbf{f}_L - b^-\mathbf{q}_L$ and $\mathbf{f}_R - b^+\mathbf{q}_R$, the flux is their mean plus their difference times $\tfrac{1}{2}(b^+ + b^-)/(b^+ - b^-)$, which is eq. 52 with the states. The branch for a general EOS takes the speeds from the two states alone, $\min(v_{x,L} - c_L, v_{x,R} - c_R)$ and $\max(v_{x,L} + c_L, v_{x,R} + c_R)$. The version for MHD uses the fast speed of the Roe average, built with the factors $X$ and $Y$ of 2.5.4 (`src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/hlle_mhd.cpp`). The LLF solver, the method of Rusanov, diffuses even more and serves for tests and where other solvers fail (`src/hydro/rsolvers/hydro/llf.cpp`, `src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/llf_mhd.cpp`). Its flux is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}_L + \mathbf{f}_R) - \tfrac{1}{2}S(\mathbf{q}_R - \mathbf{q}_L)$, with $S = \max(|v_{x,L}| + c_L, |v_{x,R}| + c_R)$ ([Toro](#toro-1999) 1999, eqs. 10.42, 10.43).

#### 2.5.3 HLLC and HLLD

HLLC adds the contact wave to the HLL solver of hydrodynamics, which yields two constant states between the outer waves, divided by the contact discontinuity ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.3.1; [Toro](#toro-1999) 1999, §10.4). Athena chooses its wave speeds after [Batten et al.](#batten-et-al-1997) (1997), so that the pressures of the two intermediate states, computed from the jump conditions across the outer waves, agree. For hydrodynamics this HLLC is as accurate as the solver of Roe or more, at much lower cost, and positive in one dimension, though not necessarily in several, so that it is the recommended solver for adiabatic hydrodynamics. [Miyoshi and Kusano](#miyoshi-kusano-2005) (2005) extended HLL to MHD with the fast, Alfvén and contact waves and four constant intermediate states. This HLLD solver reduces exactly to HLLC for a vanishing longitudinal field and is positive. In tests with Athena it typically matches the accuracy of the solver of Roe for MHD at much lower cost, the best choice for many applications.

Athena++ uses HLLC ([Toro, Spruce and Speares](#toro-et-al-1994) 1994) for hydrodynamics, also with a general EOS ([Coleman](#coleman-2020) 2020, §3.3). Before that work it estimated the extreme speeds from the Roe average of the middle state ([Toro](#toro-1999) 1999, §10.5.1, §11.3.3), which with a general EOS needs a fourth function of the EOS, $a^2(\rho, h)$ of the density and the specific enthalpy $h = H - v^2/2$, besides the three of 2.2.2 (App. A.1). The averages $\tilde{\rho} = (\rho_L\rho_R)^{1/2}$, $\tilde{v}_x$ and $\tilde{H}$ weighted by $\rho^{1/2}$, and $\tilde{a}^2 = a^2(\tilde{\rho}, \tilde{h})$, which for an ideal gas is $(\gamma - 1)\tilde{h}$, give $\lambda_{\min} = \min(v_{x,L} - a_L, \tilde{v}_x - \tilde{a})$ and $\lambda_{\max} = \max(v_{x,R} + a_R, \tilde{v}_x + \tilde{a})$ (eqs. A1 to A7). The generalisation of [Hu, Adams and Iaccarino](#hu-et-al-2009) (2009), which computes $\tilde{a}^2$ another way, gave comparable results. The PVRS method of [Toro](#toro-1999) (1999, §9.3, §10.5.2), which solves the Riemann problem approximately in primitive variables, instead reduced the errors and improved the convergence of tests 3 and 4 (2.2.4), without harming the accuracy with an ideal gas, and needs only the three functions, so Athena++ now uses it (App. A.2). It estimates the middle pressure and velocity from the arithmetic means $\bar{\rho}$ and $\bar{a}$ of the densities and sound speeds, $p_* = \tfrac{1}{2}(p_L + p_R) - \tfrac{1}{2}(v_{x,R} - v_{x,L})\bar{\rho}\bar{a}$ and $v_{x*} = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_{x,L} + v_{x,R}) - \tfrac{1}{2}(p_R - p_L)/(\bar{\rho}\bar{a})$, then the middle densities $\rho_{*L} = \rho_L + (v_{x,L} - v_{x*})\bar{\rho}/\bar{a}$ and $\rho_{*R} = \rho_R + (v_{x*} - v_{x,R})\bar{\rho}/\bar{a}$, and $\Gamma_{*K} = \rho_{*K}\,a^2(\rho_{*K}, p_*)/p_*$ for $K = L, R$, which an ideal gas reduces to $\gamma$ (eqs. A8 to A14). With $q_K = 1$ for $p_* \le p_K$ and $q_K = [1 + (\Gamma_{*K} + 1)(p_*/p_K - 1)/(2\Gamma_{*K})]^{1/2}$ otherwise, the extreme speeds are $\lambda_{\min} = v_{x,L} - a_L q_L$ and $\lambda_{\max} = v_{x,R} + a_R q_R$ (eqs. A15 to A17). For MHD, Athena++ keeps HLLD with the estimate of eq. 12 of [Miyoshi and Kusano](#miyoshi-kusano-2005) (2005).

In the code, the HLLC solver takes these speeds and the contact of [Batten et al.](#batten-et-al-1997) (1997) (`src/hydro/rsolvers/hydro/hllc.cpp`). With $b^- = \min(\lambda_{\min}, -\epsilon)$ and $b^+ = \max(\lambda_{\max}, \epsilon)$, $\epsilon$ a tiny number, the contact moves at $S_M = (T_L - T_R)/(m_L + m_R)$ and carries the pressure $p_M = (m_L T_R + m_R T_L)/(m_L + m_R)$, floored at zero, where $m_L = \rho_L(v_{x,L} - \lambda_{\min})$, $m_R = \rho_R(\lambda_{\max} - v_{x,R})$, $T_L = p_L + \rho_L v_{x,L}(v_{x,L} - \lambda_{\min})$ and $T_R = p_R + \rho_R v_{x,R}(v_{x,R} - \lambda_{\max})$. For $S_M \ge 0$ the flux is $[S_M(\mathbf{f}_L - b^-\mathbf{q}_L) - b^- p_M\,\mathbf{d}]/(S_M - b^-)$, with $\mathbf{d} = (0, 1, 0, 0, S_M)$ adding the pressure to the normal momentum and its work to the energy, and for $S_M < 0$ correspondingly with the right state. The HLLD solver follows [Miyoshi and Kusano](#miyoshi-kusano-2005) (2005), whose equations its comments cite (`src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/hlld.cpp`). The outer speeds are $S_L = \min(v_{x,L} - c_{f,L}, v_{x,R} - c_{f,R})$ and $S_R = \max(v_{x,L} + c_{f,L}, v_{x,R} + c_{f,R})$, from the fast speeds of the two states. The contact moves at $S_M = [(S_R - v_{x,R})\rho_R v_{x,R} - (S_L - v_{x,L})\rho_L v_{x,L} + p_{T,L} - p_{T,R}]/[(S_R - v_{x,R})\rho_R - (S_L - v_{x,L})\rho_L]$, with the total pressures $p_T$ (eq. 38). The densities of the outer intermediate states are $\rho^*_K = \rho_K(S_K - v_{x,K})/(S_K - S_M)$ (eq. 43), and the Alfvén waves move at $S^*_L = S_M - |B_x|/(\rho^*_L)^{1/2}$ and $S^*_R = S_M + |B_x|/(\rho^*_R)^{1/2}$ (eq. 51). The total pressure of the intermediate states is the mean of its values from the two sides (eq. 41). Where $|\rho_K(S_K - v_{x,K})(S_K - S_M) - B_x^2|$ falls below $10^{-4}$ times this pressure, the degenerate case, the transverse velocity and field keep the values of the state $K$; otherwise eqs. 44 to 47 give them. The inner states follow from eqs. 59 to 63 and coincide with the outer ones for a vanishing $B_x$. The flux is that of the state between the waves that holds the face: $\mathbf{f}_L$ for $S_L \ge 0$, $\mathbf{f}_R$ for $S_R \le 0$, and otherwise the flux of an intermediate state, which the jump conditions build from the jumps across the waves, as in $\mathbf{f}^*_L = \mathbf{f}_L + S_L(\mathbf{q}^*_L - \mathbf{q}_L)$. The version for isothermal MHD follows [Mignone](#mignone-2007) (2007) (`src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/hlld_iso.cpp`). Its intermediate states take the density and normal momentum of the HLL average (eqs. 15, 17), and the Alfvén waves at $S^*_L$ and $S^*_R$ (eq. 29) separate two outer states (eq. 20) from a central one.

#### 2.5.4 Roe's method

Linearised solvers solve exactly an approximate, linear form of the equations, $\partial\mathbf{q}/\partial t + \mathsf{A}(\bar{\mathbf{q}})\,\partial\mathbf{q}/\partial x = 0$, with $\mathsf{A}$ the Jacobian $\partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{q}$ at a constant mean state $\bar{\mathbf{q}}$ ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, §4.3.2, eq. 55). The exact solution of such a linear system contains only discontinuities, and the challenge lies in the choice of the mean state. [Roe](#roe-1981) (1981) proposed a particularly useful one, which [Cargo and Gallice](#cargo-gallice-1997) (1997) extended to adiabatic MHD. It is defined in the primitive variables $\bar{\mathbf{w}} = (\bar\rho, \bar{\mathbf{v}}, \bar P, \bar B_y, \bar B_z)$ by $\bar\rho = (\rho_L\rho_R)^{1/2}$, by means of $\mathbf{v}$ and of the enthalpy $H = (E + P^*)/\rho$, from which the pressure follows, weighted by $\rho^{1/2}$, and by $\bar B_y = (\rho_R^{1/2}B_{y,L} + \rho_L^{1/2}B_{y,R})/(\rho_L^{1/2} + \rho_R^{1/2})$ and likewise $\bar B_z$, with the weights crossed (eq. 56). With the eigenvalues $\lambda^\alpha$ and the left and right eigenvectors $\mathbf{l}^\alpha$ and $\mathbf{r}^\alpha$ of $\mathsf{A}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})$, the flux is $\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{Roe}}_{i-1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}_{L,i-1/2} + \mathbf{f}_{R,i-1/2} + \sum_\alpha a^\alpha|\lambda^\alpha|\,\mathbf{r}^\alpha)$, with $a^\alpha = \mathbf{l}^\alpha \cdot \delta\mathbf{q}_{i-1/2}$ and $\delta\mathbf{q}_{i-1/2} = \mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2} - \mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2}$ (eqs. 57 to 59). The solver includes all characteristics, so that it diffuses intermediate waves such as contacts less than HLLE does, and it gives the flux exactly when the full solution is a single discontinuity ([Roe](#roe-1981) 1981). Being linear, however, it fails for some states, with negative densities or pressures in intermediate states ([Einfeldt et al.](#einfeldt-et-al-1991) 1991). Athena then computes the flux of that face with HLLE or a more accurate solver, such as an exact one, which tests show to be needed very rarely.

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, App. B) give the eigensystems of $\mathsf{A}$ in conserved variables. For adiabatic hydrodynamics, $\mathbf{U} = (\rho, \rho v_x, \rho v_y, \rho v_z, E)$, the eigenvalues are those of 2.4.4 with $a^2 = (\gamma - 1)(H - v^2/2) = \gamma P/\rho$, and the left eigenvectors of the sound waves carry $N_a = 1/(2a^2)$ (eqs. B1 to B4). They are those of [Roe](#roe-1981) (1981), except that the eigenvectors of the two shear waves are rescaled so that they have no singularities. Isothermal hydrodynamics drops the energy, with $C$ in place of $a$ (eqs. B5 to B7). For adiabatic MHD, $\mathbf{U} = (\rho, \rho\mathbf{v}, E, b_y, b_z)$, the matrix depends on the averaging of eq. 56 through the factors $X = [(b_{y,L} - b_{y,R})^2 + (b_{z,L} - b_{z,R})^2]/[2(\rho_L^{1/2} + \rho_R^{1/2})^2]$ and $Y = (\rho_L + \rho_R)/(2\bar\rho)$, which enter as $X' = (\gamma - 2)X$ and $Y' = (\gamma - 2)Y$, with $H = (E + P + b^2/2)/\rho$ (eqs. B8 to B16). The eigenvalues are those of 2.4.4 (eq. B17), with fast and slow speeds formed as in eq. A10 from $\tilde{a}^2 = (\gamma - 1)(H - v^2/2 - b^2/\rho) - X'$ and $\tilde{C}_A^2 = C_{Ax}^2 + b_\perp^{*2}/\rho$, where $b_\perp^{*2} = (\gamma - 1 - Y')(b_y^2 + b_z^2)$ (eqs. B18 to B20). The eigenvectors reuse eqs. A13 to A17 with $\tilde{a}$ in place of $a$ (eqs. B21 to B37). For isothermal MHD the speeds use $\tilde{C}^2 = C^2 + X$ and $b_\perp^{*2} = Y(b_y^2 + b_z^2)$ (eqs. B38 to B43). **[GK]** Eq. B15 divides by $2(\rho_L^{1/2} + \rho_R^{1/2})$ without the square. Only the square gives $X$ the dimension of a squared speed, which its place in $\tilde{a}^2$ requires, and the code squares as well.

Athena++ computes the same averages and eigensystems, with the factors $X$ and $Y$ of eqs. B15 and B16 (`src/hydro/rsolvers/hydro/roe.cpp`, `src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/roe_mhd.cpp`). Where all eigenvalues have one sign, the flux is that of the upwind state. Where a density in an intermediate state turns negative, the LLF flux of 2.5.2 replaces the flux of Roe at that face; the pressure is not checked. Athena++ thus falls back on LLF where Athena falls back on HLLE.

#### 2.5.5 The carbuncle

Strong planar shocks that move along a grid direction can make higher-order Godunov methods unstable in several dimensions ([Quirk](#quirk-1994) 1994): perturbations of the shock front grow to large amplitudes at the scale of the grid ([Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) 2008, App. C). A simple test in two dimensions shows this carbuncle instability. A uniform flow of high Mach number towards $+x$, with inflow at the right boundary, reflection elsewhere and density perturbations of $10^{-4}$ from cell to cell, lets the reflected shock develop it as it crosses the grid to the left. Radiative cooling behind the shock can amplify the effect ([Sutherland, Bisset and Bicknell](#sutherland-et-al-2003) 2003). The cause is the use of one-dimensional solvers in a multidimensional flow: for a shock aligned with the grid, the fluxes perpendicular to the shock front add too little dissipation, so that transverse perturbations grow instead of decaying. The cure is to detect shocks aligned with the grid and to add dissipation to the transverse fluxes. Athena uses a version of the H-correction of [Sanders, Morano and Druguet](#sanders-et-al-1998) (1998), most easily described with the fluxes of Roe. At the face $(i-1/2, j)$ in two dimensions, each $|\lambda^\alpha|$ of eq. 57 is replaced by $\max(|\lambda^\alpha|, \eta^H_{i-1/2,j})$, separately for each eigenvalue (eq. C1). The value $\eta^H_{i-1/2,j}$ is the largest $\eta$ on the five faces of a stencil shaped like the letter H, the face itself and the four faces normal to $y$ of the cells $i-1$ and $i$ (eq. C2), with $\eta_{i-1/2,j} = \tfrac{1}{2}|(u_{i,j} + C_{f,i,j}) - (u_{i-1,j} - C_{f,i-1,j})|$, where $u$ is the velocity and $C_f$ the fast speed normal to the face. The correction enters only the final multidimensional fluxes (2.6) and matters only in shocks, where it makes the transverse dissipation comparable to that along the direction of propagation. In three dimensions the stencil grows to nine faces, one H in each plane. Fluxes of the HLL type are less prone to the instability but not immune, and they take the correction through their speeds $b^\pm$ of eqs. 53 and 54. The correction is needed only for strong shocks aligned with the grid, rarely in applications of Athena, and it removes the instability in the strong shock test of Noh (2.16). [Sanders et al.](#sanders-et-al-1998) (1998, eq. 9) proposed several forms, which differed little in tests with planar shocks in two dimensions, so that Athena adopts only this one.

Athena++ has no H-correction. It offers instead the solvers LHLLC and LHLLD of low dissipation after [Minoshima and Miyoshi](#minoshima-miyoshi-2021) (2021), whose detector of shocks suppresses the instability, as the comment of the test problem below states (`src/hydro/rsolvers/hydro/lhllc.cpp`, `src/hydro/rsolvers/mhd/lhlld.cpp`). They change the speed of the contact and the middle pressure of HLLC and HLLD. For each face, `Hydro::CalculateVelocityDifferences` provides the difference $\Delta v_n$ of the normal velocity across it and the most negative difference $\Delta v_t$ of the transverse velocities along their own directions in the two neighbouring cells (`src/hydro/calculate_velocity_differences.cpp`); in one dimension $\Delta v_t$ is not computed. With the larger sound or fast speed $c$ of the two states, $\theta = \min\{1, [c - \min(\Delta v_n, 0)]/[c - \min(\Delta v_t, 0)]\}^4$, a fourth power that the comments call empirical. In LHLLC the contact moves at $S_M = [m_R v_{x,R} - m_L v_{x,L} - \theta(p_R - p_L)]/(m_R - m_L)$, with $m_K = \rho_K(\lambda_K - v_{x,K})$ for the outer speeds $\lambda_L = \lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_R = \lambda_{\max}$, and the middle pressure is $p_M = [m_R p_L - m_L p_R + \phi\,m_R m_L(v_{x,R} - v_{x,L})]/(m_R - m_L)$, with $\phi = \chi(2 - \chi)$ and $\chi = \min[1, \max(|\mathbf{v}_L|, |\mathbf{v}_R|)/c]$. For $\theta = \phi = 1$ these are the expressions of HLLC. The factor $\theta$ weakens the jump of pressure in the speed of the contact where the flow is compressed more strongly along the face than across it, and $\phi$ lowers the dissipation in the pressure at low Mach numbers. LHLLD applies the same two factors to $S_M$ and to the total pressure of the intermediate states, with $\chi$ formed from a speed that includes the magnetic and kinetic energies.

The problem generator `quirk` sets up the variant of the test of [Quirk](#quirk-1994) (1994) by [Hanawa, Mikami and Matsumoto](#hanawa-et-al-2008) (2008, §3.2), the first of their five shock tubes (`src/pgen/quirk.cpp`; `inputs/hydro/athinput.quirk`). On $[0, 1] \times [-0.0625, 0.0625]$ with $128 \times 16$ cells, outflow along $x_1$ and periodic boundaries along $x_2$, a shock at $x = 0.4$ separates $(\rho, v_x, P) = (3.692, -0.625, 26.85)$ on the left from $(1, -5, 0.6)$ on the right, with $\gamma = 5/3$. In alternate rows the cell of the shock holds $(3.557, -0.406, 25.54)$ instead, a perturbation from row to row that physically should not grow. The integrator VL2 with PLM and a Courant number of 0.4 runs to $t = 0.4$, in MHD with a uniform normal field `bx`, zero in the input file. Afterwards the code compares the entropy $P/\rho^\gamma$ in the cells of the initial position of the shock in an odd and an even row. A difference above 0.05 signals the carbuncle, and the difference is appended to `carbuncle-diff.dat`. The regression tests run the problem with the solvers HLLE, Roe, LLF, LHLLC and HLLC for hydrodynamics and HLLE, Roe, LLF, LHLLD and HLLD for MHD (`tst/regression/scripts/tests/hydro/hydro_carbuncle.py`, `tst/regression/scripts/tests/mhd/mhd_carbuncle.py`). They fail if the difference exceeds 0.05 for any solver but Roe and HLLC or HLLD, which are expected to suffer from the instability.

### 2.6 Time integration

#### 2.6.1 The algorithm in one dimension

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2008) (2008, §4) describe the integrator in one dimension first, as is customary, although for MHD this can mislead. In one dimension the constraint on the divergence only requires a constant longitudinal field, CT becomes unnecessary, and the discrete induction equations for area and volume averages coincide, so that algorithms for MHD in one dimension extend those for hydrodynamics simply. Tests in one dimension cannot reveal errors from a field that is not solenoidal, and a rigorous suite of tests for MHD must rest on multidimensional problems. The integrator in one dimension nevertheless introduces the reconstruction and the Riemann solvers, while the integrators in two and three dimensions differ substantially and are more complex (2.6.3, 2.6.4). In one dimension the equations of adiabatic MHD in Cartesian coordinates read $\partial\mathbf{q}/\partial t + \partial\mathbf{f}/\partial x = 0$ (eq. 29), with $\mathbf{q} = (\rho, M_x, M_y, M_z, E, B_y, B_z)$ and $\mathbf{f} = (\rho v_x, \rho v_x^2 + P + B^2/2 - B_x^2, \rho v_x v_y - B_x B_y, \rho v_x v_z - B_x B_z, (E + P^*)v_x - (\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{v})B_x, B_y v_x - B_x v_y, B_z v_x - B_x v_z)$, the vectors of 2.1.1 without their sixth component (eq. 30). Vectors in lower case hold the seven components of one dimension, and their components change with the direction: along $y$ the momenta are permuted to $M_y$, $M_z$, $M_x$, and the last two components become $B_z$ and $B_x$. The finite-volume discretisation of 2.1.3 gives $\mathbf{q}^{n+1}_i = \mathbf{q}^n_i - (\delta t/\delta x)(\mathbf{f}^{n+1/2}_{i+1/2} - \mathbf{f}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2})$ (eq. 31), with the averages $\mathbf{q}^n_i$ over the cell and the fluxes $\mathbf{f}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2}$ averaged over the step at the face (eqs. 32, 33). A Godunov method computes these fluxes with a Riemann solver ([Toro](#toro-1999) 1999; [LeVeque](#leveque-2002) 2002; Figure 2). A reconstruction builds the two states on either side of each face from the averages, for CTU in primitive variables and with an advance in time in characteristic variables (2.4). Because limiters keep the profiles free of oscillations, the two states differ except in smooth flow and so pose a Riemann problem, whose solution at the face gives the flux averaged over the step (2.5).

The algorithm repeats four steps until $t^{n+1} \ge t_f$ (§4.1, Figure 3). The reconstruction computes the states on both sides of all faces from the averages at $t^n$ (2.4). A Riemann solver computes the fluxes $\mathbf{f}^{n+1/2}_{i-1/2} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{q}_{L,i-1/2}, \mathbf{q}_{R,i-1/2}, B_{x,i-1/2})$ and receives the longitudinal field of the face as a parameter (2.5). Eq. 31 updates the cell-centred conserved variables and the transverse field. The time advances to $t^{n+1} = t^n + \delta t$, and the next step follows from the updated state as $\delta t = C_\circ\,\delta x/\max_i(|v^{n+1}_{x,i}| + C^{n+1}_{fx,i})$, with the Courant number $C_\circ \le 1$, the fast speed $C_{fx}$ along $x$ and the maximum taken over all cells (eq. 34). This only estimates the condition of stability, since the Riemann solver may use speeds other than those at the cell centres.

#### 2.6.2 A first-order unsplit scheme with CT

[Gardiner and Stone](#gardiner-stone-2005) (2005, §3) build a single-step integrator of first order in two dimensions on PPM, simple enough to develop and test the two central elements of their paper: the interface states of MHD and the systematic design of the algorithms for the corner fields (2.3.5). Apart from its update, it amounts to the first half of CTU with CT (2.6.3), and the tests of its three algorithms for the corner fields follow in 2.16. PPM, or its linear variant PLM, falls roughly into three steps: reconstruction in space, characteristic evolution and evaluation of the flux (§3.1; [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) 1984; [Saltzman](#saltzman-1994) 1994; [Miller and Colella](#miller-colella-2002) 2002). The first two estimate, on each side of a face, the state averaged over the step, from which a Riemann solver, exact or approximate, computes the flux. PPM computes the interface states in primitive variables and in one dimension. The equations of MHD in two dimensions, however, contain terms in $\partial B_x/\partial x$ and $\partial B_y/\partial y$ that the one-dimensional system lacks, and in primitive variables only the induction equation has them: $\partial B_x/\partial t + \partial_y(v_y B_x - B_y v_x) = 0$, $\partial B_y/\partial t + \partial_x(v_x B_y - B_x v_y) = 0$ and $\partial B_z/\partial t + \partial_x(v_x B_z - B_x v_z) + \partial_y(v_y B_z - B_y v_z) = 0$ (eqs. 25 to 27). The constraint $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ removes them from the equation for $B_z$, which becomes $\partial B_z/\partial t + \partial_x(v_x B_z) - B_x\,\partial v_z/\partial x + \partial_y(v_y B_z) - B_y\,\partial v_z/\partial y = 0$ (eq. 28), but not from those for $B_x$ and $B_y$.

Two thought experiments show that these terms are essential and at times dominant. A circularly polarised Alfvén wave oblique to the grid becomes stationary in a suitable frame. Then $\partial B_x/\partial x$ and $\partial B_y/\partial y$ differ from zero everywhere except at extrema, the velocities are comparable to the Alfvén speed, and the term $v_x\,\partial B_y/\partial y$ of eq. 25 must exactly balance $\partial_y(v_y B_x) - B_y\,\partial v_x/\partial y$ to keep the wave stationary, and likewise in eq. 26. In the advection of a weak field loop, with constant $\rho$, $P$ and $\mathbf{v} = v_x\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, $B_z = 0$ and $\beta = 2P/B^2 \gg 1$, a problem equivalent to advecting the $z$-component of the vector potential as a passive scalar, eqs. 25 and 26 reduce to $\partial B_x/\partial t - v_x\,\partial B_y/\partial y = 0$ and $\partial B_y/\partial t + v_x\,\partial B_y/\partial x = 0$ (eqs. 29, 30). The term $v_x\,\partial B_y/\partial y$ then governs the evolution of $B_x$ entirely.

Interface states that include a characteristic evolution must therefore include these multidimensional terms. For the reconstruction along $x$ in cell $i$, the primitive state $\mathbf{V}_i = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, B_x, B_y, B_z, P)_i$ of the cell averages is split into $B_{x,i}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}_i = (\rho, v_x, v_y, v_z, B_y, B_z, P)_i$. PPM then computes the interface states of $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}$ with a characteristic evolution of $\partial\tilde{\mathbf{V}}/\partial t + \mathsf{A}\,\partial\tilde{\mathbf{V}}/\partial x = \boldsymbol{\sigma}$, where $\mathsf{A}$ is the matrix of the linearised equations in these variables at $\mathbf{V}_i$, as in 2.4.4, and the only nonzero entry of the source $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is $v_y\,\partial B_x/\partial x$, in the equation for $B_y$ (eqs. 31 to 33). The source is held constant, evaluated as $v_{y,i}(B_{x,i+1/2} - B_{x,i-1/2})/\delta x$, so that eq. 31 includes all terms of eq. 26. The states $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^L_{i+1/2}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^R_{i-1/2}$ are completed with the fields of the faces, $B_{x,i+1/2}$ and $B_{x,i-1/2}$. The field on the faces thus brings a further advantage: the longitudinal field needs no reconstruction, so that it stays continuous across the face and, as no monotonicity constraint acts on it, keeps its extrema. Along $y$ the matrix of that direction replaces $\mathsf{A}$, and the source enters the equation for $B_x$ as $v_x\,\partial B_y/\partial y$.

## 3. Software architecture

Nine subjects group the software architecture. `plan.md` lists them, with the steps that write them.

[Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-2020) (2020, §1) explain three decisions that shape the framework of Athena++. First, the mesh is abstract and independent of the physics. The discretisations of the variables, such as volume averages at cell centres, area averages on faces and point values at cell centres or vertices, are built and stored on it, and AMR, boundary conditions and parallelisation by decomposing the domain are implemented for these variables without reference to any physics. New coordinates and new physics therefore work with AMR in any geometry at once, and mesh and physics can be developed apart, as in a version of the framework on Kokkos ([Edwards, Trott and Sunderland](#edwards-et-al-2014) 2014) for heterogeneous machines with GPUs. Second, the mesh is refined by blocks ([Stout et al.](#stout-et-al-1997) 1997) rather than by overlapping patches after [Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) (1984), as in Athena, PLUTO and Enzo. With patches, a refined region is covered by several levels, on which quantities computed from the conserved variables, the temperature for instance, may differ, and source terms that depend on them, such as cooling or reaction networks, then drive the levels apart. The overlap between the domains of the processes of the Message Passing Interface (MPI) on different levels also grows complex. In Athena++ each point of the domain belongs to exactly one level, and the levels communicate only through their boundaries, which simplifies the code and improves its performance and scaling. FLASH ([Fryxell et al.](#fryxell-et-al-2000) 2000) also refines by blocks, with the library PARAMESH ([MacNeice et al.](#macneice-et-al-2000) 2000), but Athena++ uses a framework of its own, to support variables on faces, as its MHD requires, to depend on fewer libraries and to gain performance at the cost of generality. Third, the steps of an algorithm, including the sends and receives of MPI, are not executed in a fixed order but assembled into lists of tasks, each of which runs once the tasks it depends on are complete. Computation can then overlap communication, which improves scaling on very many processors, and a calculation chooses its physics by the composition of its task lists. Separate regions can even run separate lists, for example a particle-in-cell code for the collisionless corona above an accretion disc in MHD ([Miller and Stone](#miller-stone-2000) 2000). Libraries such as Legion and Charm++ offer such dynamic execution among much else, but Athena++ implements only the scheduling it needs, in a few thousand lines of its own.

### 3.2 Arrays, the mesh and its blocks

All data of the grid lie in arrays of the template class `AthenaArray`, of one to six dimensions (`src/athena_arrays.hpp`, `AthenaArray`). The data occupy one contiguous block of memory, and the operator of function call addresses them with the last index fastest, so that `A(n,k,j,i)` is the element `i + N1*(j + N2*(k + N3*n))` of an array of size $N_4 \times N_3 \times N_2 \times N_1$. Loops with `i` innermost thus run through memory in order. An array records whether it is empty, allocated, or a shallow slice of another array. Allocated memory is set to zero. Copying an array copies its data, assigning one requires the target to be allocated with the same size already, and moving one hands over its data. Arrays may be allocated after their construction (`NewAthenaArray`) and released (`DeleteAthenaArray`). `SwapAthenaArray` exchanges the data of two arrays of equal size without copying them, and `InitWithShallowSlice` and `ShallowSlice3DToPencil` make views into part of another array, such as a range of its variables or one line of cells along $x_1$, again without copying. The type `Real` of the data is `double` by default and `float` when the code is configured for single precision, and `SIMD_WIDTH` sets the width of the vector lanes from the instruction set that the compiler targets (`src/athena.hpp`). The same header declares the structures for the size of a region and the logical location of a block in the tree of refinement, the containers `FaceField` and `EdgeField` for fields on faces and edges, the enumerations of indices and directions, and the types of the functions that users may enrol (3.6).
