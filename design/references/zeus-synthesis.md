# Synthesis of ZEUS-2D

This synthesis digests the three papers on ZEUS-2D, a code for radiation magnetohydrodynamics in two space dimensions. They are kept in `References/Zeus/Docs` and present, in turn, its hydrodynamic, magnetohydrodynamic and radiation hydrodynamic algorithms and tests: [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a, [doi:10.1086/191680](https://doi.org/10.1086/191680)), [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b, [doi:10.1086/191681](https://doi.org/10.1086/191681)) and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) (1992, [doi:10.1086/191682](https://doi.org/10.1086/191682)). The code itself is not among the sources. Additions found by web search, which fill gaps in the papers or check their claims, are marked **[GK]** and cited. A **[GK]** mark covers the rest of its paragraph or list item. A paper is cited by author and year, with the numbers of its sections, equations, figures and tables, as in [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a, §2, eq. 3). Within a paragraph, later citations of the same paper give the numbers alone.

## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Origin and publication

The three papers appeared together in volume 80 of the Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series, in June 1992, on pages 753–790, 791–818 and 819–845. Stone and Norman wrote the first two, and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) the third. The [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) gives James M. Stone and Michael L. Norman as members of the National Center for Supercomputing Applications and of the Department of Astronomy, both at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. It was received on 9 January 1991 and accepted on 29 October 1991. Its copyright is held by the American Astronomical Society, with all rights reserved, and the local copies are scans from the NASA Astrophysics Data System (ADS). The computations of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) ran on the Cray X-MP of the NCSA, and its authors acknowledge discussions with David Clarke, Chuck Evans, John Hawley, Dimitri Mihalas and Louis Wicker ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, acknowledgements). The [second paper](#stone-norman-1992b) gives the same affiliations and the same dates of receipt and acceptance. Its computations, too, ran on the Cray X-MP of the NCSA. Its authors thank the same colleagues, except Louis Wicker, for discussions, and Stone thanks Mihalas, the NCSA and the Department of Astronomy for financial support ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, acknowledgements). The third paper gives Stone and Norman at the NCSA, again with the Department of Astronomy, and Dimitri Mihalas at the Department of Astronomy, with the same dates. Its computations, too, ran on the Cray X-MP of the NCSA. Its authors thank Barbara Mihalas for discussions and Randall Bramley for advice on sparse matrix solvers, Stone thanks the Department of Astronomy and the NCSA for financial support, and Mihalas thanks the University of Illinois for substantial research funds ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, acknowledgements).

ZEUS-2D descends from ZEUS, a code for astrophysical fluid dynamics that began as Norman's doctoral project, on how rotating interstellar clouds collapse and evolve as stars form ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §3; [Norman](#norman-1980) 1980; [Norman, Wilson and Barton](#norman-et-al-1980) 1980). Its algorithms were later improved and extended ([Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) 1986), and it served widely in models of jets in radio galaxies ([Norman et al.](#norman-et-al-1981) 1981, [1982](#norman-et-al-1982)). Last, David Clarke added an algorithm for MHD, for jets in radio galaxies held together by magnetic fields ([Clarke](#clarke-1988) 1988; [Clarke, Norman and Burns](#clarke-et-al-1986) 1986, [1989](#clarke-et-al-1989)). ZEUS-2D keeps ZEUS as its foundation but is a new code. It differences the equations in a covariant formalism that admits any orthogonal coordinate system, has a simpler and more accurate MHD algorithm than the later versions of ZEUS, and improves the management, control and portability of the code.

**[GK]** Stone's doctoral thesis at the University of Illinois, supervised by Norman and Mihalas, describes in detail how the code was developed and tested, and applies it to mass outflows from star-forming regions ([Stone](#stone-1990) 1990). The ADS holds the thesis under two records, [1990PhDT.......418S](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990PhDT.......418S/abstract), which names the supervisors, and [1990PhDT........16S](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990PhDT........16S/abstract), which gives the abstract.

### 1.2 Aims and scope

Hydrodynamic flows occur throughout astrophysics, from compact objects and stars up to interstellar and intergalactic gas ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §1). Their equations can rarely be solved analytically without restrictive assumptions, so that models rely on numerical solutions. Of the many numerical methods, finite differences are among the most developed and straightforward: robust, easy to code, efficient, and easy to modify and extend with new algorithms. A frontier of modelling is to include every physical effect that may matter for the dynamics, such as the Galactic magnetic field, which can dominate how the interstellar medium evolves, or the strong magnetic and radiation fields of the central object in accretion flows. Realistic models then need methods that treat all these effects self-consistently.

ZEUS-2D is a general-purpose code for astrophysical fluid dynamics in two space dimensions, with magnetic fields and radiation transfer ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §1). Generality drove its development, so that it can model a wide variety of systems. Its hydrodynamic algorithms were chosen as the most accurate ones that still let more complex physics be added easily and self-consistently. The MHD assumes flux freezing but is otherwise general. It is designed to treat all families of MHD waves properly, and to keep the evolved field free of divergence at all times. The radiation transfer uses a full transport algorithm. Closing the equations of radiation hydrodynamics with a solution of the transfer equation avoids the diffusion approximation, so that the method applies to optically thin and thick media alike, without flux limiters. Self-gravity enters through the Poisson equation, whose discretisation yields a sparse banded matrix that is solved iteratively (abstract).

ZEUS is an explicit Eulerian finite-difference code in two dimensions, built for simplicity, robustness and speed ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §3). Simple algorithms make for simple coding. Improvements can then be built in and tested quickly, new physics is easy to add, and a problem can be solved with different algorithms to tell the features of a solution that the numerics cause from those that are physical. More complex algorithms, such as higher-order Godunov methods like the piecewise parabolic method (PPM) of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984), give better results for purely hydrodynamic problems, but often need years of development before new physics can be added to them. The speed of the ZEUS algorithms allows large problems at high resolution, which is among the most important considerations in finding the converged solution of a physical problem. The summary of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) sharpens the argument: Godunov codes such as PPM do better on strong shocks, but new physics such as radiation MHD is very hard to add to them self-consistently, which rules them out for ZEUS-2D (§6).

In many astrophysical systems, magnetic fields can be crucial to the dynamics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §1). MHD processes matter in the solar corona, in the interstellar medium with its star-forming regions, in the outflows and jets of protostars, in accretion onto magnetised white dwarfs and neutron stars, and in extragalactic radio sources, and models of these systems must treat MHD effects self-consistently. The algorithms for the magnetic field stand on their own, independent of the hydrodynamic ones. They could be built into almost any explicit Eulerian finite-difference code, and, with some changes, into Lagrangian or implicit codes. The paper describes them in enough detail for others to implement or modify them, and reports an extensive series of tests for comparison with other schemes.

Two-dimensional radiation hydrodynamics was a frontier of theoretical astrophysics, as one-dimensional hydrodynamic models fail for many systems, such as accretion onto compact objects or the radiation-driven winds of hot stars that rotate rapidly or carry magnetic fields ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §1). The radiation module, the last major algorithm of the project, makes ZEUS-2D a code for radiation MHD (RMHD). Its closure, a tensor variable Eddington factor that the formally solved transfer equation provides, follows the practice of calculations of stellar atmospheres, while most multidimensional calculations of radiation hydrodynamics relied on the diffusion approximation, with an ad hoc flux limiter where the medium turns optically thin.

Of the physical processes that the equations of hydrodynamics leave out, the papers add magnetic fields and radiation transport, but not others, such as heat conduction ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §2). They present no applications to particular astrophysical problems, and refer to [Stone](#stone-1990) (1990) for them (§1), as the [second paper](#stone-norman-1992b) repeats ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §1). Its MHD leaves out resistivity and ambipolar diffusion, although adding them would be straightforward (§2).

### 1.3 The three papers

The algorithms of ZEUS-2D fall into three areas, and each paper treats one ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, abstract, §1). [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a) describe the hydrodynamics, which forms the foundation for the rest, [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b) the MHD, and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) (1992) the radiation hydrodynamics. Each paper calls the other two by number: the first [Paper II](#stone-norman-1992b) and [Paper III](#stone-et-al-1992a), the second [Paper I](#stone-norman-1992a) and [Paper III](#stone-et-al-1992a), and the third [Paper I](#stone-norman-1992a) and [Paper II](#stone-norman-1992b). The division follows the subject matter, and lets readers who are interested only in MHD or radiation skip the rest. Each paper is meant to be detailed enough for others to implement the methods, and to serve as a basis for comparing the results of other methods.

The first paper, [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a), runs to 38 pages, with 148 numbered equations, 18 figures, 3 tables and 39 references. It writes down the equations of hydrodynamics that the code solves (§2), traces the roots of ZEUS-2D and the reasons for its choice of algorithms (§3), describes the code in detail over most of its length (§4), presents its tests (§5) and summarises (§6). Three appendices give the vector and tensor operators in covariant form, the tensor artificial viscosity, and some difference formulae.

The second paper, [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b), runs to 28 pages, with 55 numbered equations, 19 figures, 3 tables and 25 references. It writes down the equations of MHD (§2), describes the algorithms for the magnetic field and how they fit into the rest of ZEUS-2D (§3), presents the tests (§4) and summarises (§5). Its abstract states the main result. Constrained transport (CT) keeps the evolved field free of divergence at all times, but only a careful choice of how the electromotive forces are computed lets it propagate every family of MHD waves accurately, shear Alfvén waves in particular. A new way of computing these forces, by the method of characteristics (MOC), achieves this, and an extensive series of tests shows that the hybrid MOC-CT method evolves all wave families accurately. The tests range from the advection of a pulse of field to coronal transients in two dimensions, and the summary calls MOC-CT an important and necessary improvement for all MHD wave modes (§4, §5).

The third paper, [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) (1992), runs to 27 pages, with 118 numbered equations, 14 figures, no tables and 20 references. It derives the moment equations of radiation and couples them to the gas dynamics (§2), solves the moment equations (§3), solves the transfer equation for their closure (§4), describes the changes to ZEUS-2D (§5), tests the solvers of the moment and the transfer equations (§6, §7) and summarises (§8). An appendix gives some difference formulae. In the method of its abstract, a full transport algorithm in two dimensions evolves the moment equations, closed by a tensor variable Eddington factor, computed by quadrature over angle from the specific intensity, which comes from solving the transfer equation formally in two dimensions by short characteristics.

### 2.1 The equations and the split solution procedure

#### 2.1.1 The equations of hydrodynamics

Astrophysical plasmas are dilute mixtures of electrons, ions, atoms and molecules ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §2). Their microscopic state is given at each instant by the distribution function in phase space, which evolves by the Boltzmann equation. When particles collide so often that their mean free path is much shorter than the length scales of the system, a statistical description by conserved macroscopic quantities suffices. These evolve by the velocity moments of the Boltzmann equation, and successively higher moments yield the equations of hydrodynamics. The paper does not derive them rigorously, but notes that collisions are frequent enough for this continuum description in many astrophysical problems of gas dynamics.

The equations are written in Lagrangian form, with the comoving derivative $D/Dt \equiv \partial/\partial t + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 4). The variables evolved are the mass density $\rho$, the velocity $\mathbf{v}$ and the internal energy per unit volume, $e$. They obey the continuity equation $D\rho/Dt + \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ (eq. 1), the equation of motion $\rho\, D\mathbf{v}/Dt = -\nabla p - \rho \nabla \Phi$ (eq. 2) and the internal energy equation $\rho\, D(e/\rho)/Dt = -p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$ (eq. 3). Two relations close the system: an equation of state for the gas pressure $p$ as a function of $\rho$ and $e$, or equivalently of $\rho$ and the temperature, and the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G \rho$ for the gravitational potential $\Phi$ (eq. 5). The equations carry neither viscous stresses nor a heat flux. The continuity equation states the conservation of mass, and the equation of motion that of momentum.

For the energy, the internal energy equation is solved rather than an equation that conserves the total energy, so that the internal energy, and with it the temperature, is more accurate in highly supersonic flows ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §2). **[GK]** At high Mach numbers the kinetic energy dominates. An internal energy found by subtracting it from the total energy then suffers from the truncation errors of the kinetic energy relative to the total, and can even turn negative (2.5 of `computational-hydrodynamics-synthesis.md`). Evolving $e$ itself avoids the subtraction. The price is that eq. 3, with its term $-p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$, is not in conservation form, so that conservation of the total energy to round-off is no longer built into the scheme.

#### 2.1.2 Operator splitting

ZEUS-2D integrates the equations explicitly in time, by finite differences and operator splitting ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4). The evolution of the state $y$, written as $\partial y/\partial t = \mathcal{L}(y)$ (eq. 6), is broken into parts by splitting the operator into terms, $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_1 + \mathcal{L}_2 + \dots$, which are applied in turn, each to the result of the one before: $(y^1 - y^0)/\Delta t = L_1(y^0)$, then $(y^2 - y^1)/\Delta t = L_2(y^1)$, and so on, where $L_i$ is a difference approximation to $\mathcal{L}_i$ (eqs. 7 to 9). The split solution only approximates that of the full nonlinear, multidimensional operator, and the order of the parts is largely free. Numerical experiments have shown, however, that such a sequence of partial updates is more accurate than a single update from the old state, and that the order matters little ([Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984b) 1984b; [Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) 1986).

The parts form two steps. The source step solves difference approximations to $\rho\, \partial\mathbf{v}/\partial t = -\nabla p - \rho \nabla \Phi - \nabla \cdot \mathbf{Q}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 10) and $\partial e/\partial t = -p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{Q} : \nabla \mathbf{v}$ (eq. 11), where $\mathbf{Q}$ is the tensor of an artificial viscosity. The transport step accounts for advection. It solves difference approximations to integral equations for a zone of volume $V$ and surface $\partial V$: $d/dt \int_V \rho\, dV = -\int_{\partial V} \rho(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_g) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ for the mass (eq. 12), and the same with $\rho\mathbf{v}$ for the momentum and with $e$ for the internal energy (eqs. 13 and 14). Here $\mathbf{v}_g$ is the velocity of the grid, which may move. The integral equations follow from the continuity equation and the divergence theorem. **[GK]** With eq. 1, eqs. 2 and 3 take the forms $\partial(\rho\mathbf{v})/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}) = -\nabla p - \rho\nabla\Phi$, in Cartesian coordinates, and $\partial e/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (e\mathbf{v}) = -p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$. Their flux terms make up the transport step, and their remaining terms, with the viscous ones, the source step. For a zone whose surface moves with $\mathbf{v}_g$, the rate of change of $\int_V q\, dV$ gains the flux $q\mathbf{v}_g \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ through the moving surface, by Reynolds' transport theorem, so that only the flux relative to the grid remains.

The source step thus contains terms that the Euler equations lack: the stresses and the heating of an artificial viscosity ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4). Like the viscosity of a real fluid, it smooths discontinuities, where the difference equations would otherwise fail. A real shock is a few mean free paths wide, but the artificial viscosity is made strong enough to spread a shock over several zones. A nonlinear form, large in shocks and negligible elsewhere, keeps its effect on smooth flow small. With it, the difference equations give the correct Rankine–Hugoniot jump conditions and the correct shock speed ([von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) 1950). Since a shock is then several zones wide, its internal structure cannot be studied, unless an adaptive mesh resolves it with very small zones.

Solving the integral forms allows conservative differencing, which keeps the total amount of each advected quantity on the grid to round-off ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4). The fluxes are measured relative to a grid that moves with $\mathbf{v}_g$ against a fixed background, so that the grid can follow global features of a flow, such as collapse or expansion, and resolve them better. In curvilinear coordinates, the scalar divergence theorem misses the terms that the gradient operator produces on the unit vectors of the velocity components. These terms act as inertial forces, and ZEUS-2D adds them in the source step.

### 2.2 The staggered mesh and the covariant formalism

#### 2.2.1 The staggered mesh

All dependent variables are functions of two spatial coordinates, $x_1$ and $x_2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). In Cartesian coordinates this means slab symmetry, and in cylindrical or spherical coordinates axial symmetry. The restriction saves memory and computing time. The methods extend readily to three dimensions, and ZEUS-3D already does so ([Clarke, Stone and Norman](#clarke-et-al-1990) 1990). Vectors and tensors keep all their components, each a function of the two coordinates, so that the velocity has a third component $v_3$ as well. This 2.5-dimensional dynamics is essential for self-consistent rotating flows and for the full set of MHD wave modes.

The domain is divided into zones, numbered by $i$ from $ii$ to $io$ in $x_1$, for inner and outer, and by $j$ from $ji$ to $jo$ in $x_2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, fig. 1). Two rows of ghost zones lie beyond each boundary. Their values come from the boundary conditions of §4.5, not from the evolution equations. Two rows are needed to give derivatives at a boundary, as inflow conditions and the higher-order interpolation of the transport step require.

Positions are given by coordinate arrays ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1, fig. 2). The a-mesh holds the zone boundaries, $x1a_i$ being the left boundary of zone $i$ and $x2a_j$ the lower boundary of zone $j$, and the b-mesh holds the zone centres $(x1b_i, x2b_j)$. The spacings $dx1a_i = x1a_{i+1} - x1a_i$ and $dx1b_i = x1b_i - x1b_{i-1}$, and likewise $dx2a_j$ and $dx2b_j$, can be chosen freely, which allows nonuniform grids. As the grid must be orthogonal, a spacing chosen in $x_1$ holds across the whole range of $x_2$, and vice versa.

The mesh is staggered ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). Scalars, and the diagonal components of even-rank tensors, sit at the zone centres. Vector components sit on the faces normal to them, and the off-diagonal components of even-rank tensors at the corners. Of the hydrodynamic variables, the density $d_{i,j}$, the internal energy density $e_{i,j}$, the rotational velocity $v3_{i,j}$ and the potential $\Phi_{i,j}$ lie at the zone centre $(x1b_i, x2b_j)$, while $v1_{i,j}$ lies at $(x1a_i, x2b_j)$ and $v2_{i,j}$ at $(x1b_i, x2a_j)$ (fig. 3). Scalars and vectors share indices instead of using half indices such as $v1_{i+1/2,j}$. The face with the same index as a zone centre always lies at the smaller coordinate, as $x1a_i < x1b_i$ and $x2a_j < x2b_j$. With the usual multidimensional form of the artificial viscosity (§4.3), the hydrodynamics needs no tensors of rank two. They appear in radiation hydrodynamics and in the tensor artificial viscosity of appendix B.

The staggering has two advantages ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). Most spatial differences are centred, as a vector formed from differences of scalars lies midway between them, and centred differences are formally of second order, against first order for one-sided differences. The transport step also needs fewer interpolations, since velocities on the faces give the flux into or out of a zone directly. On the other hand, other parts of the calculation need extra averages, such as forming the momentum from density and velocity, which can limit the overall accuracy and the rate of convergence, the rate at which the error falls as the number of grid points grows ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b). Some boundary conditions also become harder to apply. The authors judge the advantages to outweigh these drawbacks.

#### 2.2.2 Domains and grid motion

A domain need not be globally rectangular ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). For an accretion disc around a central object in cylindrical coordinates, the origin $r = z = 0$ is better left out, to avoid numerical trouble near the surface of the central object, and a staircase in the boundary near the origin achieves this. ZEUS-2D allows it by making the first and last index in each direction an array over the other direction: $ii(j)$, $io(j)$, $ji(i)$ and $jo(i)$.

The mesh can also move against a stationary background, with grid velocities on the zone faces, where the fluid velocities lie ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). Once a time step is complete, the zone boundaries of the a-mesh and the centres of the b-mesh are moved by the grid velocities. They can be prescribed freely, and independently in the two directions. Setting them equal to the fluid velocity along a strip of zones makes the code semi-Lagrangian, and in a collapse problem the grid can follow an approximation to the collapse. Several such choices are built in. The motion must keep the grid orthogonal, so the grid velocity in the 1-direction may vary only with $x_1$, and that in the 2-direction only with $x_2$. This is less general than truly two-dimensional adaptive meshes, such as those of [Berger and Oliger](#berger-oliger-1984) (1984) and [Fiedler](#fiedler-1990) (1990, private communication), and limits how well a moving grid can follow two-dimensional flows. The authors see a more sophisticated algorithm as a very profitable addition.

#### 2.2.3 The covariant formalism

Equations written and differenced in one chosen coordinate system tie the code to that system, and moving it to another is usually lengthy ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.2). ZEUS-2D instead differences the equations in a covariant form. Every vector and tensor operator is written without reference to particular coordinates, through the scale factors of the metric, which is diagonal for an orthogonal coordinate system (appendix A), and these covariant equations are differenced with the scale factors in place. The difference equations then hold in any orthogonal coordinate system, and only the scale factors depend on it. For a mesh that does not move, they are computed once, at the start.

Three coordinate systems are used, in each of which $x_3$ is the ignorable coordinate: Cartesian, with $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (x, y, z)$ and scale factors $(h_1, h_2, h_3) = (1, 1, 1)$; cylindrical, with $(z, r, \phi)$ and $(1, 1, r)$; and spherical polar, with $(r, \theta, \phi)$ and $(1, r, r\sin\theta)$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 15 to 17). In all three, $h_1 = 1$, $h_2$ is a function of $x_1$, and $h_3$ is a function of $x_1$ times a function of $x_2$. The code stores them in that form, as $h_1 = g_1 = 1$, $h_2 = g_2(x_1)$ and $h_3 = g_{31}(x_1)\,g_{32}(x_2)$, so that $g_1$ can be dropped (eqs. 18 to 20). Each of $g_2$, $g_{31}$ and $g_{32}$ has a value on the a-mesh and one on the b-mesh (fig. 4), and so do their derivatives. The derivatives $\partial g_2/\partial x_1$, $\partial g_{31}/\partial x_1$ and $\partial g_{32}/\partial x_2$ vanish in Cartesian coordinates, are 0, 0 and 1 in cylindrical ones, and 1, 1 and $\cos\theta$ in spherical polar ones (eqs. 21 to 23). The factored form saves memory and arithmetic, but rules out coordinate systems whose scale factors do not fit it, such as oblate spheroidal coordinates, where $h_1 \neq 1$, although they would suit rotationally flattened stars. Extending the implementation to them would be straightforward.

Curvilinear coordinates bring the risk of differencing errors near coordinate singularities ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.2). In spherical polar coordinates, the simple radial difference $r^{-2}\,\partial(r^2F)/\partial r \approx (r_i^2F_i - r_{i-1}^2F_{i-1})/\{[(r_i + r_{i-1})/2]^2(r_i - r_{i-1})\}$ (eq. 24) does not reproduce, near the origin, the difference over the volume, $(r_i^2F_i - r_{i-1}^2F_{i-1})/(r_i^3/3 - r_{i-1}^3/3)$ (eq. 25). The two agree at large radii, but differ badly as $r_{i-1} \to 0$, and differences in the polar angle suffer likewise. Using the volume difference is an instance of a general way of reducing difference errors near coordinate singularities, which [Evans](#evans-1986) (1986) named coordinate regularisation, and [Mönchmeyer and Müller](#monchmeyer-muller-1989) (1989) give a general method for differencing conservation laws on non-Cartesian meshes. ZEUS-2D does not regularise in full generality. It uses volume differences wherever they are needed, through the volume factors $dvl1$ and $dvl2$: $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ in Cartesian coordinates, $\Delta z$ and $\Delta(r^2/2)$ in cylindrical ones, and $\Delta(r^3/3)$ and $\Delta(-\cos\theta)$ in spherical polar ones (eqs. 26 to 28). Like the scale factors, they are defined on both meshes. Terms of the form $(h_2h_3)^{-1}\,\partial F/\partial x_1$ and $h_3^{-1}\,\partial F/\partial x_2$ are then differenced as $\Delta F/(g_{32}\,dvl1)$ and $\Delta F/(g_{31}\,dvl2)$ (eq. 29).

**[GK]** The denominators of eqs. 24 and 25 differ by exactly $(r_i - r_{i-1})^3/12$. The two formulae thus agree to relative order $(\Delta r/r)^2$ at large radii, but in the innermost zone, where $r_{i-1} = 0$, they differ by a factor of 4/3. Eq. 25 is the exact average of the divergence over the shell between $r_{i-1}$ and $r_i$, by the divergence theorem. With $g_2 = g_{31} = r$ and $g_{32} = \sin\theta$ in spherical polar coordinates, and $g_{32} = r$ in cylindrical ones, $dvl1$ is in all three systems the difference of $\int g_2 g_{31}\,dx_1$, and $dvl2$ that of $\int g_{32}\,dx_2$. A zone thus has the volume $dvl1\,dvl2\,\Delta x_3$, and eq. 29 is the volume difference of the operator it replaces, since $(h_2h_3)^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_1 = g_{32}^{-1}(g_2g_{31})^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_1$, and likewise in $x_2$.

#### 2.2.4 Operators in orthogonal coordinates

Appendix A collects coordinate-free expressions for the operators the papers use, for an orthogonal metric $g_{ij} = \mathrm{diag}(h_1^2, h_2^2, h_3^2)$ with determinant $g = h_1^2h_2^2h_3^2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. A, eq. 109). They hold for any number of dimensions and any form of the scale factors, so that they also serve calculations in three dimensions and in other orthogonal coordinates. They follow from the covariant forms of the operators, with physical components related to covariant ones by $a_i = h_ia_{(i)}$ and $T_{ij} = h_ih_jT_{(i,j)}$, and to contravariant ones by $a^i = a_{(i)}/h_i$ and $T^{ij} = T_{(i,j)}/(h_ih_j)$ (eqs. 113 and 114). Texts on tensor calculus give them, as do [Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) (1984). In physical components, the gradient of a scalar is $(h_1^{-1}\,\partial f/\partial x_1,\ h_2^{-1}\,\partial f/\partial x_2,\ h_3^{-1}\,\partial f/\partial x_3)$ (eq. 115), the divergence of a vector is $(h_1h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_2h_3a_1)/\partial x_1 + \partial(h_1h_3a_2)/\partial x_2 + \partial(h_1h_2a_3)/\partial x_3]$ (eq. 116), and the Laplacian of a scalar is the divergence of its gradient (eq. 117). The curl has the component $(\nabla\times\mathbf{a})_1 = (h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_3a_3)/\partial x_2 - \partial(h_2a_2)/\partial x_3]$, and the others by cyclic permutation (eqs. 118 to 120).

A vector's gradient is a tensor of rank two, built with the Christoffel symbols of the second kind ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. A). For an orthogonal metric, only $\Gamma^i_{ii} = \tfrac12\,\partial(\ln g_{ii})/\partial x_i$, $\Gamma^i_{jj} = -\tfrac12 g_{ii}^{-1}\,\partial g_{jj}/\partial x_i$ and $\Gamma^i_{ij} = \Gamma^i_{ji} = \tfrac12\,\partial(\ln g_{ii})/\partial x_j$ are nonzero (eqs. 121 to 123). The components follow the pattern $(\nabla\mathbf{a})_{(11)} = h_1^{-1}\,\partial a_1/\partial x_1 + (h_1h_2)^{-1}a_2\,\partial h_1/\partial x_2 + (h_1h_3)^{-1}a_3\,\partial h_1/\partial x_3$ on the diagonal and $(\nabla\mathbf{a})_{(12)} = h_1^{-1}\,\partial a_2/\partial x_1 - (h_1h_2)^{-1}a_1\,\partial h_1/\partial x_2$ off it, with the others by permutation of the indices (eqs. 124 to 126). The first index thus gives the direction of differentiation, and the second the component. **[GK]** The general expression stated before them, $(h_ih_j)^{-1}a_{i;j}$, has the indices of $a_{i;j}$ the other way round. With $a_{j;i}$ it gives these components.

The Laplacian of a vector follows from $\nabla^2\mathbf{a} = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{a}) - \nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{a}$ and the expressions above ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 127 to 129). The divergence of a tensor of rank two, $T^{ij}_{;j} = g^{-1/2}\,\partial(g^{1/2}T^{ij})/\partial x_j + \Gamma^i_{jk}T^{kj}$, contracts its second index. Its first physical component is $(h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_2h_3T_{11}/h_1)/\partial x_1 + \partial(h_3T_{12})/\partial x_2 + \partial(h_2T_{13})/\partial x_3]$ plus terms in the derivatives of the scale factors: $-T_{22}(h_1h_2)^{-1}\,\partial h_2/\partial x_1 - T_{33}(h_1h_3)^{-1}\,\partial h_3/\partial x_1 + (T_{12} + T_{21})(h_1h_2)^{-1}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_2 + (T_{13} + T_{31})(h_1h_3)^{-1}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_3$, and one in $T_{11}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_1$. The other components follow by permutation (eqs. 130 to 132). For a symmetric tensor the mixed terms add in pairs. For an antisymmetric one they cancel, and its diagonal vanishes. **[GK]** In each component, the term in the derivative of the scale factor of its own direction carries the wrong sign. Eq. 130 has $-T_{11}h_1^{-2}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_1$, and eqs. 131 and 132 have the like terms in $T_{22}$ and $T_{33}$, where a plus is right. With only $T_{11}$ nonzero, in a stretched Cartesian coordinate, the paper's form gives $h_1^{-1}\,\partial T_{11}/\partial x_1 - 2T_{11}h_1^{-2}\,\partial h_1/\partial x_1$ instead of $h_1^{-1}\,\partial T_{11}/\partial x_1$. In the three coordinate systems of ZEUS-2D these derivatives vanish, since $h_1 = 1$, $h_2$ does not depend on $x_2$ and nothing depends on $x_3$, so that the code is not affected.

Appendix C writes out the zone-centred differences that the tensor artificial viscosity of appendix B needs ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. C). The divergence of the velocity is $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j} = (g2a_{i+1}\,g31a_{i+1}\,v1_{i+1,j} - g2a_i\,g31a_i\,v1_{i,j})/dvl1a_i + (g32a_{j+1}\,v2_{i,j+1} - g32a_j\,v2_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dvl2a_j)$ (eq. 145), a difference of the fluxes through the faces of the zone over its volume factors. The diagonal components of the velocity gradient are $(\nabla v_{(11)})_{i,j} = (v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})/dx1a_i$ (eq. 146), $(\nabla v_{(22)})_{i,j} = (v2_{i,j+1} - v2_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dx2a_j) + \bar v_1\,(\partial g2b_i/\partial x_1)/g2b_i$ (eq. 147) and $(\nabla v_{(33)})_{i,j} = \bar v_1\,(\partial g31b_i/\partial x_1)/g31b_i + \bar v_2\,(\partial g32b_j/\partial x_2)/(g2b_i\,g32b_j)$ (eq. 148). Here $\bar v_1 = (v1_{i,j} + v1_{i+1,j})/2$ and $\bar v_2 = (v2_{i,j} + v2_{i,j+1})/2$ average the velocities on opposite faces of the zone. **[GK]** These are eqs. 116 and 124 in two dimensions, differenced on the staggered mesh, with the divergence in the volume form of eq. 29.

### 2.3 The source step and artificial viscosity

#### 2.3.1 Substeps and forces

The source step solves difference forms of eqs. 10 and 11, the sources and sinks of the variables, split from the advection that the transport step adds afterwards ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3). It has three substeps of its own. The first updates the velocities by the pressure gradient, gravity and the curvature terms, the inertial forces of curvilinear coordinates. The second adds the stresses and heating of the artificial viscosity, from the velocities after the first. The third adds the compressional heating. The superscripts $n$, $n + a$, $n + b$ and $n + c$ mark the values when the source step begins and after each substep.

In the first substep, $v1$ changes at the rate $-(p_{i,j} - p_{i-1,j})/[dx1b_i\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i-1,j})/2] - (\Phi_{i,j} - \Phi_{i-1,j})/dx1b_i + \bar v_2^2\,(\partial g2a_i/\partial x_1)/g2a_i + \bar v_3^2\,(\partial g31a_i/\partial x_1)/g31a_i$, with all values at time level $n$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 30). Here $\bar v_2$ averages $v2$ over the four faces next to the face of $v1$, and $\bar v_3$ averages $v3$ over the two zones on either side of it. The second component changes at the rate $-(p_{i,j} - p_{i,j-1})/[g2b_i\,dx2b_j\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i,j-1})/2] - (\Phi_{i,j} - \Phi_{i,j-1})/(g2b_i\,dx2b_j) + \bar v_3^2\,(\partial g32a_j/\partial x_2)/(g2b_i\,g32a_j)$, with $\bar v_3$ now averaged in $x_2$ (eq. 31). The staggering thus calls for averages of the density, and of the velocity components that do not lie on the face being updated. The pressure comes from the equation of state when the source step begins, mostly the ideal gas law $p = (\gamma - 1)e$, and the potential from the Poisson equation with the density of that time (§4.7). The curvature terms appear only in curvilinear coordinates, as inertial forces that the geometry adds to the divergence of the momentum flux. **[GK]** In spherical polar coordinates, with $g_2 = g_{31} = r$ and $g_{32} = \sin\theta$, they are $(v_\theta^2 + v_\phi^2)/r$ in the radial and $v_\phi^2\cot\theta/r$ in the polar equation of motion, the centrifugal terms of these coordinates.

#### 2.3.2 Artificial viscosity

The second substep adds the artificial viscosity, whose differencing depends on its form ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3.2). The customary form is that of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) (1950), derived from planar shocks in one dimension. Their insight was that a nonlinear viscous pressure, acting only in compression, gives the correct jump in entropy and the correct shock speed while staying negligible away from shocks: $q = l^2\rho(\partial v/\partial x)^2$ where $\partial v/\partial x < 0$, and $q = 0$ elsewhere, with a constant length $l$ that sets its strength (eq. 32). In several dimensions, a separate scalar viscous pressure is defined for each direction, at the zone centres: $q1_{i,j} = C_2\,d_{i,j}(v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})^2$ where $v1_{i+1,j} < v1_{i,j}$, and zero otherwise, and $q2_{i,j}$ likewise from $v2$ (eqs. 33 and 34). The dimensionless $C_2 = l/\Delta x$ counts roughly the zones over which a shock is spread, and is typically about 3. The velocities then change at the rates $-(q1_{i,j} - q1_{i-1,j})/[dx1b_i\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i-1,j})/2]$ and $-(q2_{i,j} - q2_{i,j-1})/[g2b_i\,dx2b_j\,(d_{i,j} + d_{i,j-1})/2]$, and the internal energy at the rate $-q1_{i,j}(v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})/dx1a_i - q2_{i,j}(v2_{i,j+1} - v2_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dx2a_j)$ (eqs. 35 to 37). **[GK]** This heating is never negative, since each $q$ is nonzero only where its velocity difference is negative. With eq. 32 and $\partial v/\partial x \approx \Delta v/\Delta x$, eqs. 33 and 34 call for $C_2^2$ rather than $C_2$, given $C_2 = l/\Delta x$, and appendix B, which sets $l = C_2\,\Delta x$, keeps the square. Later copies of the code settle the question in favour of eqs. 33 and 34. Two survive on the web: release 2.0.3 of the Laboratory for Computational Astrophysics, of 2007, and Stone's own version 2.0, of 2003. In both, the quadratic viscous pressure is a constant `qcon` times $\rho(\Delta v)^2$, with `qcon` = 2 by default, so that `qcon` stands for $(l/\Delta x)^2$ and the definition $C_2 = l/\Delta x$ is the slip.

This extension to several dimensions is ad hoc ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3.2). A rigorous treatment defines an isotropic tensor of artificial viscous stress. The scalar form serves in Cartesian coordinates, but can cause serious trouble in curvilinear ones ([Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) 1979): radial inflow along converging grid lines compresses the gas even where $\partial v/\partial r = 0$, and should heat it, yet the scalar form gives $q = 0$. A tensor artificial viscosity avoids this, with all the good properties of the scalar one in covariant form ([Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) 1979; [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) 1986). ZEUS-2D offers both forms. The nonlinear viscosity only has to give the correct jump conditions and shock speed, and numerical experiments show the scalar form adequate in many problems, even in non-Cartesian geometries, so that the tensor form is kept for problems that demand it.

Some tests with strong shocks also need a linear artificial viscosity, to damp oscillations in stagnant regions of the flow. Its scalar viscous pressure is $q^{\rm lin} = C_1\rho C_a\Delta v$, with $C_1$ of order unity and the adiabatic sound speed $C_a$, $C_a^2 = \gamma p/\rho$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 38; [Lapidus](#lapidus-1967) 1967; [Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) 1986). It acts in expansion as well as in compression, separately in each direction, and enters through eqs. 35 to 37 with $q^{\rm lin}$ in place of $q$, directly after the nonlinear viscosity and before the third substep. **[GK]** The paper leaves the sign of $\Delta v$ open. In eq. 37 the linear viscosity heats only if $q^{\rm lin}$ has the sign opposite to $v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j}$, so that $\Delta v$ must be the difference taken the other way, positive in compression. Both later copies of the code take $q^{\rm lin} = -$`qlin`$\,\rho\,(v1_{i+1,j} - v1_{i,j})\sqrt{e/\rho}$, which confirms this reading, but use $\sqrt{e/\rho}$ for the sound speed, which for an ideal gas differs from $C_a$ by the factor $[\gamma(\gamma - 1)]^{1/2}$.

#### 2.3.3 Compressional heating

The third substep adds the compressional heating ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.3.3). For an ideal gas, an implicit update with the time-centred pressure $p^{n+1/2} = (p^n + p^{n+1})/2$, $(e^{n+1} - e^n)/\Delta t = -p^{n+1/2}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, improves the conservation of energy (eq. 39). With $p = (\gamma - 1)e$ it can be solved for the new energy, $e^{n+c}_{i,j} = e^{n+b}_{i,j}\,[1 - (\Delta t/2)(\gamma - 1)(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}]/[1 + (\Delta t/2)(\gamma - 1)(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}]$, with the zone-centred divergence of appendix C (eq. 40). For other equations of state, a predictor with $p^n$, $e^{\rm pred} = e^{n+b} - \Delta t\,p^n(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}$ (eq. 41), is followed by a corrector with the average of $p^n$ and the pressure $p^{\rm pred}$ of $e^{\rm pred}$ (eq. 42). The partially updated variables then pass to the transport step.

#### 2.3.4 The tensor artificial viscosity

Appendix B builds the tensor artificial viscosity on the analogy of the viscous stress of a Newtonian fluid, $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mu[\nabla\mathbf{v} - \tfrac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{e}]$, with the dynamic viscosity $\mu$, the symmetrised velocity gradient $\nabla\mathbf{v}$ and the unit tensor $\mathbf{e}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. B, eq. 133; [Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) 1984, §25). The bulk viscosity is taken as zero, by Stokes's hypothesis, so that the tensor is traceless and vanishes for a fluid that expands equally in all directions. An artificial viscosity differs from a molecular one in the size of its coefficient, which must spread shocks over a few zones of the local mesh, act only in compression, and be large in shocks but negligible elsewhere. On the analogy of the nonlinear coefficient of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950), [Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) (1979) take $\mathbf{Q} = l^2\rho(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})[\nabla\mathbf{v} - \tfrac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{e}]$ where $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$, and zero elsewhere, with $l$ a few zone widths (eq. 134). Its main advantage over the scalar form is its dependence on the divergence, which is invariant, instead of the velocity gradient, a tensor that is a single number only for planar flow in one dimension.

[Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) (1979) give $\mathbf{Q}$ for one-dimensional spherical geometry, and its extension to several dimensions is direct, with two choices ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, app. B). The length $l$ must be a single number rather than one per direction, since the tensor would otherwise not be isotropic and would lose its zero trace. In practice it is set from the larger grid spacing, $l_{i,j} = \max(C_2\,dx1a_i, C_2\,dx2a_j)$. The off-diagonal, shear components are dropped, so that the viscosity smooths only compressive shock fronts and does not broaden large gradients of shear. The diagonal components are zone-centred, $Q11_{i,j} = l_{i,j}^2\,d_{i,j}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}[(\nabla v_{(11)})_{i,j} - \tfrac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}]$, and likewise $Q22$ and $Q33$, with the differences of appendix C (eqs. 135 to 137).

The viscous force and heating, $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q}$ and $-\mathbf{Q}:\nabla\mathbf{v}$, take the covariant forms $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q})_{(1)} = (g_2^2g_{31})^{-1}\,\partial(g_2^2g_{31}Q_{11})/\partial x_1$ and $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q})_{(2)} = (g_2g_{32}^2)^{-1}\,\partial(g_{32}^2Q_{22})/\partial x_2 + Q_{11}(g_2g_{32})^{-1}\,\partial g_{32}/\partial x_2$, in which the zero trace has eliminated $Q_{33}$, and $\mathbf{Q}:\nabla\mathbf{v} = l^2\rho(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\tfrac13[(\nabla v_{(11)} - \nabla v_{(22)})^2 + (\nabla v_{(11)} - \nabla v_{(33)})^2 + (\nabla v_{(33)} - \nabla v_{(22)})^2]$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 138 to 140). The paper states that they reduce to the formulation of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) in Cartesian geometry. [Tscharnuter and Winkler](#tscharnuter-winkler-1979) stress that writing the heating as a sum of squares is essential, since in their experience every other form eventually turns unstable. The difference forms divide differences of the zone-centred components across a face by the scale factors on that face, average $Q11$ in the last term of the second component, and replace eqs. 35 to 37 when the tensor form is used (eqs. 141 to 143). The viscous time step of the tensor form, $\delta t = \Delta x^2/(4\nu)$ with $\nu = l^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, replaces the viscous limit of §4.6 (eq. 144).

**[GK]** The sum of squares makes the heating $-\mathbf{Q}:\nabla\mathbf{v}$ non-negative wherever $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$, the only place where $\mathbf{Q}$ acts. In one-dimensional planar flow, eq. 134 gives $Q_{11} = \tfrac23 l^2\rho(\partial v/\partial x)^2$, the pressure of [von Neumann and Richtmyer](#von-neumann-richtmyer-1950) with $l^2$ scaled by 2/3. The later copies of the code take $l^2$ as 1.5 `qcon` times the square of the larger zone width, the factor 1.5 making the tensor form equal to the scalar one in such flow. In several dimensions the two forms differ even in Cartesian coordinates, as $Q_{11}$ depends on the whole divergence. Four printed formulae need correcting, and the width that sets $l_{i,j}$ needs a scale factor. Eq. 138 has $g_3^2g_{31}$ in the denominator, where its numerator and eq. 141 show $g_2^2g_{31}$ to be meant. Eqs. 141 and 142 divide their first differences by the scale factors alone, without the spacings $dx1b_i$ and $dx2b_j$ between the zone centres that make them derivatives. Eq. 144 lacks an absolute value, as $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} < 0$ wherever the viscosity acts, although the sign does not matter inside eq. 74, which squares each limit (2.5.2). In spherical polar coordinates $dx2a_j$ is an angle, so that $l_{i,j}$ needs the length $g2b_i\,dx2a_j$ in the 2-direction, which the code uses. Eq. 138 also relies on $g_2$ and $g_{31}$ having the same logarithmic derivative, as in all three coordinate systems of ZEUS-2D. In general, the zero trace leaves $(g_2g_{31}^2)^{-1}\,\partial(g_2g_{31}^2Q_{11})/\partial x_1 + Q_{22}\,\partial\ln(g_{31}/g_2)/\partial x_1$, which follows from eq. 130 with the sign corrected in 2.2.4.

### 2.4 The transport step

#### 2.4.1 Conservative, directionally split advection

The transport step solves difference forms of the integral eqs. 12 to 14, by which the amount of a quantity $q$ in its control volume, the zone centred on $q$, changes by the flux of $q$ through the faces ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). The scheme, of second order in space and time, changes $q\tau$, with $\tau$ the control volume, by the differences of the time-centred fluxes $\langle(vk - vgk)\,q^*_k\,\tilde A_k\rangle$ through its faces (eqs. 43 and 44). Here $vk - vgk$ is the velocity relative to the grid, $q^*_k$ the face value of $q$ given by the interpolation, and $\tilde A_k$ the area of the face, all in direction $k$. For a zone-centred $q$ the velocities lie on the faces of the control volume, which is a main reason for the staggered mesh, while other quantities need velocities averaged to the faces. Each flux is computed once and serves both zones beside its face. The fluxes then cancel in pairs when the update is summed over the grid, so that the total amount of $q$ changes only by the fluxes through the boundaries of the domain, to round-off.

The advection is split into one-dimensional sweeps ([Strang](#strang-1968) 1968): one in the 1-direction, and then one in the 2-direction with the quantities from the first ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 45 and 46). Second order needs a symmetric sequence, such as half a sweep in $x_1$, a full one in $x_2$ and another half in $x_1$, at half as much computing time again as the plain sequence. Experiments by [Finn and Hawley](#finn-hawley-1989) (1989) showed the gain not worth the cost at moderate resolutions, up to about $10^5$ zones in two dimensions, where a higher resolution with the unsymmetric sequence is more accurate for the same time. ZEUS-2D therefore splits unsymmetrically, but alternates the order of the sweeps from one cycle to the next, which may improve the accuracy at no extra cost.

#### 2.4.2 Interpolation to the faces

The hard part is to find accurate and stable values of $q$ at the faces, averaged over the time step ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). [Godunov](#godunov-1959) (1959) first advected discontinuities stably on an Eulerian grid, by upwinding: the value at a face comes from the distribution of $q$ upstream of it. Later work raised the accuracy with polynomials of higher order within the upstream zones, and found that stability at higher order also needs monotonicity. A profile that rises or falls monotonically before advection must do so after it, so that no new extrema appear. [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984b) (1984b) and [Clarke](#clarke-1988) (1988) discuss how monotonic schemes of the kind ZEUS-2D implements suppress instabilities.

Three interpolations are implemented for the face value $q^*_i$ of a zone-centred $q_i$, each named by the order of the first term it drops from the Taylor series ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). The donor cell method, of first order, takes $q$ as constant within a zone, so that $q^*_i = q_{i-1}$ where $v_i - vg_i > 0$ and $q^*_i = q_i$ where it is negative (eq. 47). It is very diffusive and serves only in tests. The van Leer method, of second order ([van Leer](#van-leer-1977) 1977), takes $q$ as linear within a zone, with $q^*_i = q_{i-1} + [\Delta x_{i-1} - (v_i - vg_i)\Delta t]\,dq_{i-1}/2$ for flow in the positive direction and $q^*_i = q_i - [\Delta x_i + (v_i - vg_i)\Delta t]\,dq_i/2$ otherwise (eq. 48). Its slopes are monotonised as the harmonic mean, $dq_i = 2\Delta q_{i-1/2}\,\Delta q_{i+1/2}/(\Delta q_{i-1/2} + \Delta q_{i+1/2})$, of the differences $\Delta q_{i+1/2} = (q_{i+1} - q_i)/\Delta x_i$ where these have the same sign, and $dq_i = 0$ otherwise (eq. 49). The slopes allow nonuniform spacing, and where they vanish the method falls back to the donor cell. It combines lower diffusion with speed, and is the method most used in ZEUS-2D. **[GK]** Eq. 48 gives the average of the linear profile over the part of the upwind zone that crosses the face in a time step.

The piecewise parabolic advection (PPA), of third order, interpolates parabolae: $q^*_i = q_{R,i-1} + \xi(q_{i-1} - q_{R,i-1}) + \xi(1 - \xi)(2q_{i-1} - q_{R,i-1} - q_{L,i-1})$ for flow in the positive direction, and the mirror form in $q_{L,i}$ and $q_{R,i}$ otherwise ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 50). Here $\xi$ is the fraction of the upwind zone that the flow relative to the grid crosses in a time step, and $q_{L,i}$ and $q_{R,i}$ are the monotonised values at the left and right faces of zone $i$, from the algorithm of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) (1984) as [Clarke](#clarke-1988) (1988) implemented it in ZEUS. The paper refers to these sources for the details, such as the constraints for monotonicity and the steepening of contacts. A parabola needs two zones upwind of a face, so that PPA uses five zones centred on the one updated, and two ghost zones at each boundary. **[GK]** Eq. 50 is the average of the parabola of [Colella and Woodward](#colella-woodward-1984) over the part of the upwind zone that crosses the face.

PPA keeps contact discontinuities to two zones, better than any other method, but at a high cost in computing time ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). For many dynamical problems in several dimensions, it is not clear that it does better than the van Leer method at a higher resolution. A third-order advection does not make the whole code converge at that order, as other parts of the procedure may dominate the error. [Finn and Hawley](#finn-hawley-1989) (1989) showed, moreover, that with a velocity that varies in space, the van Leer method and PPA must account for the velocity gradient across a zone to keep their orders of convergence. ZEUS-2D therefore mostly uses the van Leer method, although PPA is fully implemented. PPA is not PPM, the piecewise parabolic method, which pairs the same third-order interpolation with a Riemann solver.

#### 2.4.3 Fluxes on a moving grid and consistent transport

The mass shows how the fluxes are built ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). In the 1-direction, the flux through a face of zone $(i,j)$ is $d^*_{1,i,j}(v1_{i,j} - vg1_i)\,g2a_i^{n+1/2}\,g31a_i^{n+1/2}\,dvl2a_j^n$, with the interpolated density $d^*$ and with scale factors centred in time to follow the motion of the grid (eq. 51). Factors that depend on $x_2$ are not centred in time, since the grid stays fixed in $x_2$ during the sweep in $x_1$. The change of $d\,dvl1a\,dvl2a$ over the time step is then the difference of the fluxes through the two faces, with $dvl1a$ at the new time (eq. 52). The sweep in $x_2$ uses the flux $d^*_{2,i,j}(v2_{i,j} - vg2_j)\,g31b_i^n\,dx1a_i^n\,g32a_j^{n+1/2}$ and the volume factor $dvl2a$ at the new time, starting from the densities of the first sweep (eqs. 53 and 54). **[GK]** Per unit $x_3$, $g2a_i\,g31a_i\,dvl2a_j$ is the area of a face normal to $x_1$, and $g31b_i\,dx1a_i\,g32a_j$ that of a face normal to $x_2$, exactly when $x1b_i$ lies midway between $x1a_i$ and $x1a_{i+1}$.

The other variables follow in the same way, with two differences ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.4). First, they are transported consistently with the mass, a notion due to [Norman, Wilson and Barton](#norman-et-al-1980) (1980). The control-volume differencing conserves each variable globally, but the numerical diffusion of the differencing and the interpolation can spoil its local conservation, which consistent transport limits by building the flux of each variable from the flux of mass. It can much improve how well specific angular momentum is conserved locally, and with it the evolution of a system. The quantities advected are thus the density and the specific quantities: the internal energy per unit mass, $e/\rho$, and the specific momenta $s_1/\rho = v_1$, $s_2/\rho = h_2v_2$ and $s_3/\rho = h_3v_3$, whose geometric factors make $s_2$ and $s_3$ angular momenta in cylindrical and spherical geometry. Second, the transport step works with momenta and the source step with velocities, so that the variables are converted when the transport step begins and ends. As density and velocities lie at different places, the conversion uses arithmetic averages of the density. The velocities stay fixed through the transport step, and are updated from the advected densities and momenta at its end.

The mass fluxes $\dot M^1_{i,j} = d^*_{1,i,j}(v1_{i,j} - vg1_i)$ and $\dot M^2_{i,j} = d^*_{2,i,j}(v2_{i,j} - vg2_j)$ are the density fluxes without their face areas ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eqs. 55 and 56). The flux of each variable is its interpolated specific value times the mass flux, averaged where needed, times the area of the face. In the 1-direction, this gives $(e/d)^*\dot M^1$ for the energy, $v1^*\,\tfrac12(\dot M^1_{i,j} + \dot M^1_{i+1,j})$ for $s_1$, $v2^*\,g2a_i\,\tfrac12(\dot M^1_{i,j} + \dot M^1_{i,j-1})$ for $s_2$ and $v3^*\,g31a_i\,g32b_j\,\dot M^1_{i,j}$ for $s_3$ (eqs. 57 to 60), with the updates of eqs. 61 to 64, and likewise in the 2-direction (eqs. 65 to 72). The staggering shifts the control volumes. Those of the zone-centred $d$, $e/d$ and $s_3$ coincide, but those of $s_1$ and $s_2$ are centred on the faces in their own direction (fig. 5), with the volume factors $dvl1b$ and $dvl2b$ in those directions. For $d$, $e/d$ and $s_3$ the fluxes thus lie on the faces in both directions, for $s_1$ at the zone centres in the 1-direction and on the faces in the 2-direction, and for $s_2$ the other way round. With the transport step, a cycle of one time step is complete.

Eqs. 65 and 68 give the faces of the zone-centred control volume the width $dx1b_i$, where eq. 53 has $dx1a_i$, the width of the zone, for the same volume. Eqs. 66 to 68 interpolate $s1^*$, $s2^*$ and $s3^*$, where eqs. 58 to 60 interpolate $v1^*$, $v2^*$ and $v3^*$. ### 2.5 Boundary conditions and the time step

#### 2.5.1 Boundary conditions

After each update of the source or transport step, boundary conditions fill the ghost zones from the adjacent active zones by explicit formulae, without the evolution equations ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.5). Their form depends on the geometry and physics of a problem. ZEUS-2D offers five, which can be applied to each variable on each boundary separately.

- Reflecting. The zone-centred variables and the tangential velocity in the ghost zones copy the values of the active zones that mirror them. The normal velocity vanishes on the boundary and is reflected in the second ghost zone.
- Axis of symmetry. As the reflecting condition, except that the zone-centred third component of the velocity takes the negative of its mirror value, which forces it to zero on the axis.
- Inflow. The ghost zones keep prescribed values of all variables, which may vary in time, and no outflow is allowed. The condition is exact for supersonic inflow. For subsonic inflow, the boundary should let outgoing waves pass, but in practice it reflects some of them.
- Outflow. The most difficult condition, made as simple as possible: the values of the active zones are copied into the ghost zones, which extrapolates the flow along the grid lines. This is exact for supersonic outflow, but reflects waves spuriously in subsonic outflow. Where these reflections affect the dynamics, a larger domain or a more accurate condition is needed, such as extrapolation along characteristics rather than grid lines ([Thompson](#thompson-1987) 1987).
- Periodic. The zone-centred variables and the tangential velocity in the ghost zones take the values of the active zones on the far side of the grid. So does the normal velocity in the second ghost zone, while on the boundary it follows from the difference equations.

The conditions can be set for every zone of every boundary separately, so that a condition may change along a boundary, as at the inlet of a jet nozzle ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.5). The boundary conditions of each variable sit in a subroutine of their own, which any part of the code can call, so that they are easy to change. They concern the hydrodynamic variables only. [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b) and [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) (1992) give those of the magnetic field and the radiation.

#### 2.5.2 Stability and the time step

Upwinding the advected fluxes is essential for stability, as it keeps short, sawtooth oscillations from growing ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.6). An explicit code must also keep its time step within the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition, which limits the distance that information travels in a time step, by waves or with the flow, to less than a zone. In one dimension, $\Delta t \le \min[\Delta x/(|u| + C_a)]$ over all zones, with the flow speed $u$ and the adiabatic sound speed $C_a$ (eq. 73), and a von Neumann analysis of the equations derives this rigorously ([Richtmyer and Morton](#richtmyer-morton-1957) 1957). In several dimensions the one-dimensional limits are combined, and ZEUS-2D takes $\Delta t = C_0/[\max(\delta t_1^{-2} + \delta t_2^{-2} + \delta t_3^{-2} + \delta t_4^{-2})]^{1/2}$, with the maximum over all zones and the Courant number $C_0 \approx 0.5$ as a safety factor (eq. 74). The limits are $\delta t_1 = \min(\Delta x_1, \Delta x_2)/C_a$ for sound, $\delta t_2 = \Delta x_1/(v_1 - vg_1)$ and $\delta t_3 = \Delta x_2/(v_2 - vg_2)$ for the flow relative to the grid (eqs. 75 and 76), and $\delta t_4$ for the artificial viscosity.

The artificial viscosity turns the equation of motion into a diffusion equation, and explicit diffusion is stable only for $\Delta t \le (\Delta x)^2/(4\nu)$, with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, eq. 77). Comparison with the Navier–Stokes equations gives $\nu = l^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$ for the artificial viscosity, so that $\delta t_4 = \min[(\Delta x_1)^2/(4l^2\Delta v_1/\Delta x_1),\ (\Delta x_2)^2/(4l^2\Delta v_2/\Delta x_2)] = \min[\Delta x_1/(4C_2\Delta v_1),\ \Delta x_2/(4C_2\Delta v_2)]$ (eq. 78). The time step follows the characteristic speeds as they change, but for accuracy it may grow by at most 30 per cent from one step to the next, while for stability it may shrink by any amount. Fully implicit differencing escapes the Courant condition, but is inefficient for flows with short dynamical time scales and is not used for the hydrodynamics. With radiation, however, the Courant condition for all characteristic speeds can become too restrictive. The radiation variables are therefore evolved fully implicitly, in a mixed explicit and implicit scheme that [Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) (1992) describe.

**[GK]** As eq. 74 squares each limit, the signs of $v_1 - vg_1$, $v_2 - vg_2$ and $\Delta v$ in eqs. 76 and 78 do not matter there, nor that of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$ in eq. 144. The two forms of eq. 78 agree only if $C_2 = (l/\Delta x)^2$, the slip of the definition noted in 2.3.2. The later copies of the code take the second form, with `qcon`. In spherical polar coordinates $\Delta x_2$ is an angle, so that the widths in the 2-direction in eqs. 75, 76 and 78 need the factor $g_2$, as the length $g2b_i\,dx2a_j$ in 2.3.4 does.

### 2.6 Self-gravity

The Poisson equation is elliptic, and so easier to solve than the hyperbolic equations of hydrodynamics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). The solver must work on a nonuniform mesh in two dimensions with general boundary conditions, which rules out many of the fastest methods for special problems, such as spectral and Fourier methods, and ZEUS-2D solves the sparse matrix of the differenced equation instead. With the potential zone-centred, the covariant Laplacian of appendix A and the volume differences of 2.2.3 near the origin, the equation becomes $a_1\Phi_{i+1,j} + a_2\Phi_{i,j+1} + a_3\Phi_{i,j} + a_4\Phi_{i-1,j} + a_5\Phi_{i,j-1} = S_{i,j}$ (eq. 79). The coefficients $a_1 = g2a_{i+1}\,g31a_{i+1}\,dvl2a_j/dx1b_{i+1}$ and $a_4 = g2a_i\,g31a_i\,dvl2a_j/dx1b_i$ couple the neighbours in $x_1$, and $a_2 = g32a_{j+1}\,dvl1a_i/(g2b_i^2\,dx2b_{j+1})$ and $a_5 = g32a_j\,dvl1a_i/(g2b_i^2\,dx2b_j)$ those in $x_2$. The paper prints $a_3$ as their sum, and the source as $S_{i,j} = 4\pi G\,d_{i,j}\,dvl1a_i\,dvl2a_j$. The five-point molecule couples each zone to its four neighbours, so that an $N \times N$ grid gives $N^2$ coupled linear equations, with a sparse, banded matrix (fig. 6). The implicit moment equations of the radiation give matrices of the same structure.

The boundary conditions enter the matrix or the right-hand side without changing its structure ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). A boundary can be of Neumann type, with zero slope of the potential, at an axis or equator of symmetry, where it changes the diagonal elements. Or it can be of Dirichlet type, with the potential prescribed in the ghost zones, at an outer boundary far from most of the mass, where it changes the right-hand side. The Dirichlet values come from a multipole expansion ([Jackson](#jackson-1975) 1975), $\Phi_B = G\sum_l P_l(\mu_B)\,r_B^{-(l+1)}M_l$ with the moments $M_l = \int_V \rho\,r^lP_l(\mu)\,dV$ (eqs. 80 and 81). Here $P_l$ is the Legendre polynomial of the cosine $\mu_B$ of the angle between the position of the boundary point and the axis of symmetry, and $r_B$ is its distance from the origin, so that the method needs curvilinear coordinates with such an axis. The polynomials follow from $(n + 1)P_{n+1}(x) = (2n + 1)xP_n(x) - nP_{n-1}(x)$, with $P_0 = 1$ and $P_1 = x$ (eq. 82), and the moments are sums of $d_{i,j}\,r_{i,j}^lP_l(\mu_{i,j})\,dvl1a_i\,dvl2a_j$ over the zones (eqs. 83 and 84). When most of the mass lies far from the boundaries, a few moments suffice. When the density near the boundaries is significant the series converges slowly, and it may diverge for a mass distribution without a central concentration. ZEUS-2D adds moments until the boundary potential has converged to one part in $10^3$, up to 100 terms.

The matrix of eq. 79 is symmetric and positive definite, and several standard methods solve it ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). The alternating direction implicit method (ADI) of [Norman and Winkler](#norman-winkler-1986) (1986) and [Black and Bodenheimer](#black-bodenheimer-1975) (1975) seeks the steady state of the diffusion equation $\partial\Phi/\partial g = \nabla^2\Phi - 4\pi G\rho$, in an iteration time $g$ unrelated to the physical time, by repeated one-dimensional sweeps (eq. 85). Each sweep needs only tridiagonal matrices, for which fast, vectorisable algorithms are known ([Richtmyer and Morton](#richtmyer-morton-1957) 1957). A dynamic ADI (DADI), which chooses the iteration step adaptively, converges much faster than the simple sequence of steps used here ([Larson, Hewett and Anderson](#larson-et-al-1989) 1989). The authors have also used the incomplete Cholesky conjugate gradient method (ICCG) and the generalised minimal residual method (GMRES) ([Bramley](#bramley-1989) 1989, private communication), with comparable times for grids of about $10^5$ zones, and in practice they use these rather than ADI. The implicit radiation hydrodynamics uses the same solvers and shares their storage, and as all of them rest on library routines, new ones such as DADI are easy to add. This matters, since the sparse solvers take most of the computing time of a run.

A solution is often not needed every cycle unless the density changes rapidly ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.7). Rearranged, eq. 79 gives a trial potential $\Phi'_{i,j} = (S_{i,j} - a_1\Phi_{i+1,j} - a_2\Phi_{i,j+1} - a_4\Phi_{i-1,j} - a_5\Phi_{i,j-1})/a_3$ from the stored one (eq. 86), and the equation is solved only where $|\Phi'_{i,j} - \Phi_{i,j}|/\Phi_{i,j} > \epsilon$, with $\epsilon \approx 10^{-5}$ (eq. 87). In practice this cuts the solutions to about one cycle in ten.

**[GK]** As printed, eq. 79 cannot hold. Its diagonal coefficient $a_3$ is the sum of the others with the same sign, so that a constant potential would not satisfy the homogeneous equation and the matrix would not be a discrete Laplacian. The diagonal must carry the sign opposite to that of the neighbours, and eq. 86 then changes accordingly. The positive boundary potential of eq. 80 belongs to a potential of the opposite sign to that of eqs. 2, 5 and 30, which satisfies $-\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$ and is positive near a mass. Its matrix, with positive diagonal and negative neighbours, is the positive definite one the paper describes. The later copies of the code use this convention: a comment declares the potential positive, the equation of motion adds its gradient, and the test of eq. 86 has the opposite signs. With the potential of eq. 5, eq. 80 needs a minus sign. Eq. 84 also lacks the factor $2\pi$ of the azimuth, since $dvl1a_i\,dvl2a_j$ is the volume of a zone per unit of $\phi$, and the code includes it.

### 2.7 Hydrodynamic tests

#### 2.7.1 Approach and advection tests

ZEUS-2D implements the algorithms of ZEUS anew and adds capabilities, such as the covariant formalism and third-order interpolation for all variables, so that it needs a full set of hydrodynamic tests ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5). Tests first catch errors in the coding, but their main task is to benchmark the algorithms. They show the strengths and limits of the methods, which helps to tell the features of a simulation that the physics causes from artefacts of the numerics. The tests isolate single terms of the difference equations first and build up to problems that use the whole code: advection in one dimension, then a shock tube in one dimension, and finally strong shocks in two dimensions.

The transport step is the most complicated part of the procedure and is tested first, with the source terms left out and a constant velocity, for which the analytic solutions are trivial ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.1). Results are shown for the density, but the other variables gave identical results, and every test in one dimension was run in each direction separately. A square pulse in Cartesian geometry, 50 zones wide and centred at $x = 30$, is advected over five times its width, so that its edges should end at $x = 255$ and 305 (fig. 8). The donor cell method loses both the shape and the amplitude of the pulse. The van Leer method keeps the amplitude and spreads each edge over about 14 zones, PPA over 6, and PPA with the steepening of discontinuities over 2. Donor cell is unfit for simulations, van Leer and PPA do well, and PPA with steepening could hardly do better. Whatever the scheme, the conservative differencing keeps the area under the pulse to within one part in $10^7$ to $10^8$. The paper gives neither the size of the grid nor the Courant number.

The rate of convergence measures the accuracy of the advection more precisely ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.1). The error is the $L_1$ norm $\epsilon = \sum_{i=1}^N |q_i - \tilde q_i|/N$ over the $N$ grid points, between the numerical solution $q_i$ and the analytic one $\tilde q_i$ (eq. 88). It should fall as $\epsilon \propto \Delta x^r$, and the rate $r$ is the slope of $\log\epsilon$ against the logarithm of the resolution, in principle 1, 2 and 3 for schemes of first, second and third order. A Gaussian pulse, advected over ten times its width, serves this test, as the discontinuities of a square pulse can never be resolved (fig. 9). PPA is the most accurate at every resolution, with an error at the finest more than a thousand times smaller than that of donor cell, and that of the van Leer method a hundred times smaller. PPA converges at the rate 3.0, the van Leer method at 2.1 and donor cell at 0.6, close to the expected values. Other waveforms mainly serve to compare advection schemes ([Clarke](#clarke-1988) 1988; [Woodward](#woodward-1986) 1986), whereas these tests check the coding, and so use few waveforms. The paper gives neither the resolutions nor the width of the pulse.

The covariant formalism is tested by advection in curvilinear geometry ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.2). In a relaxation problem, a uniform density starts with the velocity $v_r = v_0r$ and decays as $\rho = \rho_0e^{-2v_0t}$ in cylindrical and as $\rho = \rho_0e^{-3v_0t}$ in spherical geometry, so that both reach $6.14 \times 10^{-6}\rho_0$, at $v_0t = 6$ and $v_0t = 4$ respectively. ZEUS-2D gives $5.78 \times 10^{-6}\rho_0$ and $5.60 \times 10^{-6}\rho_0$, errors of 5.9 and 8.8 per cent after a fall of nearly six orders of magnitude. The profiles stay perfectly flat, which confirms the terms that depend on the geometry.

#### 2.7.2 Pressure-free collapse

The collapse of a homogeneous sphere without pressure is a last test of advection in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.3). Every mass shell follows the analytic solution of [Hunter](#hunter-1962) (1962), $r/r_0 = \cos^2\beta$ and $\rho/\rho_0 = \cos^{-6}\beta$, with $\beta + \tfrac12\sin 2\beta = t\,[8\pi G\rho(0)/3]^{1/2}$ (eqs. 89 and 90). All shells thus reach the origin together at $\beta = \pi/2$, at the free-fall time $\tau_{ff} = [3\pi/(32G\rho(0))]^{1/2}$. With $\rho = G = 1$ at the start in spherical geometry, $\tau_{ff} = 0.543$, and the run ends at $t = 0.535$, by which time the density has grown by nearly three orders of magnitude. The gravitational force of a homogeneous sphere is computed exactly, so that the test probes the radial advection. On a stationary grid, the density shows a spurious spike in the first few zones at the origin, while elsewhere the numerical and analytic solutions agree well (fig. 10). [Mönchmeyer and Müller](#monchmeyer-muller-1989) (1989) traced such an error to a staggered mesh centred on the coordinates. A mesh centred on the volumes removes it, as does coordinate regularisation in full generality, but at the cost of substantial changes to the difference equations and the transport step. A grid that collapses with the cloud, its velocities set to those of the fluid everywhere but at the boundaries, reduces the advection errors and substantially improves the solution at the origin, which shows the use of a moving mesh. The paper does not give the number of zones or the boundary conditions.

#### 2.7.3 The Sod shock tube

A shock tube tests all transport and source terms in one dimension, the artificial viscosity included ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.4). It became the standard test of hydrodynamic codes with [Sod](#sod-1978) (1978), and later [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984a) (1984a, [b](#hawley-et-al-1984b)). Two states meet at $t = 0$, hot and dense on the left and cool and rarefied on the right, and nonlinear waves run away from the discontinuity: a shock to the right and a rarefaction to the left. The exact solution of this Riemann problem shows whether a code gives the correct jump conditions and shock speed, and gives them stably. The problem is Cartesian and one-dimensional, but can be run in either direction, and along the axis in cylindrical geometry. Sod's states have $\gamma = 1.4$, pressure and density 1.0 on the left, and pressure 0.1 and density 0.125 on the right, with the discontinuity at $x = 0.5$ on a face between zones and the gas at rest. [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984a) (1984a) describe how the exact solution is obtained.

ZEUS-2D, with 100 zones and the van Leer method, follows the exact solution well, with the shock at the right, then the contact discontinuity, and the rarefaction at the left ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, fig. 11). The shock and the contact are spread over about 4 zones, as expected from the artificial viscosity and the second-order advection, and the head and foot of the rarefaction, where the slope jumps, are slightly smoothed. PPA does not reduce this smoothing much. Small overshoots appear where a slope jumps from zero. [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) (1986) showed with an adaptive code in one dimension that these are the correct solution of the viscous difference equations as $\Delta x \to 0$, a sign of low diffusion. The specific internal energy has the wrong amplitude behind the shock, probably because the shock tube is naturally posed in density and pressure, while consistent transport advects the specific internal energy. A code without consistent transport would suit this problem slightly better. [Hawley, Smarr and Wilson](#hawley-et-al-1984b) (1984b) compared the common astrophysical algorithms on this problem, those of ZEUS-2D among them, and the results agree exactly with theirs. They concluded that these algorithms gave the most accurate results of those tested. The paper does not state the time at which the solution is shown.

#### 2.7.4 Strong shocks

[Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984) compared hydrodynamic codes on three demanding problems with strong shocks, the first one-dimensional and the others two-dimensional, and ZEUS-2D was run on all three to benchmark it against those codes ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5).

In the first, two strong blast waves interact in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5.1). A gas with $\gamma = 1.4$ lies at rest between reflecting walls at $x = 0$ and 1, with density 1 throughout and pressure 1000 for $x < 0.1$, 100 for $x > 0.9$ and 0.01 in between. The two shocks reflect from the walls and collide, which produces an intricate pattern of shocks and discontinuities. [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) give no analytic solution, but a PPM calculation at very high resolution as the reference. With 1200 zones at $t = 0.038$, both the van Leer method and PPA place all shocks and contacts where the PPM solution has them (fig. 12). PPA does better, with sharper contacts and a density spike at $x = 0.76$ closer to the PPM value of about 6.5, as expected for a test built to show how the overall accuracy depends on the thickness of discontinuities. Methods that keep shocks thinner, such as PPM or adaptive meshes ([Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) 1986), do better still, but the simple algorithms of ZEUS-2D do quite well.

The second is a flow at Mach 3 through a wind tunnel with a step, in two dimensions ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5.2). The tunnel is 3 units long and 1 high, with a step 0.2 high at 0.6 from its left end. The top and bottom walls reflect, gas flows in on the left and out on the right, and the gas starts with density 1.4, pressure 1.0 and $\gamma = 1.4$, so that Mach 3 means a velocity of 3.0. As [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984) do, the flow near the corner of the step is corrected to reduce the numerical errors of this singular point. In the first four zones above the step and just right of its corner, the density and the speed, but not the direction of the velocity, are reset so that the entropy and the sum of enthalpy and kinetic energy per unit mass equal their values in the zone just left of and below the corner, and the first two zones of the row above are treated alike. On a staggered mesh this is very hard to do properly, since a zone-centred velocity averages those of the faces, so that resetting it changes the velocities of the neighbouring zones too. The solutions therefore suffer from errors generated at the corner, especially at low resolution, but they still compare the advection schemes of ZEUS-2D consistently, and qualitatively with other schemes at high resolution. The grids cover a domain four units long, of which only the left three are analysed, to avoid errors from the outflow boundary.

At $t = 4.0$, on grids of $20 \times 80$, $40 \times 160$ and $80 \times 320$ zones, higher resolution makes the discontinuities thinner and brings the shocks closer to their positions in the best PPM solution ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, figs. 13 and 14). On the coarsest grid, the Mach stem at the upper wall has not yet formed. The error from the corner shows as a spurious Mach stem at the lower reflection, where the shock meets a spurious boundary layer along the step, and it shifts the shocks and reflections, so that only the finest grids come close to the solution of [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984). PPA does better at every resolution: the shock positions and the Mach stem's length lie closer to the PPM values, and the contact from the triple point is sharper. It costs more computing time, about 2.2 times that of the van Leer method on the finest grid (table 1).

The third is the double Mach reflection, in which a Mach 10 shock reflects from a ramp ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.5.3). Since a rectangular mesh cannot easily hold a sloping wall, the shock is set up oblique to the mesh and reflects from the lower boundary instead. The domain, 1 unit high and 3 long, holds gas at rest with pressure 1.0 and density 1.4, and $\gamma = 1.4$. A plane shock at 60° to the $x$-axis runs from $x = 1/6$ on the lower boundary to the top at $y = 1$, with the postshock state from the jump conditions: density 8, pressure 116.5 and a velocity of 8.25 normal to the shock. The postshock state is imposed on the left boundary and on the lower boundary between $x = 0$ and $1/6$. The lower boundary reflects from $x = 1/6$ to 3, the right boundary lets the gas out, and the upper boundary follows the exact motion of the shock. Setting the shock obliquely on the grid causes a small error, visible as stray contours from the upper right corner back to the reflected shock, which the results of [Woodward and Colella](#woodward-colella-1984) (1984) also show and which is hard to remove. Again the grids cover four units, of which the left three are shown.

At $t = 0.2$, on grids of $30 \times 120$, $60 \times 240$ and $120 \times 480$ zones, resolution again matters greatly, but without the troublesome corner of the step, the solutions agree much better with the best PPM results at every resolution ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, figs. 15 and 16). On finer grids the shocks are much narrower, and the intricate structure near the reflection, with its small jet along the wall bounded by a contact discontinuity, comes out much better. PPA is again better at every resolution, and on the finest grid it differs little from the best PPM solution, whose shocks are thinner and smoother. It is 2.1 times slower than the van Leer method there (table 2).

The tables give the cost on the Cray X-MP ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, tables 1 and 2). For the wind tunnel, the van Leer method took 1149, 2310 and 4764 cycles and 14.7, 80.5 and 546 s on the three grids, and PPA 1227, 2510 and 5114 cycles and 28.1, 166 and 1190 s. For the double Mach reflection, the van Leer method took 415, 853 and 1734 cycles and 10.5, 64.2 and 456 s, and PPA 434, 888 and 1802 cycles and 19.7, 129 and 970 s. **[GK]** On the finest grids this amounts to about $2.2 \times 10^5$ zone updates per second with the van Leer method and $1.1 \times 10^5$ with PPA.

#### 2.7.5 Tests of the Poisson solver

The Poisson solver is easy to test, as many analytic potentials are known, and even without them the density can be recovered from the computed potential as a check ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §5.6). The tests use analytic solutions, which also check the gradient of the potential, the quantity that enters the equations. The first object, a uniform sphere of radius $R$ and density $\rho_0$, has $\Phi = 2\pi G\rho_0(R^2 - r^2/3)$ inside and $\tfrac43\pi G\rho_0R^3/r$ outside, and the gradient $-\tfrac43\pi G\rho_0r$ and $-\tfrac43\pi G\rho_0R^3/r^2$ (eqs. 91 and 92). The second is a centrally condensed sphere with $\rho = \rho_c/[1 + (r/r_c)^2]$ inside $R$, for which $\Phi = 4\pi G\rho_cr_c^2\{\arctan(r/r_c)/(r/r_c) + \tfrac12\ln[(1 + (r/r_c)^2)/(1 + (R/r_c)^2)] - 1\}$ inside and $-4\pi G\rho_cr_c^3[R/r_c - \arctan(R/r_c)]/r$ outside, with the gradient $GM(r)/r^2$ and $M(r) = 4\pi\rho_cr_c^3[r/r_c - \arctan(r/r_c)]$ within $R$ (eqs. 93 to 95). The third is a homogeneous ellipsoid of density $\rho_0$ with semi-axes $a_1$ and $a_3$, whose potential follows [Chandrasekhar](#chandrasekhar-1969) (1969, p. 42) (eqs. 96 to 108). It involves index symbols for oblate and prolate spheroids that depend on the eccentricity, and outside the body the larger root $\lambda$ of $r^2/(a_1^2 + \lambda) + z^2/(a_3^2 + \lambda) = 1$. Each object was set up in cylindrical and in spherical coordinates, with and without equatorial symmetry.

The relative errors in the potential and its gradient, with equatorial symmetry and then without, are as follows ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, table 3). In cylindrical coordinates, they are $2.23 \times 10^{-4}$ and $6.04 \times 10^{-3}$, then $1.56 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.91 \times 10^{-2}$, for the homogeneous sphere; $3.80 \times 10^{-4}$ and $2.21 \times 10^{-3}$, then $4.29 \times 10^{-3}$ and $2.22 \times 10^{-2}$, for the centrally condensed sphere; and $2.42 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.96 \times 10^{-2}$, then $8.37 \times 10^{-3}$ and $6.44 \times 10^{-2}$, for the ellipsoid. In spherical coordinates, the same sequence reads $4.02 \times 10^{-5}$ and $1.11 \times 10^{-4}$, then $1.31 \times 10^{-4}$ and $9.02 \times 10^{-4}$; $5.60 \times 10^{-4}$ and $9.98 \times 10^{-4}$, then $5.71 \times 10^{-4}$ and $9.99 \times 10^{-4}$; and $3.95 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.86 \times 10^{-2}$, then $5.83 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1.74 \times 10^{-2}$. For the ellipsoid without equatorial symmetry, which gives the largest errors, most of the error lies at the surface, where the density drops abruptly to zero and the grid represents the smooth boundary only roughly, so that resolution matters there (figs. 17 and 18). The largest error in the gradient, 6.4 per cent in cylindrical coordinates, falls to 1.7 per cent in spherical ones, which describe the ellipsoid better. Even without assumed symmetry, the error contours pass smoothly and symmetrically across the equator. The paper gives neither the grids nor the sizes of the objects.

**[GK]** Eqs. 91 and 92, and the potential of the ellipsoid after Chandrasekhar, take the potential as positive, as the code does (2.6), while eqs. 94 and 95 take it as negative, as eq. 5 does. Outside the spheres, eq. 91 gives $+GM/r$ and eq. 94 $-GM/r$. Each pair is consistent within its own convention.

#### 2.7.6 Conclusions from the tests

The tests show that the algorithms are implemented correctly and how they perform, insight that helps to avoid artefacts of the numerics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §6). PPA has the lowest diffusion and the highest rate of convergence and is the clear choice for pure advection, but it typically takes twice as long as the van Leer method in two dimensions. Donor cell is too diffusive for any practical use and never serves in simulations. The shock tube reproduces exactly the published results for the same algorithms, and the tests with strong shocks compare qualitatively well with the best PPM results. The small errors in the gradient of the potential come mainly from the limited resolution of the density distribution.

### 2.8 Magnetohydrodynamics

#### 2.8.1 The equations of magnetohydrodynamics

A large-scale magnetic field that threads a plasma exerts stresses on its moving charged particles, whose motion in turn alters the strength and the distribution of the field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §2). Where the gyroradii of the particles are comparable to the scales of interest, the plasma must be described microscopically, by the Maxwell–Boltzmann equations for the distribution function of the particles. Where they are much smaller, a statistical description suffices: the equations of ideal MHD, which evolve the macroscopic conserved quantities. Like the equations of hydrodynamics, they follow from moments of the Maxwell–Boltzmann equations over velocity, here with the further assumptions of quasi-neutrality and of Ohm's law as a closure for the current density ([Spitzer](#spitzer-1962) 1962).

With these assumptions, and following [Jackson](#jackson-1975) (1975), the equations of MHD take the following form ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 1 to 4). The continuity equation, $D\rho/Dt + \rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, and the internal energy equation, $\rho\, D(e/\rho)/Dt = -p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, are those of hydrodynamics (2.1.1). The equation of motion gains the Lorentz force, $\rho\, D\mathbf{v}/Dt = -\nabla p - \rho\nabla\Phi + (1/4\pi)(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\times\mathbf{B}$, and the induction equation, $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = \nabla\times(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$, evolves the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$. An equation of state $p(\rho, e)$ and the Poisson equation for the gravitational potential $\Phi$ close the system. **[GK]** The printed eq. 2 has $p\nabla\Phi$ in place of $\rho\nabla\Phi$. This is a misprint, since only $\rho\nabla\Phi$ is a force per unit volume, and eq. 2 of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a) has it.

Further assumptions shape the terms in $\mathbf{B}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §2). The Lorentz force omits the displacement current from Ampère's law, which holds only for phenomena of low frequency, such as those the code is meant for. In Faraday's law, which yields the induction equation, the electric field is eliminated by keeping only the advective term of the generalised Ohm's law. The justification is that resistivities in astrophysics are mostly so low that the field is in effect frozen into the fluid. In many systems the gas is so highly ionised that collisions between charged and neutral particles couple the two tightly, and flux freezing holds well. Where the ionisation drops markedly, as in the densest parts of molecular clouds, the collisions no longer keep the coupling perfect, and the plasma, with its field, drifts through the neutral gas, a process called ambipolar diffusion. ZEUS-2D includes neither ambipolar diffusion nor resistivity. The authors call adding the diffusive terms to the algorithms straightforward, and a fruitful subject for future work.

Like those of hydrodynamics, the equations of MHD are coupled, nonlinear and hyperbolic partial differential equations ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §2). Many of the algorithms they require are those of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) (1992a), and §3 describes what the magnetic field adds and changes.

#### 2.8.2 The divergence constraint and the wave families

Compared with hydrodynamics, MHD poses two main numerical difficulties ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). First, Maxwell's equations require the field to stay free of divergence, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$, at all times. Unless the evolution equations build this constraint in, the numerical errors in the evolved components will in general violate it, and as the errors accumulate, the violation can grow so gross that the calculation must be stopped. [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) discuss the issue in detail. Second, MHD has entirely new families of waves. They bring a wealth of new physics, and the algorithms must propagate every MHD wave stably and accurately.

The need for algorithms that keep the field free of divergence has long been recognised ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). Until recently, the usual approach was to evolve the vector potential $\mathbf{A}$, with $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$, instead of the field, and to obtain the field by differencing the potential wherever it is needed. If the difference forms of divergence and curl are built consistently, the discrete counterpart of the identity $\nabla\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{A} = 0$ holds exactly. In two dimensions, the evolution of the potential reduces to the advection of a single scalar. The original ZEUS evolved the potential in this way to simulate axisymmetric jets with MHD ([Clarke](#clarke-1988) 1988).

The trouble with the potential lies in the Lorentz force, which needs its second derivatives ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). [Clarke, Norman and Burns](#clarke-et-al-1989) (1989) argue that a Lorentz force of first-order accuracy requires a scheme of third order for the potential, such as PPA. [Choptuik](#choptuik-1986) (1986), on the other hand, stresses that numerical derivatives of a smooth function need not lose order of accuracy compared with the analytic derivative. Still, [Clarke](#clarke-1988) (1988) showed beyond doubt, by numerical tests, that many advection problems need a scheme of higher order to give an accurate current, the first derivative of the field. Even then, the current and the force reverse anomalously near shocks, contact discontinuities and other sharp features of the field. These reversals act directly on the flow, and accelerate and heat it spuriously.

[Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) developed constrained transport (CT), which builds the constraint directly into the difference equations for the field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). As the components of the field are evolved themselves, no numerical second derivatives are needed, and the current comes out accurate and smooth, without anomalous reversals. At the same time, the method keeps $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ to machine round-off at all times. For these reasons ZEUS-2D adopts CT.

The second difficulty calls for a scheme that represents all MHD waves accurately ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). The upwind schemes that evolve the hydrodynamic variables might seem suitable for the field as well. MHD, however, has a family of incompressible transverse waves without a hydrodynamic analogue, the Alfvén waves, which couple the velocity and the field tightly. Applied to the MHD equations, the hydrodynamic upwind schemes were found to give these waves a large dispersion error. The error shows most clearly where sharp or discontinuous features evolve, or where smooth features meet a boundary, and there it creates oscillations of large amplitude and short wavelength that are stable, but neither damped nor diffused away. Of the methods tried, only schemes that are upwind in the characteristics of the Alfvén waves removed the error. A large intrinsic diffusion removes it as well, but the authors consider that unsuitable.

One method of this kind is the method of characteristics, MOC ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). Codes built entirely on it exist ([Lou, Rosner and Ulmschneider](#lou-et-al-1987) 1987) and are upwind in every family of MHD waves by construction, but they give up the conservative form, the divergence constraint and the simple difference forms of the primitive equations. ZEUS-2D therefore solves by the MOC only the subset of the equations that describes how Alfvén waves propagate, as an intermediate step that yields time-advanced quantities for the update of the field components.

In axisymmetric calculations in two dimensions, the symmetry keeps the toroidal component free of divergence, as $\partial B_\phi/\partial\phi \equiv 0$, so that only the poloidal components need a formalism that preserves the constraint, such as CT ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3). The poloidal components are those in the plane of the mesh, and the toroidal component is the one normal to it. The toroidal field is evolved by a simple algorithm built on the MOC (§3.3), and the poloidal field by a form of the MOC within CT, called MOC-CT (§3.2). **[GK]** The argument holds in all three coordinate systems of ZEUS-2D, not only in the axisymmetric ones: with no dependence on $x_3$, the component $B_3$ drops out of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$ (2.2.4).

#### 2.8.3 The centring of the field

Before the difference equations for the field can be written, its components must be placed on the staggered mesh of 2.2.1, with the 3-direction normal to the computational plane of $x_1$ and $x_2$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.1). The paper counts the right centring among the key aspects of any numerical scheme. An Alfvén wave couples the velocity and field components perpendicular to its direction of propagation, $v_1$ with $B_1$ for waves along the 2-direction and $v_2$ with $B_2$ for waves along the 1-direction. The field is therefore centred like the velocity: $B_1$ on the faces normal to the 1-direction, $B_2$ on those normal to the 2-direction, and $B_3$ at the zone centres (fig. 1). With this centring, each poloidal component is perpendicular to a face of the zone, like the momenta. This differs from the centring that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) originally described.

The discrete components are thus $B1_{i,j} = B_1(x1a_i, x2b_j)$, $B2_{i,j} = B_2(x1b_i, x2a_j)$ and $B3_{i,j} = B_3(x1b_i, x2b_j)$, at the positions of $v1_{i,j}$, $v2_{i,j}$ and $v3_{i,j}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.1). An important derived quantity is the current density, which the paper defines as $\mathbf{j} = \nabla\times\mathbf{B}$. With this centring, its poloidal components come to lie where the orthogonal field components are, $j_1$ with $B_2$ and $j_2$ with $B_1$, while $j_3$ lies at the zone corners (fig. 1). The figure places the 3-component of the electromotive force (EMF) of the two-dimensional CT scheme at the corners as well.

**[GK]** In Gaussian units, Ampère's law without the displacement current gives the current density $(c/4\pi)\nabla\times\mathbf{B}$, and the Lorentz force per unit volume is $\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B}/c$, which yields the term $(1/4\pi)(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\times\mathbf{B}$ of eq. 2 of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b). The $\mathbf{j}$ of its §3.1 drops the factor $c/4\pi$. Its centring follows from the curl of 2.2.4 when nothing depends on $x_3$. Then $j_1$ is a difference in $x_2$ of the zone-centred $h_3B_3$ and so lies on the faces normal to $x_2$, and $j_2$, a difference of the same in $x_1$, lies on the faces normal to $x_1$. The component $j_3$ combines differences of $h_2B_2$ in $x_1$ and of $h_1B_1$ in $x_2$, which both fall on the corners.

#### 2.8.4 Constrained transport

The poloidal field is evolved in three parts ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2): the CT formalism, which preserves the constraint (§3.2.1), the MOC-CT scheme, by which every poloidal family of MHD waves evolves accurately (§3.2.2), and the differencing of the Lorentz force in the equation of motion (§3.2.3).

[Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) built CT on the insight that a scheme which evolves the magnetic flux conservatively satisfies the constraint identically ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). Stokes' theorem turns Faraday's law, eq. 4, into the integral form $\partial\Phi_S/\partial t = \oint_{\partial S}(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (eq. 5), where $\Phi_S$ is the magnetic flux through a surface $S$ with boundary $\partial S$. The flux through a surface thus changes at the rate given by the line integral, around its edges, of the electromotive force (EMF) $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$. With the centring of 2.8.3, the components of the flux lie on the faces of the control volume.

The boundary of each face consists of four edges, and the components of the EMF are centred on the midpoints of the edges ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). On the two-dimensional grid, $\epsilon_1 = v_2B_3 - v_3B_2$ then lies on the faces normal to the 2-direction, where $B_2$ lies, $\epsilon_2 = v_3B_1 - v_1B_3$ on those normal to the 1-direction, where $B_1$ lies, and $\epsilon_3 = v_1B_2 - v_2B_1$ at the zone corners. Fig. 2 extends a zone along $x_3$ to show the contours and the direction of integration, with the field components drawn against the coordinate directions. With the EMF taken as constant along each edge, the right-hand rule gives $(\Phi1^{n+1}_{i,j} - \Phi1^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = (\epsilon3_{i,j+1} - \epsilon3_{i,j})\,\Delta x3$ for the flux through a face normal to $x_1$ (eq. 6), and $(\Phi2^{n+1}_{i,j} - \Phi2^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = -(\epsilon3_{i+1,j} - \epsilon3_{i,j})\,\Delta x3$ for one normal to $x_2$ (eq. 7), where $\Delta x3$ is the length of an edge along $x_3$. The terms in $\epsilon_1$ and $\epsilon_2$, which the full form of the equations shows on the edges along $x_1$ and $x_2$, cancel by symmetry in two dimensions.

Summing eqs. 6 and 7 over the faces of a zone gives the change of the total flux through its surface ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). The EMF of each edge enters the equations of the two faces it bounds once each, with opposite signs, so that all EMF terms cancel in the sum. If the old flux satisfied the constraint, with no net flux through the closed surface by Gauss's law, so does the new: eqs. 6 and 7 guarantee that the changes of the flux are free of divergence. Global conservation follows if the EMF is computed once for the whole grid and the same values update neighbouring zones. Summed over all zones, eqs. 6 and 7 then conserve the total flux through the domain to machine round-off. As [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) point out, this is exactly analogous to the conservative differencing of hydrodynamic variables such as the density, where the flux through each zone face, computed once, is used twice: once as mass leaves a zone and once as it enters the next.

Expressing the fluxes by the face areas and the field components turns eqs. 6 and 7 into evolution equations for the components themselves ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988), with general relativistic MHD in mind, derived covariant equations in terms of the magnetic vector density, which make no reference to the metric of a particular coordinate system and so hold in any. For nonrelativistic MHD, where the components of the metric are known analytically, covariant equations in which they appear explicitly, as in the formalism of 2.2.3, are more convenient than a transformation to the magnetic vector density. The flux components and the edge length are then $\Phi_1 = h_2h_3\,dx_2\,dx_3\,B_1$, $\Phi_2 = h_1h_3\,dx_1\,dx_3\,B_2$ and $\Delta x3 = h_3\,dx_3$ (eqs. 8 to 10).

Substituting these expressions into eqs. 6 and 7 gives the difference equations by which ZEUS-2D evolves the poloidal field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 11 and 12). The 1-component is $B1^{n+1}_{i,j} = [B1^n_{i,j}\,g2a^n_i\,g31a^n_i\,g32b^n_j\,dx2a^n_j + \Delta t\,(g31a^{n+1/2}_i\,g32a^{n+1/2}_{j+1}\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j+1} - g31a^{n+1/2}_i\,g32a^{n+1/2}_j\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j})]/(g2a^{n+1}_i\,g31a^{n+1}_i\,g32b^{n+1}_j\,dx2a^{n+1}_j)$, and the 2-component is $B2^{n+1}_{i,j} = [B2^n_{i,j}\,g31b^n_i\,g32a^n_j\,dx1a^n_i - \Delta t\,(g31a^{n+1/2}_{i+1}\,g32a^{n+1/2}_j\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i+1,j} - g31a^{n+1/2}_i\,g32a^{n+1/2}_j\,\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j})]/(g31b^{n+1}_i\,g32a^{n+1}_j\,dx1a^{n+1}_i)$. The scale factors appear at the old and the new time, which accounts for the compression or expansion of a moving grid, and the terms in the EMF are centred in time, at $n + 1/2$. Only the EMF remains to be specified. **[GK]** The last lines of eqs. 6 and 7 factor out one length $\Delta x3$ for both edges, which is exact only where $h_3$ is the same on them, as in Cartesian coordinates. Eqs. 11 and 12 take $h_3$ at each edge, $g31a_i\,g32a_j$ for the edge through the corner $(i, j)$, so that an edge enters both faces it bounds with the same length, and the cancellation holds in every geometry.

CT preserves the constraint, but cannot create it: the initial field must itself be free of divergence ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.1). Setting it up from a vector potential, as [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) suggest, is a convenient way to ensure this. Perhaps more important, the boundary conditions on the field must not introduce divergence into the domain, a point that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) also stress. ZEUS-2D therefore sets boundary values for the EMF and evolves the field components of the ghost zones like the active ones, and §3.4 gives the kinds of boundary conditions for the EMF.

#### 2.8.5 The electromotive force by the method of characteristics

Within CT, any method of computing the EMF keeps the field free of divergence, but in practice the method must be stable and must describe the MHD flow accurately ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). The stability requirement comes from the transport terms of the induction equation, through which advection of the fluid produces an EMF. [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) gave a stable method for advection problems, which can be written schematically as $\epsilon3^{n+1/2}_{i,j} = v1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}\,B2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j} - v2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}\,B1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ (eq. 13), where the starred quantities are time-centred values at the zone corners, where the EMF lies. They must be interpolated in space and time from the face-centred values that ZEUS-2D evolves. [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) averaged the velocity arithmetically at the old time, without centring it in time, and interpolated the field upstream with the van Leer method of 2.4.2.

This way of computing the EMF proved entirely adequate for the advection tests of [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988), but in fully dynamical problems it fails to carry shear Alfvén waves accurately along poloidal field lines ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2), as the tests of §4.6 show. This motivated the MOC-CT scheme.

The core of MOC-CT is to compute with the MOC the time-advanced values of both the velocity and the field from which the EMF is formed ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). The MOC step is simplified by using only the characteristics of the Alfvén waves and leaving out those of the compressive, fast and slow, MHD waves. The compressive families are tightly coupled to the hydrodynamics through the thermodynamics, so that the hydrodynamic algorithms, which evolve the compressive hydrodynamic waves, that is sound, also treat the compressive MHD waves accurately. The full MHD algorithm of ZEUS-2D thus accounts for all families of MHD waves in multidimensional flows, which the tests of §4 demonstrate.

The equations of the MOC step follow from those of MHD for a moving, incompressible fluid in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). With all variables functions of $x$ alone, and the units of $B$ chosen such that $\mu_0 = 1$, they reduce to $\partial v/\partial t = (B_x/\rho)\,\partial B/\partial x - \partial(v_xv)/\partial x$ (eq. 14) and $\partial B/\partial t = B_x\,\partial v/\partial x - \partial(v_xB)/\partial x$ (eq. 15), where $v_x$ and $B_x$ are the components along $x$, and $v$ and $B$ stand for the transverse components that the waves carry. The constraint in one dimension, $\partial B_x/\partial x \equiv 0$, and incompressibility, $\partial v_x/\partial x \equiv 0$, simplify the induction equation and the Lorentz force considerably. The leading terms, coupled, describe pure transverse Alfvén waves along $x$, and the second terms the advection by the fluid. The equations thus carry information in a moving fluid only by Alfvén waves, and admit no compressive modes. **[GK]** With $\mu_0 = 1$ the factor $1/4\pi$ of eq. 2 is absorbed into $B$, which amounts to measuring the Gaussian field in units of $(4\pi)^{1/2}$, and the Alfvén speed is $|B_x|/\rho^{1/2}$.

Multiplying eq. 15 by $\rho^{-1/2}$ and adding it to eq. 14 or subtracting it gives the characteristic form $Dv/Dt \mp \rho^{-1/2}\,DB/Dt = 0$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eq. 16), with $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + (v_x \pm B_x/\rho^{1/2})\,\partial/\partial x$ (eq. 17). The upper signs hold along the forward characteristic $C^+$, and the lower ones along the backward characteristic $C^-$. Their coefficient, $v_x \pm v_A$, is the Alfvén speed carried along by the flow. Along these characteristics, which are straight lines of slope $v_x \pm v_A$ in spacetime, the changes of velocity and field in each direction are therefore not independent.

The difference equations follow from a one-dimensional spacetime diagram centred on the position of the EMF, where the partially updated values $v_i^{*,n+1/2}$ and $B_i^{*,n+1/2}$ are needed ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2, fig. 3). By construction they lie midway between the velocities and fields known at time level $n$, which on the staggered mesh places them at the zone corners, with the EMF. Traced back along $C^+$ and $C^-$ to the old time level $n$, at which all variables are known, the characteristics end at their footpoints. Upwind interpolation of the values at time level $n$, with the donor cell, van Leer or PPA method, gives the time-averaged values over the domain of dependence of each footpoint: $v_i^{-,n}$ and $B_i^{-,n}$ on $C^-$, and $v_i^{+,n}$ and $B_i^{+,n}$ on $C^+$. For the velocities and the fields alike, the upwinding uses the characteristic speeds $v_x \pm v_A$. Along the two characteristics the equations are then differenced as $(v_i^{*,n+1/2} - v_i^{+,n}) - (B_i^{*,n+1/2} - B_i^{+,n})/(\rho_i^+)^{1/2} = 0$ (eq. 18) and $(v_i^{*,n+1/2} - v_i^{-,n}) + (B_i^{*,n+1/2} - B_i^{-,n})/(\rho_i^-)^{1/2} = 0$ (eq. 19). To centre the denominators approximately in time, $\rho_i^+ = \rho_{i-1}^n$ and $\rho_i^- = \rho_i^n$. This pair of linear equations is solved directly for $v_i^{*,n+1/2}$ and $B_i^{*,n+1/2}$. **[GK]** The solution is $B_i^{*,n+1/2} = [v_i^{-,n} - v_i^{+,n} + B_i^{+,n}/(\rho_i^+)^{1/2} + B_i^{-,n}/(\rho_i^-)^{1/2}]/[(\rho_i^+)^{-1/2} + (\rho_i^-)^{-1/2}]$, with $v_i^{*,n+1/2}$ then from eq. 18. For equal densities, $v^*$ is the mean of the two footpoint velocities plus $(B^- - B^+)/(2\rho^{1/2})$, and $B^*$ the mean of the two footpoint fields plus $\rho^{1/2}(v^- - v^+)/2$.

In several dimensions the characteristic equations are solved by directional splitting, to obtain time-advanced values of all components of velocity and field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). First, at each grid point, the pair for Alfvén waves along the 2-direction is solved: $Dv_1/Dt \mp \rho^{-1/2}\,DB_1/Dt = 0$ with $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + (v_2 \pm B_2/\rho^{1/2})\,h_2^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_2$ (eqs. 20 and 21), differenced like eqs. 18 and 19 but upwinded with the characteristic speeds $v_2 \pm B_2/\rho^{1/2}$. It yields $v1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ and $B1^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ at every zone corner. The pair for Alfvén waves along the 1-direction follows: $Dv_2/Dt \mp \rho^{-1/2}\,DB_2/Dt = 0$ with $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t + (v_1 \pm B_1/\rho^{1/2})\,\partial/\partial x_1$ (eqs. 22 and 23), upwinded with $v_1 \pm B_1/\rho^{1/2}$, which yields $v2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ and $B2^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j}$ at every corner.

Because the velocity and field components share their positions on the mesh, the MOC applies naturally to the coupled pair in each direction, and both sweeps give their results at the zone corners ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). This is why ZEUS-2D departs from the centring of [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988). On grids where velocity and field components do not coincide, averaging either quantity to where the other lies was found to spoil how the characteristics follow Alfvén waves, and gave no suitable algorithm. With both components known at every corner at the advanced time, eq. 13 gives the EMF, and the CT update follows. The directional splitting extends naturally to three dimensions ([Clarke, Stone and Norman](#clarke-et-al-1990) 1990).

Directional splitting only approximates the true characteristic problem in several dimensions ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.2). Interpolating along the principal axes of the characteristic cone, rather than along the grid lines, would probably be more accurate. On a staggered mesh, however, the exact direction of the field lines, and with it that of the characteristics, is not known at any one point, and averaging the field components to a common position to find it would probably add diffusion, much as the present splitting is likely to do. Which formulation is the most accurate is left to future numerical work, and the tests of §4 show the accuracy of the present one.

#### 2.8.6 The Lorentz force

Accurate Alfvén waves need more than an EMF from values of the MOC and a CT update of the field: the Lorentz force in the equation of motion must also use partially updated values of the field, from a similar MOC calculation ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.2.3). Written out, in the units of 2.8.5, the Lorentz force gives $\partial v_1/\partial t = [B_2/(\rho h_2)]\,\partial B_1/\partial x_2 - [B_2/(\rho h_2)]\,\partial(h_2B_2)/\partial x_1 - [B_3/(\rho h_3)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_1$ (eq. 24) and $\partial v_2/\partial t = [B_1/(\rho h_2)]\,\partial(h_2B_2)/\partial x_1 - [B_1/(\rho h_2)]\,\partial B_1/\partial x_2 - [B_3/(\rho h_2h_3)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_2$ (eq. 25). Coupled to the induction equation, the first term of each describes Alfvén waves, while the others change the momentum through gradients of the magnetic pressure. The update is therefore split in two parts.

Substep 1 of the source step, described in 2.3.1, takes the gradients of the magnetic pressure ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 26 to 29). For $v_1$ they give $(v1^{n+a}_{i,j} - v1^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = -\langle B2_{i,j}\rangle/(d_{i-1,j} + d_{i,j}) \times [g2b_i(B2_{i,j} + B2_{i,j+1}) - g2b_{i-1}(B2_{i-1,j} + B2_{i-1,j+1})]/(g2a_i\,dx1b_i) - (B3_{i-1,j} + B3_{i,j})/(d_{i-1,j} + d_{i,j}) \times (g31b_i\,B3_{i,j} - g31b_{i-1}\,B3_{i-1,j})/(g31a_i\,dx1b_i)$ (eq. 26), and for $v_2$ they give $(v2^{n+a}_{i,j} - v2^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = -\langle B1_{i,j}\rangle/(d_{i,j-1} + d_{i,j}) \times [(B1_{i,j} + B1_{i+1,j}) - (B1_{i,j-1} + B1_{i+1,j-1})]/(g2b_i\,dx2b_j) - (B3_{i,j-1} + B3_{i,j})/(d_{i,j-1} + d_{i,j}) \times (g32b_j\,B3_{i,j} - g32b_{j-1}\,B3_{i,j-1})/(g2b_i\,g32a_j\,dx2b_j)$ (eq. 27). The averages $\langle B1_{i,j}\rangle = (B1_{i,j} + B1_{i+1,j} + B1_{i,j-1} + B1_{i+1,j-1})/4$ and $\langle B2_{i,j}\rangle = (B2_{i,j} + B2_{i,j+1} + B2_{i-1,j} + B2_{i-1,j+1})/4$ bring the field to the positions of $v2$ and $v1$ (eqs. 28 and 29). These terms, as everywhere in the source step, take the stored values of the field, not values advanced in time.

The Alfvén terms are added with the MOC-CT update, from time-advanced values $B1'^{n+1/2}_{i,j}$ and $B2'^{n+1/2}_{i,j}$ that a MOC solution of the split characteristic equations gives in the frame of the moving fluid ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 30 and 31): $(v1^{n+1}_{i,j} - v1^{n+a}_{i,j})/\Delta t = 2\langle B2_{i,j}\rangle/(d_{i-1,j} + d_{i,j}) \times (B1'^{n+1/2}_{i,j+1} - B1'^{n+1/2}_{i,j})/(g2a_i\,dx2a_j)$ and $(v2^{n+1}_{i,j} - v2^{n+a}_{i,j})/\Delta t = 2\langle B1_{i,j}\rangle/(d_{i,j-1} + d_{i,j}) \times (g2a_{i+1}\,B2'^{n+1/2}_{i+1,j} - g2a_i\,B2'^{n+1/2}_{i,j})/(g2b_i\,dx1a_i)$. These values are not those of the EMF. CT is not operator split, so that the characteristic equations of MOC-CT, from eqs. 14 and 15, describe Alfvén waves in an Eulerian frame. The equation of motion is split, however, and its Lorentz force acts in effect in a Lagrangian frame. For the Lorentz force, the characteristic equations must therefore follow Alfvén waves in the frame comoving with the fluid: they are eqs. 14 and 15 without their advective second terms.

These characteristic equations are eqs. 20 and 22 with the comoving derivatives $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t \pm (B_2/\rho^{1/2})\,h_2^{-1}\,\partial/\partial x_2$ for waves along the 2-direction and $D/Dt = \partial/\partial t \pm (B_1/\rho^{1/2})\,\partial/\partial x_1$ for waves along the 1-direction ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 32 and 33). They are differenced as eqs. 18 and 19, but the footpoint values are upwinded with the speeds $\pm B_2/\rho^{1/2}$ and $\pm B_1/\rho^{1/2}$. The velocities that the same solution gives are not needed and are discarded.

**[GK]** In the terms in $\langle B1_{i,j}\rangle$, eqs. 27 and 31 of [Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) (1992b) print the density sum $d_{i-1,j} + d_{i,j}$, which belongs to the position of $v1$. At the position of $v2$ the sum is $d_{i,j-1} + d_{i,j}$, as in the second term of eq. 27, and both later copies of the code use it.

#### 2.8.7 The toroidal field

In two dimensions the toroidal field satisfies the constraint by symmetry, and needs no constraint-preserving algorithm such as CT ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.3). Torsional Alfvén waves still need a specialised algorithm to propagate accurately. The algorithms are self-consistent, in that MOC-CT reduces to this one for stationary flows.

In two dimensions, the equation of motion and the induction equation give for the 3-components, in full ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 34 and 35), $\partial v_3/\partial t = -(h_2h_3)^{-1}[\partial(h_2h_3^2v_1v_3)/\partial x_1 + \partial(h_3^2v_2v_3)/\partial x_2] + [B_1/(\rho h_3)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_1 + [B_2/(\rho h_2h_3)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_2$ and $\partial B_3/\partial t = -h_2^{-1}[\partial(h_2v_1B_3)/\partial x_1 + \partial(v_2B_3)/\partial x_2] + B_1h_3\,\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial x_1 + (B_2h_3/h_2)\,\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial x_2$. The first two terms of each are transport terms, the advection of $v_3$ or $B_3$ by the flow in each direction, and the last two are source terms. With the operator splitting of 2.1.2, the source step solves difference forms of $\partial v_3/\partial t = [B_1/(\rho h_3)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_1 + [B_2/(\rho h_2h_3)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_2$ and $\partial B_3/\partial t = B_1h_3\,\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial x_1 + (B_2h_3/h_2)\,\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial x_2$ (eqs. 36 and 37). The transport step solves the integral forms $d/dt\int_V \rho h_3v_3\,dV = -\oint_{\partial V}\rho h_3v_3(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_g)\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ and $d/dt\int_A B_3\,dA = -\oint_{\partial A}B_3(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_g)\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (eqs. 38 and 39). Eq. 39 integrates the field over the area of the zone rather than its volume, since magnetic flux is the more natural quantity to transport. **[GK]** The transport terms of eq. 34 equal $-\nabla\cdot(h_3v_3\mathbf{v})$, which is $h_3$ times the advection of $v_3$, $-h_3^{-1}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla(h_3v_3)$, plus $-h_3v_3\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$. They thus lack a factor $h_3^{-1}$, as their dimensions show where $h_3 = r$, and hold only without compression. The transport step solves eq. 38 for $\rho h_3v_3$, which has the right conservative form, so that the slip does not reach the algorithm.

The source terms of eqs. 36 and 37 cause the motion of torsional Alfvén waves ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.3). Differencing them directly gives no accurate algorithm for torsional Alfvén waves that contain discontinuities. Of the many algorithms tested, the only suitable ones were either very diffusive, such as the Lax–Friedrichs and Lax–Wendroff methods, or upwind along the characteristics of these waves. ZEUS-2D takes the latter, which can be much less diffusive. The MOC estimates partially updated values of $v_3$ and $B_3$ at the half step, which then enter difference forms of eqs. 36 and 37, so that it serves only as an intermediate step and does not evolve the variables itself.

To derive the characteristic equations, eqs. 36 and 37 are written as $\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial t = h_3^{-2}\{(B_1/\rho)\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_1 + [B_2/(\rho h_2)]\,\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial x_2\}$ and $\partial(h_3B_3)/\partial t = h_3^2[B_1\,\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial x_1 + (B_2/h_2)\,\partial(v_3/h_3)/\partial x_2]$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eqs. 40 and 41). Combined as in 2.8.5, they give $D(v_3/h_3)/Dt \mp h_3^{-2}\rho^{-1/2}\,D(h_3B_3)/Dt = 0$ along the forward and the backward characteristic, $C^+$ and $C^-$ (eq. 42). The comoving derivatives are those of eqs. 32 and 33 for waves along the 2- and the 1-direction, as the source step acts in a Lagrangian frame. Like eqs. 20 and 22 for the poloidal directions, eq. 42 relates the changes of the angular velocity and of the magnetic flux along the characteristics of the wave.

The differencing follows 2.8.5, with the rotational velocity $\Omega \equiv v_3/h_3$ and the magnetic flux $h_3B_3$ as dependent variables ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.3). The paper writes $\Phi$ for this flux, a symbol that 2.6 uses for the gravitational potential. Along the two characteristics in one dimension, $(\Omega_i^{*,n+1/2} - \Omega_i^{+,n}) - (\Phi_i^{*,n+1/2} - \Phi_i^{+,n})/(h_3^2\rho^{1/2})_i^+ = 0$ and $(\Omega_i^{*,n+1/2} - \Omega_i^{-,n}) + (\Phi_i^{*,n+1/2} - \Phi_i^{-,n})/(h_3^2\rho^{1/2})_i^- = 0$ (eqs. 43 and 44). Here $\Omega_i^{\pm,n}$ and $\Phi_i^{\pm,n}$ are the values at the old time, interpolated upwind at the footpoints of $C^\pm$, and $\Omega_i^{*,n+1/2}$ and $\Phi_i^{*,n+1/2}$ the advanced values at the zone interfaces (fig. 3). The denominators are centred in time with values of $h_3$ and $\rho$ for the midpoint of each characteristic. The pair is solved directly, and the interface values enter difference forms of eqs. 36 and 37.

In two dimensions, $v_3$ and $B_3$ are updated first for torsional Alfvén waves along the 1-direction and then, identically, along the 2-direction ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.3). In the first sweep, eqs. 43 and 44 are solved at every grid point with footpoint values upwinded by the speeds $\pm B_1/\rho^{1/2}$, and $(v3^{n+a}_{i,j} - v3^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = (B1_{i,j} + B1_{i+1,j})/(2\,g31b_i\,d_{i,j}\,dx1a_i)\,(\Phi^{*,n+1/2}_{i+1,j} - \Phi^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j})$ and $(B3^{n+a}_{i,j} - B3^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = (B1_{i,j} + B1_{i+1,j})\,g31b_i/(2\,dx1a_i)\,(\Omega^{*,n+1/2}_{i+1,j} - \Omega^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j})$ (eqs. 45 and 46). In the second, the speeds are $\pm B_2/\rho^{1/2}$, and $(v3^{n+1}_{i,j} - v3^{n+a}_{i,j})/\Delta t = (B2_{i,j} + B2_{i,j+1})/(2\,g32b_j\,d_{i,j}\,g2b_i\,dx2a_j)\,(\Phi^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j+1} - \Phi^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j})$ and $(B3^{n+1}_{i,j} - B3^{n+a}_{i,j})/\Delta t = (B2_{i,j} + B2_{i,j+1})\,g32b_j/(2\,g2b_i\,dx2a_j)\,(\Omega^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j+1} - \Omega^{*,n+1/2}_{i,j})$ (eqs. 47 and 48). **[GK]** As printed, eqs. 45 to 48 agree with eqs. 36 and 37 only if $\Omega$ and $\Phi$ carry, in each sweep, just the factor of $h_3$ that varies along it, $g_{31}$ in the 1-direction and $g_{32}$ in the 2-direction. With $\Omega = v_3/h_3$ and $\Phi = h_3B_3$ in full, eqs. 45 and 46 lack the factor $g32b_j$, in the denominator and in the numerator, and eqs. 47 and 48 the factor $g31b_i$. The partial factors in turn would change the denominators of eqs. 43 and 44 from $h_3^2\rho^{1/2}$ to $g_{31}^2\rho^{1/2}$ or $g_{32}^2\rho^{1/2}$. Both later copies of the code use $g_{31}$ alone in the 1-direction and $g_{32}$ alone in the 2-direction, but solve the characteristic equations for $v_3$ and $B_3$ themselves, not for $\Omega$ and $\Phi$.

The directional splitting is expected to spread the wave across the field lines, downwind, which a staggered mesh probably cannot avoid entirely ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.3). Whether a more elaborate algorithm can reduce this is left to future tests, which should not change the premise of the MOC update. The algorithm suits only $2\tfrac12$-dimensional simulations. Three-dimensional calculations need the full MOC-CT scheme for every component of the field.

Once the source terms are updated by the MOC, $v_3$ and $B_3$ are advected ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.3). The rotational velocity is advected like a zone-centred scalar, as in the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a). For the toroidal field, the magnetic flux is advected rather than the field, by a directionally split update of eq. 39. In the 1-direction the flux through the faces is $\mathcal{F}^1_{i,j} = \dot M^1_{i,j}\,(B3/d)^*_{1,i,j}\,g2a_i^{n+1/2}$, and $(B3^{n+1}_{i,j}\,g2b^{n+1}_i\,dx1a^{n+1}_i - B3^n_{i,j}\,g2b^n_i\,dx1a^n_i)/\Delta t = -(\mathcal{F}^1_{i+1,j} - \mathcal{F}^1_{i,j})$ (eqs. 49 and 50). Here $(B3/d)^*_{1,i,j}$ is the value on the face, interpolated upwind by donor cell, van Leer or PPA. In the 2-direction, $\mathcal{F}^2_{i,j} = \dot M^2_{i,j}\,(B3/d)^*_{2,i,j}$ and $(B3^{n+1}_{i,j}\,g2b^n_i\,dx2a^{n+1}_j - B3^n_{i,j}\,g2b^n_i\,dx2a^n_j)/\Delta t = -(\mathcal{F}^2_{i,j+1} - \mathcal{F}^2_{i,j})$ (eqs. 51 and 52). With the mass fluxes $\dot M^1$ and $\dot M^2$ of 2.4.3, and with $B_3/\rho$ interpolated rather than $B_3$, the transport is consistent, which keeps the diffusion of the toroidal field relative to the mass small, as flux freezing requires.

#### 2.8.8 Boundary conditions and the time step

CT keeps the field free of divergence only if the initial field is, and if the boundary conditions introduce no monopoles ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.4). As boundary conditions free of divergence are in general hard to construct, ZEUS-2D applies them not to the field components but to the EMF, and updates the field in the ghost zones with the difference equations themselves. The EMF lies at the zone corners, so that its two rows of ghost values beyond the boundary are $i = iim1$ and $iim2$ at the inner $i$ boundary and $i = iop2$ and $iop3$ at the outer one, in the index names of 3.1, and likewise in $j$. The EMF on the boundary itself, at $i = ii$ and $iop1$ or $j = ji$ and $jop1$, comes from the difference equations. All types of boundary condition of the hydrodynamic variables are available for the EMF. Although only its 3-component is nonzero, and it is always tangential to the boundaries, the hydrodynamic conditions for tangential vectors cannot be applied to it directly. As the EMF is a product of velocity and field, its conditions follow from theirs:

- Reflecting. The normal components of velocity and field are reflected and the tangential ones continued, so that the EMF in a ghost zone is the negative of that in the mirrored active zone.
- Inflow. The EMF in the ghost zones takes prescribed values, which may vary in time.
- Outflow. The EMF in each ghost zone equals that in the first active zone.
- Periodic. The EMF in the ghost zones equals that in the corresponding active zones at the opposite side of the grid.

A further condition, for planes of symmetry such as an equator, reflects the velocity and the tangential component of the field and continues its normal component ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.4). **[GK]** It makes the EMF in a ghost zone equal to that in the mirrored active zone, since $\epsilon_3 = v_1B_2 - v_2B_1$ then keeps its sign.

The toroidal components are unconstrained and take the hydrodynamic boundary conditions directly, with one important exception ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.4). If the evolution equation at an outflow boundary omits the incoming characteristic, outgoing Alfvén waves leave without reflection ([Thompson](#thompson-1987) 1987; [Vanajakshi, Thompson and Black](#vanajakshi-et-al-1989) 1989). Numerically, every quantity is given zero gradient along the incoming characteristic, so that at outflow boundaries the angular velocity and the toroidal field are projected into the ghost zones, and outgoing torsional Alfvén waves pass freely. At an equatorial symmetry plane, the rotational velocity is reflected, and the toroidal field reflected with a change of sign. As for the hydrodynamics, each ghost zone can take its own condition, and the conditions of each variable sit in their own subroutine, callable from any point of the code, for modularity and quick changes.

MHD also changes the time step ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.5). The explicit Courant condition of 2.5.2 must include the speeds of MHD waves, as $\Delta t \le \min(\Delta x)/(|u| + C_f)$ (eq. 53), with the local flow speed $u$ and the fast magnetosonic speed $C_f$, where $C_f^2 = v_A^2 + C_a^2$ with the Alfvén speed $v_A$ and the sound speed $C_a$. As $C_f > C_a$, the hydrodynamic condition may not ensure stability in strong fields. For MHD, this limit replaces $\delta t_1$ of eq. 75 of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a).

### 2.9 Magnetohydrodynamic tests

#### 2.9.1 Approach and advection tests

The MHD algorithms of ZEUS-2D include entirely new methods rather than new implementations of old ones, so that extensive tests matter even more than for the hydrodynamics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4). Unlike hydrodynamic codes, MHD codes for astrophysics were then a fairly new field of research, and the paper assembles a new collection of tests, from advection problems in one dimension to full simulations in two, where the line between test and application blurs. [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-1992b) (1992) describe how each test is set up and run in more detail, so that others can calibrate their codes against the suite.

The simplest test advects a square pulse of transverse field, a double current sheet, in one dimension, a test that [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988) first described ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.1). As in the hydrodynamic advection tests of 2.7.1, the source terms are switched off and the velocity is held fixed. CT, however, does not split the induction equation into transport and source terms, but updates the fluxes through the EMF for both at once, so that the field component along the flow must vanish to keep Alfvén waves from arising. The pulse, 50 zones wide and of unit amplitude, starts centred at $x = 30$, as in 2.7.1, and the run stops when it has moved five times its width, so that its edges should lie at $x = 255$ and $305$ (fig. 5). The paper gives neither the grid, the velocity nor the Courant number.

A pulse of $B_2$ advected in the 1-direction, and likewise one of $B_1$ in the 2-direction, was run with the van Leer method, PPA and PPA with the steepener ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.1, fig. 5). The results agree point for point with the hydrodynamic tests of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a). Van Leer diffuses most, spreading discontinuities over about 14 zones, and PPA with the steepener least, over only two, comparable to the results of [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988). The 3-component of the current density, analytically a delta function at each edge of the pulse, gets a finite width from the diffusion of each scheme, and its peaks grow narrower and higher with the order of the scheme, to about 0.15, 0.25 and 0.65 in fig. 5. More important, the currents show no anomalous reversals of sign. Methods that evolve the vector potential can give nonmonotonic currents, as [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) showed, which act on the flow through the Lorentz force and make the results unreliable to an unknown degree. CT's real advantage is that the currents need no second derivative. A pulse of $B_3$ advected in either direction also agrees point for point with the hydrodynamic tests and with those of CT.

#### 2.9.2 The MHD Riemann problem

The MHD analogue of the Sod shock tube, described by [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) (1988), tests transport and source terms together in one dimension ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.2). Unlike the hydrodynamic problem, it has no known analytic solution, partly because the MHD equations are not convex, unlike the Euler equations, as [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) first proved. Physically, two waves of one family can then share their speed, so that compound waves form, a rarefaction joined to a shock of its own family, such as a slow rarefaction joined to a slow shock. This behaviour sets MHD flows clearly apart from hydrodynamic ones, and refutes the common belief that magnetosonic waves behave just like the sound waves of the Euler equations.

[Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) take a gas with $\gamma = 2.0$ and the states $p_l = 1.0$, $\rho_l = 1.0$, $(B_x)_l = 0.75$ and $(B_y)_l = 1.0$ on the left and $p_r = 0.1$, $\rho_r = 0.125$, $(B_x)_r = 0.75$ and $(B_y)_r = -1.0$ on the right ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.2). The domain $x \in (0, 800)$ has 800 zones, the discontinuity lies at $x = 400$ on a zone interface at $t = 0$, and the run ends at $t = 80$. The hydrodynamic states are those of the Sod problem, with a field added that kinks at the discontinuity, where its lines make the angle $\theta$ with the shock normal, $\tan\theta = 1/0.75$. Fig. 6 shows the result with the van Leer method. The paper gives neither the boundary conditions nor the Courant number.

[Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988) solved the problem with the Lax–Friedrichs and Lax–Wendroff methods, flux-corrected transport, and an upwind scheme of second order built for it ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.2). Without an analytic solution, ZEUS-2D is compared with the last, in fig. 2 of [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988). The solution is far more complex than its hydrodynamic counterpart: a fast rarefaction and a slow compound wave move to the left, and to the right a contact discontinuity, then a slow shock, then a fast rarefaction. The compound wave shows the nonconvexity. The positions and widths of the features agree qualitatively very well with those of [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988). The only serious discrepancy is an anomalous undershoot or overshoot at the foot of each fast rarefaction. The scheme is upwind in the Alfvén characteristics but not in the magnetosonic ones, just as hydrodynamic schemes can overshoot at rarefactions of sound waves. A scheme upwind in all characteristics, like that of [Brio and Wu](#brio-wu-1988), removes the overshoot, but is much more complex and slower. ZEUS-2D does substantially better than their results with flux-corrected transport and the Lax–Wendroff and Lax–Friedrichs methods (their figs. 6, 3 and 4), and its relatively simple methods come close to the fully upwind scheme.

#### 2.9.3 The Weber–Davis wind

Another dynamical test in one dimension, which J. Hawley suggested to the authors, is the semi-analytic solution of [Weber and Davis](#weber-davis-1967) (1967) for a magnetic stellar wind ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). It was first studied for the magnetic braking of stellar rotation, to explain why stars on the main sequence change from fast to slow rotation near spectral type F5. The original models are one-dimensional and hold only in the equatorial plane. [Sakurai](#sakurai-1985) (1985) outlined how to obtain the two-dimensional structure of such winds, but as these solutions are very hard to find, the tests use the original one-dimensional models.

[Sakurai](#sakurai-1985) (1985) reviews how the solution is found ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). For a steady flow in one dimension, the MHD equations reduce to four algebraic equations, which conserve mass, magnetic flux, angular momentum and total energy, closed by a polytropic equation of state and by a condition of flux freezing that ties the toroidal components of field and velocity to the radial ones. They reduce further to a Bernoulli equation $H(r, \rho) = E$ for the density profile (eq. 54). For a given total energy $E$, the profile is a contour of the Bernoulli function $H$, whose topology resembles that of hydrodynamic winds, but with three critical points through which solutions can cross: the slow, the Alfvén and the fast point, where the radial velocity equals the speed of slow, Alfvén or fast waves. $H$, and with it the solution, is fixed by three parameters: the polytropic exponent $\gamma$, $\Theta$, the thermal energy at the Alfvén point in units of the gravitational potential energy there, and $\omega$, the rotational energy in the same units.

Of the contours for a given set of parameters, only the one that passes smoothly through all three critical points, with a density that falls monotonically, is physically acceptable; the others violate entropy considerations or the condition of zero pressure at infinity ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). As the Bernoulli function is nonlinear and complicated, this contour has no analytic form, and root finding must trace it from radius to radius. Great care is needed to stay on the single physical root and not to diverge onto inadmissible ones, which is hardest where the roots cross, at the critical points. [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-1992b) (1992) give the method.

A solution can test ZEUS-2D in two ways ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). It can fill the whole domain at the start, to check that the code holds it stably, or set the inner radial boundary, with vacuum elsewhere, to check that the code builds up the correct solution on the whole grid. Both give identical results, provided the transients of the second have died away, which typically takes about 10 fluid crossing times. The runs use 100 radial zones in a strip of spherical polar coordinates around $\theta = \pi/2$, and stop after several fluid crossing times, which gives any difficulty ample time to grow; later times differ little.

The first solution takes the parameters of [Sakurai](#sakurai-1985) (1985), $\gamma = 1.2$, $\Theta = 0.5$ and $\omega = 0.25$, for which the three critical points lie well apart, on the domain $r \in (0.5, 3.0)$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3, fig. 7). Density, internal energy and the radial and azimuthal field fall monotonically, the rotational velocity has its maximum close to the inner boundary and decreases beyond, and the radial velocity rises steadily: magnetic stresses accelerate the wind outward. As $B_r \propto r^{-2}$ and $B_\phi \propto r^{-1}$, the field is radial at the stellar surface, the base of the wind, and becomes ever more azimuthal farther out, as the rotation sweeps it back. The numerical solution follows the analytic one very closely in all variables but the rotational velocity, and even for $v_\phi$ the relative errors stay below 1 per cent over the whole grid (fig. 8). Dips in the errors mark where they change sign. The errors are largest at the first few points next to each boundary. The authors ascribe this to the ghost zones, held at the analytic values while the active zones relax slightly to a solution of the difference equations, so that the slope near the boundaries gains a significant error. The rotational velocity shows it: it dips sharply at the inner boundary, where its slope is wrong, and then follows the analytic solution closely, but with the wrong amplitude.

The second solution takes the original parameters of [Weber and Davis](#weber-davis-1967) (1967), $\gamma = 1.22$, $\Theta = 1.5$ and $\omega = 0.3$, which suit the solar wind ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3, figs. 9 and 10). The Alfvén and fast points then lie close together, while the slow point lies deep inside, at $r = 0.11$, where the solution curve is so steep that root finding becomes very difficult. The domain $r \in (0.2, 1.3)$ therefore excludes it. Agreement is again excellent except for the rotational velocity, whose error is still only about 1 per cent at most. It again stems from wrong slopes at the inner boundary, which matter more here, where the slopes are steeper at these small radii.

The results, errors included, are identical whether the solution starts on the grid or grows from the boundary values ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). The time-dependent code follows the solution through the critical points even with the inner boundary in the submagnetosonic part of the wind, and accelerates the flow correctly from submagnetosonic inflow to supermagnetosonic outflow. The wind tests the rotational terms of the equations well, as it is dominated by the inertia of the rotating flow, which sweeps back the radial field lines, and it bears directly on what ZEUS-2D is meant for: winds driven magnetically from discs and protostars. The time-dependent runs also show that the one-dimensional wind solution is stable.

#### 2.9.4 Convergence tests

Having no discontinuities, the wind suits tests of convergence, the rate at which the error against the correct solution, analytic or of infinite resolution, falls as the number of grid points grows, which has nothing to do with the convergence of iterative solvers ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). [Hawley](#hawley-1989) (1989) distinguishes three kinds:

- Convergence in a single time step. The analytic solution is set on the grid and advanced one step, and the mean absolute error per unit time, $\epsilon = N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N |X_i(\Delta t) - \tilde X(\Delta t)|/\Delta t$, with the numerical solution $X_i$, the analytic solution $\tilde X$, $N$ grid points and the time step $\Delta t$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, eq. 55), is compared over several resolutions. The slope of the error against resolution on logarithmic axes gives the rate.
- Convergence over an extended evolution. The analytic solution is advanced to a fixed time, and the error, as before but not divided by $\Delta t$, gives the rate over several resolutions.
- Self-convergence. Something such as the boundary conditions may stop the code short of the analytic solution, although it still converges to another numerical solution of the problem. The code is run to a fixed time at two resolutions, the relative change of all variables between them is computed, and a third resolution gives the rate from how the two changes compare.

The first kind is cheap enough for very high resolutions, and in one step the effects of single terms can be isolated, such as advection alone in the error of the density ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). For an explicit code, leaving out the points near the boundaries also removes the errors of the boundary conditions. The [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) used the second kind for the advection algorithms (2.7.1). The third suits problems whose analytic boundary conditions are hard to impose, or that have no analytic solution at all.

Both the single-step and the self-convergence test measured the convergence of the whole code on the one-dimensional wind, where the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) had measured that of advection alone ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). They used the first solution, with $\gamma = 1.2$, $\Theta = 0.5$ and $\omega = 0.25$, on $r \in (0.5, 3.0)$, with 50 to 1000 radial grid points and the van Leer method, leaving out the first and last two points against errors from the boundaries. The single-step test gives the asymptotic rate 1.0 for all variables but the rotational velocity, whose errors follow no systematic trend (fig. 11). The self-convergence test gives asymptotic rates between 2.2 for the density and 1.5 for the internal energy (fig. 12), although the cost in computing time kept it from resolutions as high as those of the single-step test.

The cause of the difference between the two methods is uncertain ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). As CT is built as an intrinsically two-dimensional scheme that always updates both poloidal components, the wind runs on a finite slice of polar angle, with periodic boundaries in that direction, so that angular variations stay small next to the radial ones; the finite angular extent may still affect the rates. For a code that is fully one-dimensional, [Hawley](#hawley-1989) (1989) reports single-step rates of 1.0 to 1.7 in all variables. The rates near 2 that the second-order van Leer method should give, and gave for advection in the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a), at 2.1, are not reached throughout, which suggests that a step other than the radial advection dominates the error. It may be the arithmetic averaging that the staggered mesh needs for some variables, such as the momentum density from density and velocity, or, as the radial velocity varies in space, the need for a velocity-corrected transport that accounts for the structure of the velocity within a zone ([Finn and Hawley](#finn-hawley-1989) 1989) to reach second order with van Leer. The question needs further work.

Convergence tests, single-step ones above all, reveal subtle bugs well ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.3). A rate near zero, or a very erratic one, for some variable suggests a bug, and as single steps largely decouple the rates of the variables, they often point to the equation at fault, which almost no other kind of test can do.

#### 2.9.5 The coronal transients of Low

A fully two-dimensional test is vital to verify the multidimensional algorithms ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.4). The two-dimensional wind solutions of [Sakurai](#sakurai-1985) (1985) rest on a very elaborate numerical method of their own, which makes them hard to use as tests, and multidimensional stellar winds were still an active field of research. The analytic, time-dependent models of solar coronal transients in two and three dimensions of [Low](#low-1984) (1984, and works cited there) serve instead. As Low gives such solutions for a variety of situations, even with self-gravity, they may become standard tests for MHD codes.

Low built his models to study coronal transients, violent ejections of coronal plasma, often, that intense observation of the corona had revealed over the preceding decade ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.4; [Hundhausen](#hundhausen-1989) 1989). The corona proved much more active than thought: transients may occur twice a day, with energies of $10^{31}$ erg, ejecting up to $10^{15}$ g of matter each. In the models, a hot, magnetised bubble has been ejected at $t = t_0$ and is driven into a spherically symmetric, unmagnetised ambient medium. Where the two media meet, at the contact surface, a strong shock forms and runs ahead, heating and compressing the ambient gas. With the shock at $R_S$ and the contact surface at $R_C$, the flow later has three regions. Inside $R_C$, a spherically symmetric radial outflow carries an axially symmetric field, whose stresses are balanced by the pressure gradients of an axially symmetric distribution of pressure and density that moves with the outflow. Between $R_C$ and $R_S$, a shell of hot, compressed, unmagnetised ambient gas flows radially outward. Beyond $R_S$, the undisturbed ambient medium extends to infinity.

The spherically symmetric outflow follows from the jump conditions of a strong shock and the properties of isentropic flow ([Courant and Friedrichs](#courant-friedrichs-1948) 1948), and the axially symmetric magnetic part from the methods of [Low](#low-1984) (1984), which the paper does not repeat ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.4, table 1). Inside $R_C$, $p_s = (4\nu^3)^{-1}(GM/r)^4$, $\rho_s = \nu^{-3}(GM/r)^3$ and $v_r = r/t$. In the shell between $R_C$ and $R_S$, $p_s = (7/6)\Phi^{-4}d_0\eta R_0^2\exp[2GMR_0^6/(3\eta\zeta^9)]$, $\rho_s = 7\Phi^{-3}d_0(R_0/\zeta)^8\exp[2GMR_0^6/(3\eta\zeta^9)]$ and again $v_r = r/t$. Beyond $R_S$, the gas is at rest with $\rho_s = d_0(R_0/r)^{26/7}\exp\{[2GM/(3\eta R_0^3)](R_0/r)^{9/7}\}$. Here $\Phi = \eta^{1/2}t$, a dimensionless time and not the potential of 2.6, $\zeta = R_0\Phi^{1/6}$, $R_C = \zeta_C\Phi$, $R_S = R_0\Phi^{7/6}$ and $d_0 = 10^8m\exp[-2GM/(3\eta R_0^3)]$. The table corrects five misprints in the data of Low's own table for the radial outflow.

The magnetic medium inside $R_C$ follows from two arbitrary generating functions, of the poloidal and of the toroidal field, and of Low's five examples the second, of his §II, proved the most convenient ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.4, table 2). It gives $B_r = 2A_0r^{-2}[P_0 + (\lambda\zeta)^{1/2}J_{3/2}(\lambda\zeta)]\cos\theta$, $B_\theta = -A_0\lambda(r\Phi)^{-1}[(\lambda\zeta)^{1/2}J_{1/2}(\lambda\zeta) - (\lambda\zeta)^{-1/2}J_{3/2}(\lambda\zeta)]\sin\theta$ and $B_\phi = A_0\lambda(r\Phi)^{-1}[P_0 + (\lambda\zeta)^{1/2}J_{3/2}(\lambda\zeta)]\sin\theta$, with Bessel functions $J$. It adds $(4\pi r^4)^{-1}A_0P_0(2 - \lambda^2\zeta^2)\tilde A$ to the pressure and $(2\pi GMr^3)^{-1}A_0P_0(4 - \lambda^2\zeta^2)\tilde A$ to the density of table 1, where $\tilde A = A_0[P_0 + (\lambda\zeta)^{1/2}J_{3/2}(\lambda\zeta)]\sin^2\theta$.

The parameters, in cgs units, are $\lambda = 5.54 \times 10^{-11}$ cm$^{-1}$, $A_0 = 1.5 \times 10^{21}$ G cm$^2$, $P_0 = 1.01327$, $\zeta_C = 1.104 \times 10^{11}$ cm, $\eta = 5.24 \times 10^{-8}$ s$^{-2}$, $\nu = 2.42 \times 10^{20}$ cm$^3$ s$^{-2}$ g$^{-1/3}$, $R_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{11}$ cm, $t_0 = 8.74 \times 10^3$ s, $M = 2.0 \times 10^{33}$ g, $m = 1.673 \times 10^{-24}$ g and $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-8}$ cm$^3$ s$^{-2}$ g$^{-1}$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, table 3). The pressure of the unshocked ambient medium is not specified. For so strong a shock, the jump conditions do not depend on it, but it cannot be arbitrary, since it must hold the ambient medium in hydrostatic equilibrium against the gravity of the Sun. Rather than evaluate that pressure, the test ignores gravity beyond $R_S$. **[GK]** Table 3 prints $\lambda = 5.54 \times 10^{11}$ cm$^{-1}$, but $\lambda\zeta$ must be of order unity, which needs $10^{-11}$. With it, $x_C = \lambda\zeta_C = 6.116$, and $\cos x_C - \sin x_C/x_C = 1.01327$, the value of $P_0$. As the shell outside $R_C$ is unmagnetised, $\tilde A$ should vanish at the contact surface, which it does if the term $(\lambda\zeta)^{1/2}J_{3/2}(\lambda\zeta)$ stands for $\sin(\lambda\zeta)/(\lambda\zeta) - \cos(\lambda\zeta)$, that is $(\pi/2)^{1/2}$ times its value with the standard Bessel function.

**[GK]** In tables 1 and 2, $\zeta$ must be the similarity variable $r/\Phi$: only then does the derivative of $\tilde A$ give $B_\theta$, and only then do the formulae for the shell conserve mass and balance pressure against gravity in the coasting flow $v_r = r/t$. The notes of table 1 define instead $\zeta = R_0\Phi^{1/6}$, its value at the shock, as $R_C = \zeta_C\Phi$ places the contact surface at $\zeta = \zeta_C$. At the shock the formulae satisfy the jump conditions of a strong shock for $\gamma = 4/3$: the density is seven times that of the ambient gas, the velocity 6/7 of the shock speed, and the pressure 6/7 of the ambient density times the square of the shock speed. The paper does not state $\gamma$, and [Low](#low-1984) (1984) notes that the original self-similar theory requires 4/3.

The analytic solution of tables 1 to 3 initialises a grid of 128 zones in radius and 80 in polar angle in spherical polar coordinates, on $r \in (1 \times 10^{11}, 5 \times 10^{11})$ cm and $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.4). Although the models are symmetric about the equator, the run covers the full range of $\theta$ to check that the code keeps this symmetry. The lower radial boundary follows the time-dependent analytic solution, the outer one is an outflow boundary, and both polar boundaries are axes of symmetry. The run ends at $t = 2.62 \times 10^4$ s, the time of Low's last plot of this solution, which allows a comparison with his figs. 5 to 7, and the analytic solution gives the error at any time. Fig. 13 shows contours of density, poloidal field lines and contours of the toroidal field at $\Phi = 2$, 4 and 6, and numerical and analytic solutions agree well. A magnetic bubble leaves the corona behind the shock and the contact surface, which have both left the grid by the final time.

Fig. 14 quantifies the agreement with the relative errors at the end of the run, at most 6.16 per cent in the density, 6.86 per cent in the radial velocity, and 3.07 and 9.46 per cent in the radial and the toroidal field ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.4). Two features dominate them. A wave of error comes from the discontinuities of the solution at $t = t_0$, which no finite resolution can represent exactly, as in every test with shocks. The last zone in radius carries a large error from the outflow boundary, which handles submagnetosonic outflow poorly, so that some problems may need a better condition.

#### 2.9.6 Magnetic braking of an aligned rotator

Here a homogeneous cylinder, the disc, of density $\rho_d$ lies in a homogeneous ambient medium of density $\rho_x$, both threaded by a uniform axial field of strength $B_0$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.5). At $t = 0$ the disc is set rotating at a constant angular velocity. The shear at its surfaces launches torsional Alfvén waves along the field lines, which spin up the ambient medium and brake the disc, and the solution follows the angular velocities of disc and medium as these waves travel. [Mouschovias and Paleologou](#mouschovias-paleologou-1980) (1980) give an exact, time-dependent analytic solution, which makes the problem an excellent test of the propagation of torsional Alfvén waves.

The solution of [Mouschovias and Paleologou](#mouschovias-paleologou-1980) is dimensionless, with $B_0 = 1$, $\rho_x = 1$ and lengths in units of the half-thickness $Z = 1$ of the disc, so that time is measured in Alfvén crossing times of the disc at the Alfvén speed of the ambient medium ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.5). The single parameter $\alpha = \rho_d/\rho_x$, which equals $\rho_d$ in these units, fixes the braking time and characterises the solutions, which spacetime diagrams of the Alfvén waves make clear (fig. 15). For $\rho_d = \rho_x$ the density is continuous at the surfaces of the disc, and the waves launched there travel to infinity without reflection, as two square pulses, in the rotational velocity and in the field, moving apart at the Alfvén speed. For $\rho_d > \rho_x$ the waves reflect partially at the surfaces of the disc, with the reflection coefficient $\mathcal{R} = (\alpha^{1/2} - 1)/(\alpha^{1/2} + 1)$, and each partial transmission sends a new wavefront into the ambient medium, so that the solution forms a staircase whose steps count the reflections so far. The mean angular velocity of the disc then decays exponentially, on the braking time $t_{MB} = (\rho_d/\rho_x)(Z/v_{A,ext})$, which is $\alpha$ in these units. [Mouschovias and Paleologou](#mouschovias-paleologou-1980) give the full solution for a discontinuous initial condition (DIC), with the rotational velocity jumping at the surface of the disc, and a continuous one (CIC), with the rotational velocity decreasing smoothly, like a cosine, to zero outside the disc, and [Stone et al.](#stone-et-al-1992b) (1992) give Fortran subroutines that compute both. **[GK]** The paper prints $\mathcal{R} = [\alpha^{1/2} + 1]/[\alpha^{1/2} - 1]$, which exceeds 1 and diverges for $\alpha = 1$, where the text itself finds no reflection. The amplitude reflection coefficient at the density jump is its inverse.

The test was run with the MOC of ZEUS-2D for several density ratios and both initial conditions, with very small amplitudes of the torsional waves, so that gradients of the toroidal field do not launch compressive waves that disturb the evolution ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.5). Fig. 16 shows the DIC for a density ratio of 10, with van Leer and PPA interpolation in the MOC update, on 300 zones covering $z \in (0, 15)$, of which 20 lie in the disc, $z < 1$. The run stops at $t = 13$, shortly before the leading wavefront reaches the edge of the grid. Rotational velocity and toroidal field agree excellently with the analytic solution, and the widths of the discontinuities match the advection tests: about 14 zones with van Leer and about six with PPA without the steepener. Of the schemes tried, only the MOC gives suitable results. Straightforward differencing of the coupled toroidal terms of the equation of motion and the induction equation gives an unacceptable dispersion error, which only very diffusive schemes, the Lax–Friedrichs or Lax–Wendroff methods for instance, damp, at the price of smearing sharp features of the flow.

With the CIC, fig. 17, the agreement is again excellent, although van Leer, unlike the more accurate PPA, does not resolve the minima of the angular velocity correctly ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.5). This run stops later, at $t = 15$, after the first wavefront has left the grid, and the outflow boundary does not reflect it: with the MOC, the outflow boundaries are perfectly transparent to torsional Alfvén waves, a welcome consequence of upwind differencing. A very large density ratio, which gives a long braking time, followed over many braking times, shows that the diffusion of the interpolation does not keep the MOC from following how the angular velocity of the disc decays exponentially. Last, the test was repeated in spherical polar coordinates, to see whether the directionally split MOC holds in two dimensions. The Alfvén waves then follow the axial field, crossing the grid lines instead of following them, and meet a curved outer boundary. The method still carries them axially, without spurious polar motion along the curved grid lines, and they pass the outer boundary cleanly, without reflection.

#### 2.9.7 Shear Alfvén waves

The need for the MOC for torsional Alfvén waves first led to MOC-CT for the poloidal field, and the poloidal, or shear, analogue of the braking problem shows why ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.6). In Cartesian geometry, a "disc", the region $x < 1$, given an initial shear motion normal to the field lines instead of a toroidal one, poses the same problem with the same solution, so that the solutions of [Mouschovias and Paleologou](#mouschovias-paleologou-1980) (1980) check how shear Alfvén waves travel along poloidal field lines. In the runs of figs. 18 and 19, the 2-velocity is perturbed in $1 < x < 2$, or in $2 < x < 3$ for the moving fluid, which launches two square pulses of $v_2$ and $B_2$ that travel in opposite directions along the field lines.

Fig. 18 shows a shear wave travelling along $x_1$, with the DIC and a density ratio of one, under the original EMF of CT by [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988), which upwinds the field components with the fluid velocity rather than along the Alfvén characteristics ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §4.6). Its large dispersion error is plain, and the result is nearly identical to that of the first, straightforward differencing of the toroidal field. This test, together with the search for an accurate algorithm for the toroidal waves, led to MOC-CT, which reduces to the MOC of the toroidal field for $v = 0$ and essentially to the original method for $v_A = 0$. With MOC-CT, fig. 19, a stationary fluid, $v_x = 0$, gives the same results as the toroidal test, and the shear waves evolve accurately. In a moving fluid, $v_x = 1.5$, the fronts should travel at $v_x \pm v_A$, which with $v_A = 1.0$ gives 0.5 for the backward and 2.5 for the forward waves, and the pulses travel at these speeds.

#### 2.9.8 Conclusions from the tests

The summary restates the design: CT, after [Evans and Hawley](#evans-hawley-1988) (1988), keeps the field free of divergence, and the MOC propagates Alfvén waves accurately ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §5). The advection tests, which mainly catch coding errors, match the quality of the hydrodynamic ones, and CT, unlike some methods based on the vector potential, gives monotonic profiles of the current density. The Riemann problem shows that the algorithms follow the new kinds of wave of MHD, such as compound waves, and how much more complex it is than its hydrodynamic counterpart. The winds of [Weber and Davis](#weber-davis-1967) check the coupling of velocity and field in a rotating outflow, and their convergence tests show that the full code converges at less than second order, for reasons that need further work. Convergence tests in general quantify the overall performance of a code well, and help to find subtle bugs. The coronal transients of [Low](#low-1984) (1984), the only two-dimensional MHD test, agree excellently, and such solutions could serve as standard tests of multidimensional MHD codes.

Magnetic braking of an aligned rotator ranks among the most important tests, as it checks the propagation of nonlinear Alfvén waves ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §5). Of the methods tried, only one based on the MOC propagated steep nonlinear Alfvén waves suitably. The others had either an unacceptable dispersion error, such as schemes upwind only in the fluid velocity, with oscillations of large amplitude and short wavelength, or too much diffusion, such as Lax–Wendroff. Adding much diffusion would indeed cure the dispersion error. The MOC for the toroidal field and velocity instead carries torsional Alfvén waves accurately with little diffusion, and MOC-CT does the same for poloidal Alfvén waves. The tests thus mark MOC-CT as an important and necessary improvement for evolving all MHD wave modes accurately, and the authors hope that the algorithms will be a solid foundation for later numerical MHD.

### 2.10 The radiation moment equations

#### 2.10.1 Radiating fluids and their closure

In a radiating fluid, the radiation holds a significant share of the energy, momentum and stress of the fluid, or dominates the exchange of energy ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2). The conservation laws of the matter must then be extended by those of the radiation, which are derived in the same way. As the equations of gas dynamics are velocity moments of the Boltzmann equation for the distribution function of the particles, the equations of radiation dynamics are angular moments of the transfer equation, the equation that describes photons in phase space as massless particles in the extreme relativistic limit. Both hierarchies of moments pose a closure problem, as the first $n$ equations contain the moment of order $n + 1$. Gas dynamics closes them with an equation of state, relating the pressure to the densities of mass and energy, which presumes a mean free path much shorter than every length scale, so that strict local equilibrium holds and the distribution is very nearly isotropic.

The radiation equations could be closed likewise by taking the specific intensity, the distribution function of the photons, as isotropic, which leads to the diffusion approximation, used in one form or another in most calculations of radiation hydrodynamics ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2). A photon's mean free path is in general not short compared with the length scales of the system, however, near boundaries or where the medium is optically thin, so the radiation is not isotropic and no simple closure exists. ZEUS-2D therefore solves the transfer equation for the specific intensity as a function of direction, and computes from it the relation between two moments, the radiation pressure and the energy density, which closes the moment equations. This internal consistency makes the method apply to flows that contain or develop optically thin regions, where methods based on diffusion run into trouble.

#### 2.10.2 The comoving moment equations

The moment equations must first be written in a frame of reference, which is not trivial, as they contain frame-dependent terms of order $v/c$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.1). The frame must suit the problem, and the equations must keep every term of order $v/c$ that can arise in it in any physical regime. Failing to tell frames apart, or keeping terms of inconsistent order within one frame, can cause serious errors. Gas dynamics escapes the difficulty, as the frame-dependent terms of a fluid without radiation are only of order $v^2/c^2$, the kinetic energy density being the most familiar.

ZEUS-2D uses the comoving, or Lagrangian, frame, which keeps each fluid parcel momentarily at rest ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.1). In this frame the properties of the matter are isotropic, which makes the interaction of radiation and matter easiest to treat, and its microphysics follows from statistical mechanics. The frame also matches the hydrodynamics, whose variables are Lagrangian ones solved on an Eulerian mesh, and admits tools such as adaptive meshes. Against these advantages, the equations take a more complex form in the comoving frame. Solving them only to order unity, which is fully justified for nonrelativistic flows with $v/c \ll 1$, limits this complexity, but restricts the radiation module to such flows from the start.

The equations to order unity follow from a dimensional analysis of those to order $v/c$, which in turn follow from the comoving transfer equation ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.1). That equation can be derived by a transformation of the transfer equation of the inertial frame to order $v/c$, which must carry every variable and derivative over to the comoving frame, or directly, without an inertial frame, from the Boltzmann equation of the photons. The paper takes both derivations from the literature ([Buchler](#buchler-1979) 1979; [Castor](#castor-1972) 1972; [Kaneko, Morita and Maekawa](#kaneko-et-al-1984) 1984; [Lindquist](#lindquist-1966) 1966; B. W. [Mihalas](#mihalas-1988) 1988; [Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) 1984). To order $v/c$, the moment equations of zeroth and first order, integrated over frequency, read in the comoving frame $\rho\,D(E_0/\rho)/Dt + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}_0 + 2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{F}_0/c^2 + \nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}_0 = \int_0^\infty[4\pi\eta_0(\nu_0) - c\chi_0(\nu_0)E_0(\nu_0)]\,d\nu_0$ (eq. 1) and $(\rho/c^2)\,D(\mathbf{F}_0/\rho)/Dt + \nabla\cdot\mathsf{P}_0 + (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathsf{P}_0 + \mathbf{a}E_0)/c^2 + (\nabla\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{F}_0)/c^2 = -c^{-1}\int_0^\infty\chi_0(\nu_0)\mathbf{F}_0(\nu_0)\,d\nu_0$ (eq. 2). Here $E_0$, $\mathbf{F}_0$ and $\mathsf{P}_0$ are the radiation energy density, flux and stress tensor in the comoving frame, $\mathbf{a} = D\mathbf{v}/Dt$ is the acceleration of the fluid, and the emissivity $\eta_0$ and the extinction coefficient $\chi_0$ have their usual meaning. With four equations for ten unknowns, the components of $E_0$, $\mathbf{F}_0$ and $\mathsf{P}_0$, the system needs a closure, the tensor variable Eddington factor.

Each term has a physical meaning ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.1; [Castor](#castor-1972) 1972). In the energy equation, eq. 1, the terms stand, in turn, for the temporal change of radiation energy, its transport, the equivalent inertia of the flowing radiant energy, the work of the radiation pressure, and the energy gained from the matter, each per unit volume. In the momentum equation, eq. 2, they stand for the temporal change of radiation momentum, its transport, the equivalent inertia of the radiation's enthalpy density, the coupling of radiation momentum to a shearing flow, and the momentum gained from the matter. To order $v/c$, the two equations contain all dynamical effects of radiation on the time scale of the fluid flow ([Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) 1984). As moments, they also remove the angles from the problem and lower its dimensionality, which is why the radiation field is evolved by the moment equations rather than, say, by the time-dependent transfer equation.

The equations are reduced to order unity by dropping the terms of order $v/c$ in all physical regimes, while every term that is of order unity in some regime stays, even if formally of order $v/c$ in another, as such terms can dominate elsewhere ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.1). Three regimes matter. In the streaming limit, $\lambda_p/l \ge 1$ for the mean free path $\lambda_p$ of a photon and a length scale $l$ of the system, radiation streams through an optically thin fluid, with $P \approx E$ and $F \approx cE$. In the static diffusion limit, $v/c \ll \lambda_p/l$, it diffuses through an optically thick medium nearly at rest, with $P \approx E/3$ and $F/cE$ of order $\lambda_p/l$. In the dynamic diffusion limit, $v/c \ge \lambda_p/l$, a moving optically thick medium carries it along, with $P = E/3$ and $F/cE$ of order $v/c$. On the time scale of the fluid flow, time derivatives scale as $v/l$ and gradients as $1/l$.

By these scalings, the inertial term of the energy equation is never of order unity, while each of its other terms is so in some regime, so that the rate of change, the transport, the work of the radiation pressure and the interaction with matter stay ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.1). In the momentum equation, the transport and the interaction with matter reach order unity, and the terms in the acceleration and the shear stay below; the rate of change, the transport and the interaction are kept. The comoving equations to order unity are thus $\rho\,D(E_0/\rho)/Dt + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}_0 + \nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}_0 = \int_0^\infty[4\pi\eta_0(\nu_0) - c\chi_0(\nu_0)E_0(\nu_0)]\,d\nu_0$ (eq. 3) and $(\rho/c^2)\,D(\mathbf{F}_0/\rho)/Dt + \nabla\cdot\mathsf{P}_0 = -c^{-1}\int_0^\infty\chi_0(\nu_0)\mathbf{F}_0(\nu_0)\,d\nu_0$ (eq. 4). They capture every dynamical effect of radiation on a nonrelativistic fluid. **[GK]** By the scalings given, the first term of eq. 2 is at most of order $v/c$ on the time scale of the fluid flow, so that the stated criterion alone would drop it, yet eq. 4 keeps it. On the time scale of the radiation flow, $l/c$, it is of order unity in the streaming limit.

#### 2.10.3 The equations of radiation MHD

Radiation acts on the matter by exchanging momentum, which accelerates or decelerates the flow, and energy, which heats or cools it, so that the equations of motion and energy gain terms for the interaction ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.2). With the internal energy equations of matter and radiation added, the complete equations of RMHD are the continuity equation, $D\rho/Dt + \rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ (eq. 5), the equation of motion, $\rho\,D\mathbf{v}/Dt = -\nabla p - \rho\nabla\Phi + (1/4\pi)(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\times\mathbf{B} + c^{-1}\chi_F\mathbf{F}$ (eq. 6), the radiation energy equation, $\rho\,D(E/\rho)/Dt = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} - \nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P} + 4\pi\kappa_PB - c\kappa_EE$ (eq. 7), the total internal energy equation, $\rho\,D[(e + E)/\rho]/Dt = -\nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P} - p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}$ (eq. 8), the radiation momentum equation, $(\rho/c^2)\,D(\mathbf{F}/\rho)/Dt = -\nabla\cdot\mathsf{P} - c^{-1}\chi_F\mathbf{F}$ (eq. 9), the induction equation (eq. 10) and the Poisson equation (eq. 11). The radiation variables, now written without subscripts, remain those of the comoving frame. LTE is assumed, so that the source function $S = \eta/\chi$ equals the Planck function $B$, a scalar not to be confused with the field $\mathbf{B}$. Three mean opacities appear: the flux mean $\chi_F$, defined by $\chi_F\mathbf{F} = \int_0^\infty\chi(\nu)\mathbf{F}(\nu)\,d\nu$, the Planck mean $\kappa_P = B^{-1}\int_0^\infty\chi(\nu)B(\nu)\,d\nu$ and the energy mean $\kappa_E = E^{-1}\int_0^\infty\chi(\nu)E(\nu)\,d\nu$ (eqs. 12 to 14).

Combining the energy equations of matter and radiation into one, eq. 8, the first law of thermodynamics of the radiating fluid, rather than solving them separately, cancels the terms of their interaction, which keeps the scheme accurate near radiative equilibrium ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.2). Constitutive relations close the set: $p(\rho, e)$, $\chi_F(\rho, e)$, $\kappa_E(\rho, e)$, $\kappa_P(\rho, e)$ and $B(\rho, e)$. The moment equations, eqs. 7 to 9, are closed by a tensor variable Eddington factor $\mathsf{f}$: $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{f}E$ (eq. 15) replaces the radiation stress tensor, the second angular moment, by the energy density, the zeroth. Its components are quadratures of the specific intensity over solid angle, $\mathsf{f} = \oint I\,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}\,d\omega/\oint I\,d\omega$ (eq. 16), and $I(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{n}, \nu)$ is found by solving the transfer equation $\partial I/\partial s = \chi(S - I)$ formally (eq. 17).

#### 2.10.4 Full transport without iteration

A closure by a tensor variable Eddington factor, computed by formally solving the transfer equation with its dependence on angle and frequency, was long thought too costly for multidimensional calculations, which therefore mostly used the nonequilibrium diffusion approximation, with the Eddington approximation $f_{ii} = 1/3$ and $f_{ij} = 0$ for $i \ne j$, and no formal solution ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.2). This cuts the computational cost considerably, but suits only optically thick flows with isotropic radiation, and may fail wherever the radiation streams. No prior test tells whether some part of the grid will turn optically thin, and diffusion applied to optically thin flows raises the well-known problem of flux limiting, whose numerical fixes, the flux limiters, are of unknown generality and reliability. Only full transport treats correctly situations expected in many astrophysical systems, such as a local optically thin region, the envelope of an accretion disc, irradiated directly by a remote intense source, the central object.

ZEUS-2D therefore solves the moment equations, eqs. 7 to 9, and the transfer equation, eq. 17, in two dimensions, the latter for the Eddington factors of eq. 16 ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.2). To keep the problem tractable, the moment equations are solved with the Eddington factors taken as known, and the factors are then updated from the new structure of the fluid. Ideally the two would be solved together, by iterating between them to consistency, but the moment equations can take ten times the computing time of all other hydrodynamic algorithms of an explicit code together, so that they can be solved only once per time step. Solving the two separately, without iteration, works because the Eddington factor measures the anisotropy of the radiation, whose geometry usually changes little over one or more time steps, so that lagging the factors by a step or more causes no serious error. This fails where the system evolves on the time scale of the radiation flow, as when an ionisation front enters a cold medium or breaks out of hot, dense material into near vacuum, and iteration is then essential. Still, closing the moment equations with Eddington factors from a formal solution, rather than with arbitrary flux limiters, makes the full transport of ZEUS-2D improve considerably on flux-limited diffusion. **[GK]** The two later copies of the code, Stone's version 2.0 of 2003 and release 2.0.3 of 2007, contain no solver of the transfer equation. They close the moment equations with Eddington factors computed from flux limiters, in the manner of flux-limited diffusion, while keeping the Newton–Raphson solution and the AFL formula of 2.10.5 and 2.10.6.

The equations rest on three assumptions ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §2.2). Keeping only terms of order unity excludes relativistic flows, consistent with the nonrelativistic MHD. Assuming LTE makes the source function of eq. 7 the Planck function. Relaxing LTE would need a delicate system of rate equations for the thermodynamic state of the matter, and as the coupled moment and rate equations already need iteration, as in stellar atmospheres, multidimensional non-LTE radiation hydrodynamics would need far more computing than was available; in one dimension, [Klein et al.](#klein-et-al-1988) (1988) had treated non-LTE, frequency-dependent transfer coupled to hydrodynamics. Last, the dependence on frequency is suppressed, with frequency-integrated variables and mean opacities. Frequency-dependent transfer is practical for static problems and one-dimensional hydrodynamics, where methods such as multigroup ones are well developed, and future work may consider it, both for the dynamics and for maps of the emergent intensity computed from finished flows.

#### 2.10.5 Implicit moment equations and automatic flux limiting

The moment equations are split like the hydrodynamics into a source and a transport step, after the Lagrangian derivatives are expanded with the continuity equation ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3). The source step solves difference forms of $\partial E/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} - \nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P} + 4\pi\kappa_PB - c\kappa_EE$, $\partial(e + E)/\partial t = -\nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P} - p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}$ and $c^{-2}\,\partial\mathbf{F}/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\mathsf{P} - c^{-1}\chi_F\mathbf{F}$ (eqs. 18 to 20). The transport step conserves $E$, $e + E$ and $\mathbf{F}$ in integral form, as $d/dt\int_V q\,dV = -\oint_{\partial V}q(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_g)\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ for each of them (eqs. 21 to 23), with the grid velocity $\mathbf{v}_g$. As $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector, applying the scalar divergence theorem to it misses terms from the divergence acting on the unit vectors in curvilinear coordinates, as for the equation of motion of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a).

The moment equations are coupled equations of advection and diffusion ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3). In the streaming limit, with $F \approx cE$, the energy equation becomes $\partial E/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot cE$, an advection at the speed of light. In the static diffusion limit of a static medium in radiative equilibrium, with $F \approx -(c/3\chi)\nabla E$, it becomes $\partial E/\partial t = \nabla\cdot(c/3\chi)\nabla E$, a diffusion with coefficient $D \approx c/3\chi$. An explicit method would face Courant limits set by $c$ and by an effective viscosity $\nu = D$, far more restrictive than those of the hydrodynamics. As systems are to evolve on dynamical rather than light-crossing time scales, the hydrodynamics stays explicit, but the moment equations must be solved implicitly.

In two dimensions, with the Eddington tensor given, eqs. 18 to 20 are four equations for $e$, $E$ and the two components of $\mathbf{F}$ that do not vanish, a considerable challenge to solve implicitly ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3). Automatic flux limiting (AFL), after [Mihalas and Weaver](#mihalas-weaver-1982) (1982), reduces their number by integrating the radiation momentum equation approximately but analytically in time. Written as $\partial\mathbf{F}/\partial t + c\chi_F\mathbf{F} = -c^2\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{f}E)$ (eq. 24), it has the integrating factor $e^{c\chi_Ft}$, so that integration over a time step of the flow gives $\mathbf{F}(t + \Delta t)e^{c\chi_F(t + \Delta t)} - \mathbf{F}(t)e^{c\chi_Ft} = -c^2\int_t^{t + \Delta t}\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{f}E)e^{c\chi_Ft}\,dt$ (eq. 25). With averages $\langle\chi_F\rangle$ and $\langle\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{f}E)\rangle$ over the step, it becomes $\mathbf{F}(t + \Delta t) = e^{-x}\mathbf{F}(t) - (1 - e^{-x})(c/\langle\chi_F\rangle)\langle\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{f}E)\rangle$, with $x = c\langle\chi_F\rangle\Delta t$ (eq. 26).

The exponentially attenuated prescription is stable, and copes with the stiffness that arises when $x$, the distance a photon flies in $\Delta t$ over its mean free path, is much larger than unity ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3). Its accuracy depends on how well the averages are approximated. They are centred variably in time, $\langle X\rangle = X^{n+\alpha} = \alpha X^{n+1} + (1 - \alpha)X^n$, with $\alpha$ free but above 0.5 for stability. Inserted into eqs. 18 and 19, eq. 26 leaves two coupled implicit equations for $e$ and $E$, highly nonlinear, as the opacities and the Planck function vary with the temperature, for which methods in two dimensions are at hand. **[GK]** For $x \gg 1$, eq. 26 gives $\mathbf{F} = -(c/\chi_F)\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{f}E)$, the diffusion limit, which for $\mathsf{f} = \mathsf{I}/3$ is Fick's law $\mathbf{F} = -(c/3\chi_F)\nabla E$. For $x \ll 1$ it gives the explicit update $\mathbf{F}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{F}(t) - c^2\Delta t\,\nabla\cdot(\mathsf{f}E)$, so that the flux passes smoothly between the two limits.

#### 2.10.6 Centring and the implicit source step

Following the rule of 2.2.1 for scalars, vectors and tensors, $E$, $P11$, $P22$ and $P33$ lie at $(x1b_i, x2b_j)$, $F1$ at $(x1a_i, x2b_j)$, $F2$ at $(x1b_i, x2a_j)$ and $P12$ at $(x1a_i, x2a_j)$, and the components of the Eddington tensor lie with those of $\mathsf{P}$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3.1, fig. 1). The mean opacities and the Planck function follow from the zone-centred densities of mass and energy, so that $\kappa_E$, $\kappa_P$ and $B$ are zone-centred, and $\chi_F$ lies on the faces. An energy-weighted harmonic mean gives the opacities at the faces, which, without an adaptive mesh, is needed for reasonable transport of energy across unresolved interfaces ([Mihalas and Mihalas](#mihalas-mihalas-1984) 1984). The velocity gradient, a tensor of rank two, is centred like $\mathsf{P}$, as in the tensor artificial viscosity of 2.3.4.

The source step differences eqs. 18 and 19 in time as $(E^{n+1} - E^n)/\Delta t = 4\pi\kappa_P^{n+\theta}B^{n+\theta} - c\kappa_E^{n+\theta}E^{n+\theta} - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}^{n+\theta} - \nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}^{n+\theta}$ and $(e^{n+1} + E^{n+1} - e^n - E^n)/\Delta t = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}^{n+\theta} - \nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}^{n+\theta} - p^{n+\theta}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, eqs. 27 and 28). Every quantity on the right is centred as $X^{n+\theta} = \theta X^{n+1} + (1 - \theta)X^n$, with $\theta$ free but above 0.5 for stability ([Richtmyer and Morton](#richtmyer-morton-1957) 1957), and $\mathbf{F}^{n+1}$ in $\mathbf{F}^{n+\theta}$ comes from the AFL formula, eq. 26. Differenced in space on the staggered mesh, the equations become the zone-centred conditions $E^{n+1}_{i,j} - E^n_{i,j} - \Delta t[4\pi\kappa^{n+\theta}_{P,i,j}B^{n+\theta}_{i,j} - c\kappa^{n+\theta}_{E,i,j}E^{n+\theta}_{i,j} - (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}^{n+\theta})_{i,j} - (\nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}^{n+\theta})_{i,j}] = 0$ and $e^{n+1}_{i,j} + E^{n+1}_{i,j} - e^n_{i,j} - E^n_{i,j} + \Delta t[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}^{n+\theta})_{i,j} + (\nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}^{n+\theta})_{i,j} + p^{n+\theta}_{i,j}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i,j}] = 0$ (eqs. 29 and 30), with the terms of 2.10.8 and the divergence of the velocity of 2.2.4. On the staggered grid in two dimensions, the divergence of the flux with the AFL formula couples nine radiation energy densities, and the averages of the opacity on the faces couple five material energy densities.

The difference equations are very nonlinear, as the opacities and the Planck function depend on $e$ and $E$, and are solved by linearising and iterating, with the Newton–Raphson method for its simplicity, robustness and quadratic convergence ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3.2). For a system $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ (eq. 31), each iteration solves $\mathsf{J}(\mathbf{x}^\nu)\,\delta\mathbf{x}^\nu = -\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^\nu)$ with the Jacobian $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ (eq. 32), starting usually from the solution of the last time step, and updates $\mathbf{x}^{\nu+1} = \mathbf{x}^\nu + \delta\mathbf{x}^\nu$ (eq. 33) until $\delta\mathbf{x}^\nu < \epsilon$. For a grid of about $N$ zones on a side, the moment equations form $2N^2$ coupled equations, each depending on nine radiation and five material energy densities, so that eq. 32 is a large, sparse, banded matrix system. With $\mathbf{x}_{i,j} = (e_{i,j}, E_{i,j})$, it sets $a1_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i-1,j+1} + a2_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i,j+1} + a3_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i+1,j+1} + a4_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i-1,j} + a5_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i,j} + a6_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i+1,j} + a7_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i-1,j-1} + a8_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i,j-1} + a9_{i,j}\mathbf{x}_{i+1,j-1}$ equal to the right-hand side (eq. 34), where the coefficients are $2 \times 2$ blocks of Jacobian derivatives (fig. 2).

An implicit method must build the boundary conditions into the matrix itself ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3.2). Neumann conditions, with zero gradient, change the diagonal elements, the coefficients of $e_{i,j}$ and $E_{i,j}$ in the equations of zone $(i,j)$, and Dirichlet conditions, with prescribed values, change the right-hand side. The five boundary conditions of the hydrodynamics serve the radiation too: reflecting, axial and outflow conditions are of Neumann type, and inflow of Dirichlet type. With difference molecules of nine and five points, the Jacobian of each of eqs. 29 and 30 needs fourteen derivatives, which are tedious to derive with the boundary conditions, and which the paper offers on request. Assembled into the sparse matrix with the right-hand side and an initial guess, they go to a sparse linear solver, a form of the generalised minimal residual method (GMRES) ([Bramley](#bramley-1989) 1989, private communication). Robust and efficient as it is, it converges poorly at very large Courant numbers, when the hydrodynamic time step far exceeds the explicit stability limit, and better solvers were being implemented. As most of the computing time of the radiation module goes into sparse systems, often several per time step because of the Newton–Raphson iteration, the performance of the solver matters greatly.

The iteration is controlled as in the code WH80s ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3.2; [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) 1986). No Newton–Raphson iteration may change the solution by more than about 10 per cent, nor may all iterations together change a variable by more than about 10 per cent. The solution has converged when successive iterations change it by less than $\epsilon \approx 10^{-5}$, typically after 4 to 8 iterations. Without convergence after 20, the step is shortened and the iteration begins anew, and the moment equations then advance with the shorter step until one full hydrodynamic step is done, before control returns to the hydrodynamics. More than six reductions count as divergence, and stop the computation.

#### 2.10.7 The transport step of the radiation

After the implicit update, the radiation variables are advected explicitly, exactly like the hydrodynamic variables of 2.4, under the same limit on the time step ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3.3). Fluxes through every interface come from donor cell, van Leer or PPA interpolation, and update the variables directionally split. In the 1-direction, the fluxes are $\mathcal{F}^1_{i,j} = (E/d)^*_{1,i,j}\,\dot M^1_{i,j}\,g2a_i^{n+1/2}\,g31a_i^{n+1/2}\,dvl2a_j^n$ for $E$, $\mathcal{G}^1_{i,j} = (F1/d)^*_{1,i,j}\,\tfrac12(\dot M^1_{i,j} + \dot M^1_{i+1,j})\,g2b_i^{n+1/2}\,g31b_i^{n+1/2}\,dvl2a_j^n$ for $F1$ and $\mathcal{H}^1_{i,j} = (F2/d)^*_{1,i,j}\,\tfrac12(\dot M^1_{i,j} + \dot M^1_{i,j-1})\,g2a_i^{n+1/2}\,g31a_i^{n+1/2}\,dvl2b_j^n$ for $F2$ (eqs. 35 to 37), with the mass fluxes of 2.4.3. Their differences across each control volume change $E\,dvl1a\,dvl2a$, $F1\,dvl1b\,dvl2a$ and $F2\,dvl1a\,dvl2b$ over the step, with $dvl1$ at the new time (eqs. 38 to 40). The 2-direction then uses $\mathcal{F}^2_{i,j} = (E/d)^*_{2,i,j}\,\dot M^2_{i,j}\,g31b_i^n\,dx1a_i^n\,g32a_j^{n+1/2}$, $\mathcal{G}^2_{i,j} = (F1/d)^*_{2,i,j}\,\tfrac12(\dot M^2_{i,j} + \dot M^2_{i-1,j})\,g31a_i^n\,dx1b_i^n\,g32a_j^{n+1/2}$ and $\mathcal{H}^2_{i,j} = (F2/d)^*_{2,i,j}\,\tfrac12(\dot M^2_{i,j} + \dot M^2_{i,j+1})\,g31b_i^n\,dx1a_i^n\,g32b_j^{n+1/2}$ (eqs. 41 to 43), with $dvl2$ at the new time (eqs. 44 to 46). The transport is consistent with the mass, which reduces the diffusion of the radiation relative to the matter.

As the scalar divergence theorem misses terms of the covariant radiation momentum equation in curvilinear coordinates, source terms are added ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §3.3): $(F1^{n+1}_{i,j} - F1^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = \langle v2\rangle_{i,j}\langle F2\rangle_{i,j}(\partial g2a_i/\partial x_1)/g2a_i$ and $(F2^{n+1}_{i,j} - F2^n_{i,j})/\Delta t = -v2_{i,j}\,\tfrac14(F1_{i,j} + F1_{i+1,j} + F1_{i,j-1} + F1_{i+1,j-1})(\partial g2b_i/\partial x_1)/g2b_i$ (eqs. 47 and 48), with the four-point averages $\langle v2\rangle_{i,j}$ and $\langle F2\rangle_{i,j}$ around the position of $F1$ (eqs. 49 and 50). **[GK]** The printed eq. 48 has a plus sign. The 2-component of $(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{F}$ is $\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla F_2 + v_2F_1(\partial h_2/\partial x_1)/h_2$, as the requirement that it vanish for a uniform field shows, so that the term missing from the advection of $F_2$ as a scalar enters the right-hand side with a minus sign, like the Coriolis term of the hydrodynamic momentum; eq. 47 has the right sign. Eq. 41 repeats the misprint of eqs. 65 and 68 of the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a) (2.4.3), the width $dx1b_i$ for a face of the zone-centred control volume, where the text uses $dx1a_i$.

#### 2.10.8 Difference formulae

Appendix A gives the remaining terms of eqs. 29 and 30 ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, app. A). The work of the radiation pressure is $(\nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathsf{P}^{n+\theta})_{i,j} = (\nabla v_{(11)})_{i,j}f11_{i,j}E^{n+\theta}_{i,j} + (\nabla v_{(22)})_{i,j}f22_{i,j}E^{n+\theta}_{i,j} + (\nabla v_{(33)})_{i,j}(1 - f11_{i,j} - f22_{i,j})E^{n+\theta}_{i,j} + [\langle(\nabla v_{(12)})_{i,j}\rangle + \langle(\nabla v_{(21)})_{i,j}\rangle]\langle P12^{n+\theta}_{i,j}\rangle$ (eq. 92), with the diagonal components of 2.2.4 and $E^{n+\theta} = \theta E^{n+1} + (1 - \theta)E^n$ (eq. 93). ### 2.11 The transfer equation and the Eddington factors

#### 2.11.1 The static transfer problem

The closure of 2.10.3 needs the angular distribution of the radiation, which the transfer equation, solved formally, provides ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4). This step is kept apart from the implicit update of the moment equations, and LTE makes the source function and the opacities depend on the local state of the matter alone. In keeping with these approximations, the transfer equation is solved in its static form, as its time-dependent terms matter only where the system evolves on the time scale of the radiation flow, where the approximations fail anyway. The fluid is taken as frozen in its momentary state, and the radiation field that crosses it at that instant is computed.

Even in a static medium, the transfer problem is hard because of its many dimensions ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4, fig. 3). At each point of spacetime, the specific intensity integrated over frequency depends on two angles of the direction of propagation, so that with axial symmetry and a static medium it remains a function of four variables: in cylindrical coordinates, of $z$ and $R$, of the polar angle $\Theta$ to the axis and of an azimuthal angle $\Phi$ measured from the radial direction. The difference equations must cover this space of position and angle, and the resolution in angle is vital, as the variation with angle is precisely what fixes the Eddington factors.

The most efficient solvers exploit the symmetries of their coordinate system, which makes a covariant solver of this kind very hard to build, although covariant forms of the transfer equation exist ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4; [Kaneko, Morita and Maekawa](#kaneko-et-al-1984) 1984; [Munier and Weaver](#munier-weaver-1984) 1984). For the other dynamical equations, covariance only means differencing the covariant forms of the operators (2.2.3). The transfer solver is therefore restricted to cylindrical and Cartesian coordinates.

#### 2.11.2 The radiation grid

The hydrodynamic grid resolves only the position, so that the transfer equation needs a grid in angle as well ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.1). In flat spacetime, photons travel on straight lines, the geodesics, which makes it natural to difference the equation along rays. At one point, rays at several angles $\Theta$ to the axis resolve the polar angle, and together they span a plane parallel to the axis. For the field to be known at every point of the hydrodynamic grid, many such planes are needed, each tangent to one of the radial shells of the grid and labelled by its impact parameter, the radius of that shell, so that $N$ radial grid points need $N$ tangent planes (fig. 4). Each plane cuts many shells, and at each crossing the outward radial unit vector makes a different azimuthal angle $\Phi$ with the plane. The variation in azimuth on one shell is therefore assembled from its crossings with many planes, and for the innermost shell $C$ further core planes, which pierce it, supply it. As larger shells cross more planes, the field is resolved better in azimuth at large radii, which suits radiation expected to be close to isotropic in the core and strongly peaked far out.

On each plane, the radiation grid, where the transfer equation is solved, is a geometric projection of the hydrodynamic mesh, with the horizontal coordinate $x$, in zones $ix = 1$ to $nx$, and the vertical coordinate $z$, in zones $iz = 1$ to $nz$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.1, fig. 4). The plane's impact parameter and the count of radial mesh points set $nx$, which outside the core decreases steadily with the impact parameter. The number of axial mesh points, and whether the problem is symmetric about the equator, set $nz$. To resolve the polar angle fully everywhere, the radiation grid spans the whole range $-Z < z < Z$, even with equatorial symmetry. The grid of crossings is rectangular, but its spacing in $x$ is unequal even where the hydrodynamic grid is equidistant. The properties of the matter, for the opacities and the source function, are projected from the hydrodynamic mesh as well. Boundary values of the intensity are set on the left and lower edges of the radiation grid when $0 < \Theta < \pi/2$, and on the left and upper edges when $\pi/2 < \Theta < \pi$.

Space is thus resolved by many tangent planes, the polar angle by many rays on each plane, and the azimuth by the geometrical device of assembling it from the crossings of each shell with the planes ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.1). The four-dimensional intensity follows from solving the transfer equation at the $N^2$ points of the hydrodynamic mesh, on $N + C$ tangent planes, each with $M$ polar angles. The device obtains the full angular variation efficiently, but has drawbacks. It is precisely what makes a covariant solver so difficult, as it uses the cylindrical geometry to choose the impact parameters and the azimuthal symmetry to treat all points of a shell as equivalent. It fails for three-dimensional grids, and is harder to apply in other axisymmetric coordinates, such as spherical polar ones. The discrete values of $\Phi$ cannot be chosen, since the crossings fix them, which rules out an adaptive discretisation in azimuth. Being of order $\arccos(\Delta r/r)$, they cannot resolve the field at small $\Phi$ on the innermost shells, where $r$ is comparable to $\Delta r$.

Other geometries thus need another radiation grid ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.1). A fully covariant solver could in principle start from the covariant transfer equation, with its derivatives in space and in angle; solving along straight rays, the geodesics, avoids the latter. Position and angle could then be treated as independent coordinates, perhaps with an adaptive mesh in angle, but a fully covariant solver in several dimensions with an adaptive mesh in angle or frequency is left to future work.

#### 2.11.3 The method of short characteristics

With the $N + C$ tangent planes formed, the transfer equation is solved on each plane for each polar angle ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2). On a plane it reads $(\mu/\chi)\,\partial I/\partial z + (\gamma/\chi)\,\partial I/\partial x = S - I$ (eq. 51), with the direction cosines $\gamma$ and $\mu$ on the $x$- and $z$-axes and $I = I(x, z, \gamma, \mu, \nu)$. Scattering can enter the source function as $S = (1 - \xi)J + \xi B$ (eq. 52), with the mean intensity $J = (c/4\pi)E$ and the destruction probability $\xi$, $0 < \xi < 1$. With frequency dependence, scattering would couple the transfer problems at different frequencies implicitly, as photons are scattered from one frequency to another; solving the problem integrated over frequency, with total opacities and source functions, avoids this. Along each ray of the discrete set at a point $(x, z)$, the equation becomes $\partial I/\partial s = \chi(S - I)$ (eq. 53), where the path length $s$ along the direction of propagation satisfies $ds = dx/\gamma = dz/\mu$ (eq. 54).

Two methods can give the intensity at each point ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2). Long characteristics trace a ray from each grid point back to the boundary of the domain, where boundary conditions give the intensity, and integrate along it with the physical variables interpolated onto the ray. With $N^2$ points in two dimensions and about $N$ interpolations per ray, the cost grows as $N^3$, prohibitive for large grids. Short characteristics integrate only across the cell adjacent to the point, and bring in the neighbouring cells through interpolation along grid lines. The interpolation can make the method more diffusive than long characteristics, which interpolation of higher order reduces to the order of accuracy of the spatial interpolation in the hydrodynamics. With one ray segment per cell, the cost grows only as $N^2$, and ZEUS-2D uses short characteristics.

Near a point $O$ of the radiation grid, the short characteristic runs from the upwind point $M$ on the boundary of the cell to $O$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2, fig. 5). Along it, the formal solution is $I_O = I_Me^{-\Delta\tau_M} + \int_0^{\Delta\tau_M}S(t)e^{-(\Delta\tau_M - t)}\,dt$ (eq. 55), with the upwind intensity $I_M$, the source function $S(t)$ along the segment as a function of the optical depth $t$ from $M$, and the optical depth $\Delta\tau_M = \int_M^O\chi\,ds$ between $M$ and $O$ (eq. 56). Its numerical evaluation needs $I_M$, $\Delta\tau_M$ and an approximation to the integral $C = e^{-\Delta\tau_M}\int_M^O S(t)e^t\,dt$ (eq. 57).

#### 2.11.4 Interpolation and the discrete formal solution

Even on a rectangular mesh, $M$ is in general not a vertex, but lies on a horizontal or a vertical mesh line, so that $I_M$ must be interpolated from neighbouring points of known intensity ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2). Which line the ray crosses depends on the local spacings $\Delta x_{ix}$ and $\Delta z_{iz}$ and on the direction cosines, set by the discrete polar angle, and an efficient test decides it ([Mihalas, Auer and Mihalas](#mihalas-et-al-1978) 1978; [Kunasz and Auer](#kunasz-auer-1988) 1988). With $a_M = \gamma\Delta z_{iz-1}/(\mu\Delta x_{ix-1})$ for $\mu > 0$ and $a_M = \gamma\Delta z_{iz}/(|\mu|\Delta x_{ix-1})$ for $\mu < 0$ (eq. 58), $M$ lies on a horizontal line if $a_M < 1$. The path length along $MO$ is then $\Delta s_M = \Delta z_{iz-1}/\mu$, or $\Delta z_{iz}/|\mu|$ for $\mu < 0$ (eq. 59), and $I_M = (1 - a_M)I_{ix,iz-1} + a_MI_{ix-1,iz-1}$ for $\mu > 0$, with $iz + 1$ in place of $iz - 1$ for $\mu < 0$ (eq. 60). If $a_M > 1$, $M$ lies on a vertical line, $\Delta s_M = \Delta x_{ix-1}/\gamma$ (eq. 61), and $I_M = (1 - 1/a_M)I_{ix-1,iz} + (1/a_M)I_{ix-1,iz-1}$ for $\mu > 0$, again with $iz + 1$ for $\mu < 0$ (eq. 62).

Linear interpolation needs only the two neighbouring vertices, but the interpolations inherent in short characteristics diffuse the field in angle: sharp gradients of the intensity spread over neighbouring points downwind ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2). Parabolic interpolation reduces this diffusion and gives more accurate results ([Kunasz and Auer](#kunasz-auer-1988) 1988), but needs three upwind points and can overshoot near discontinuities of the intensity, with negative, unphysical values of $I_M$ where $I$ falls steeply to zero. The problems resemble those of interpolation in Eulerian advection, where monotonisation removes overshoots and undershoots and parabolic interpolation with steepeners near discontinuities is well developed (2.4.2). A robust interpolation of the radiation variables along grid lines could very likely be built by analogous methods.

The optical depth of the segment follows from taking $\ln\chi$ as piecewise linear along the ray ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, eqs. 63 and 64; [Mihalas et al.](#mihalas-et-al-1978) 1978). With $\beta = \ln(\chi_M/\chi_O)$, the opacity then falls exponentially from $\chi_M$ to $\chi_O$ over $\Delta s_M$, and $\Delta\tau_M = (\chi_M - \chi_O)\Delta s_M/\beta$. For $\beta \ll 1$, a series from the summation formula of Euler and Maclaurin gives $\Delta\tau_M = \tfrac12\Delta s_M[\chi_O + \chi_M - (\beta/6)(\chi_M - \chi_O)(1 - \beta^2/60)]$ (eq. 65). Taking the source function as piecewise linear along the ray likewise gives $C = \Psi_MS_M + \Psi_OS_O$ (eq. 66), with the weights $\Psi_M = (1 - e^{-\Delta\tau_M})/\Delta\tau_M - e^{-\Delta\tau_M}$ and $\Psi_O = 1 - (1 - e^{-\Delta\tau_M})/\Delta\tau_M$, where $S_M$ is interpolated linearly along mesh lines, like $I_M$, and $S_O$ is known at the vertex $O$. For very small optical depths $x = \Delta\tau_M$, Taylor series replace the weights: $\Psi_M \approx x(\tfrac12 - \tfrac13x + \tfrac18x^2 - \dots)$ and $\Psi_O \approx \tfrac12x(1 - \tfrac13x + \tfrac1{12}x^2 - \dots)$ (eqs. 67 and 68).

With linear interpolation of the intensity and the source function, the formal solution becomes $I_O = I_Me^{-\Delta\tau_M} + S_O - S_Me^{-\Delta\tau_M} - (S_O - S_M)(1 - e^{-\Delta\tau_M})/\Delta\tau_M$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, eq. 69). At large optical depth it tends to $I_O \approx S_O$, the diffusion limit, and without internal sources it gives the exponential attenuation $I_O = I_Me^{-\Delta\tau_M}$. The paper calls the determination of $I_O$ stable and always positive, with the correct limits. **[GK]** As printed, eqs. 64 and 65 have $\chi_O - \chi_M$ where $\chi_M - \chi_O$ stands here. With the stated $\beta = \ln(\chi_M/\chi_O)$, which eq. 63 also implies, they would give a negative optical depth; they hold for $\beta = \ln(\chi_O/\chi_M)$ instead. The series avoids the cancellation in $(\chi_M - \chi_O)/\beta$ as $\beta \to 0$, and agrees with the exact form through fifth order in $\beta$. Eq. 63 writes $S_M - S_O$, the symbols of the source function, for positions along the ray, and eq. 59 needs $|\mu|$ for $\mu < 0$, as eq. 58 has it. Eq. 69 prints its last term with a plus sign, against the weights of eq. 66. With these weights, which are never negative, $I_O$ is a positive combination of $I_M$, $S_M$ and $S_O$, which is what keeps it positive. At small optical depth it tends to $I_M$, as a transparent segment leaves the intensity unchanged, rather than to the $I_M + (S_O - S_M)$ that the paper states.

The interpolation of $I_M$ hides the main difficulty of implementing short characteristics efficiently ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2). As it needs the intensity at two upwind vertices, the formula is recursive. For $\mu > 0$ the solution is built up row by row or column by column from the lower left corner, away from the boundary values on the lower and left edges, and for $\mu < 0$ from the upper left corner, away from those on the left and upper edges. Such sweeps respect every dependence, but cannot be vectorised. Sweeping along diagonals of the mesh, for $\mu < 0$ from lower left to upper right and for $\mu > 0$ from lower right to upper left, vectorises the method and makes it very efficient. **[GK]** For $\mu > 0$, each vertex depends only on vertices with a smaller sum $ix + iz$, so that the vertices of one diagonal of constant $ix + iz$, which runs from lower right to upper left, do not depend on each other; for $\mu < 0$ the same holds for the diagonals of constant $ix - iz$.

Once the field is built for one value of $\mu$ over the whole radiation grid, the next value follows, and after all values of $\mu$ the next tangent plane, until all planes are done ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.2). The full dependence on position and angle then yields the angular moments, and with them the Eddington factors.

#### 2.11.5 The Eddington factors

The formal solution serves above all to give how the radiation depends on direction at every point of the hydrodynamic grid, from whose angular moments the Eddington factors follow; the transfer solver can also act as a graphical post-processor for the emergent intensity ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.3). In cylindrical coordinates the direction of propagation is $\mathbf{n} = (1 - \mu^2)^{1/2}\cos\Phi\,\hat{\mathbf{r}} + (1 - \mu^2)^{1/2}\sin\Phi\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} + \mu\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, with $\mu = \cos\Theta$ (eq. 70, fig. 3). The nonzero components of the Eddington tensor in two dimensions need four moments: $E = c^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{-1}^1 I\,d\mu\,d\Phi$, $P_{zz} = c^{-1}\int\int I\mu^2\,d\mu\,d\Phi$, $P_{rz} = c^{-1}\int\int I(1 - \mu^2)^{1/2}\mu\cos\Phi\,d\mu\,d\Phi$ and $P_{rr} = c^{-1}\int\int I(1 - \mu^2)\cos^2\Phi\,d\mu\,d\Phi$ (eqs. 71 to 74).

The rays of a plane sample the polar angle over $0 \le \Theta \le \pi$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.3). With equatorial symmetry, rays with $\mu > 0$ above the plane of symmetry match rays with $\mu < 0$ below it, so that rays over $0 \le \Theta \le \pi/2$ suffice, as the radiation grid spans both halves (2.11.2). Any set of $\Theta$ may serve, but one equally spaced in $\mu$, and so in solid angle, is the most common. The azimuth comes from the crossings of each shell with the tangent planes. Each plane crosses a shell twice, once for incoming rays, $\pi/2 < \Phi < \pi$, and once for outgoing ones, $0 < \Phi < \pi/2$, and axial symmetry supplies the values for $-\pi \le \Phi \le 0$. The field is thus resolved over the whole range of $\Phi$, but at values that the geometry alone fixes.

From the intensities $I_{i,j} = I(\mu_i, \Phi_j)$ at the discrete angles, quadratures give the moments as nested sums, $E = \sum_jW1_j(\sum_iW2_iI_{i,j})$, and likewise $P_{zz}$ with $W3$ and $W4$, $P_{rz}$ with $W5$ and $W6$, and $P_{rr}$ with $W7$ and $W8$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, eqs. 75 to 78). For the weights, $I$ is taken as a separable function, piecewise linear in $\mu$ and in $\cos\Phi$ within each cell of angle (eq. 79), so that the integrals can be done analytically. Once the discrete angles are chosen at the start, the weights are computed for the whole run. Their formulae are complex and hard to code reliably, so that test integrals check them at each initialisation. The sums of $W1$ and of $W3$ must give $\int_0^{2\pi}d\Phi = 2\pi$, that of $W5$ must give $\int\cos\Phi\,d\Phi = 0$ and that of $W7$ must give $\int\cos^2\Phi\,d\Phi = \pi$, while those of $W2$, $W4$, $W6$ and $W8$ must give $\int_{-1}^1d\mu = 2$, $\int\mu^2\,d\mu = 2/3$, $\int(1 - \mu^2)^{1/2}\mu\,d\mu = 0$ and $\int(1 - \mu^2)\,d\mu = 4/3$ (eqs. 80 to 86). In practice they match the analytic values to at least one part in $10^6$, even with few discrete angles. **[GK]** In eq. 79, the factor in $\cos\Phi$ starts from $I_j$, where the factor in $\mu$ starts from $I_{i-1}$; interpolation from the node $j - 1$ needs $I_{j-1}$. As the interpolant reproduces a constant intensity exactly, the test integrals check the coding of the weights rather than the angular resolution, which is why few angles suffice.

Storing the four-dimensional intensity at all points of space and angle before the quadratures would need much memory ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.3). The intensity of each tangent plane is therefore added into the quadratures as soon as it is computed and then discarded, and the quadratures are complete only after every ray of every plane. The Eddington tensor then follows from eq. 16. The quadratures are zone-centred, but each Eddington factor sits with its component of the pressure tensor (2.10.6). With $x_1 = z$ and $x_2 = r$ in cylindrical coordinates (2.2.3), this gives $f11_{i,j} = P_{zz,i,j}/E_{i,j}$ and $f22_{i,j} = P_{rr,i,j}/E_{i,j}$ at the zone centres, and at the corners $f12_{i,j} = (P_{rz,i-1,j} + P_{rz,i,j} + P_{rz,i-1,j-1} + P_{rz,i,j-1})/(E_{i-1,j} + E_{i,j} + E_{i-1,j-1} + E_{i,j-1})$ (eqs. 87 to 89). The Eddington factors take boundary conditions consistent with those of the radiation energy density and pressure, reflecting, inflow or outflow, and enter the next solution of the moment equations.

### 2.12 Radiation hydrodynamic tests

#### 2.12.1 Approach and the streaming limit

Few two-dimensional calculations of radiation hydrodynamics are fit to serve as tests, so that, as for the MHD, a new collection of tests had to be designed ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §6). As the moment and transfer solvers are independent modules, each is tested on its own. The moment equations behave in entirely different ways in the streaming limit, the static diffusion limit and the dynamic diffusion limit of 2.10.2. Suitable choices of the properties of the matter and of the velocity test the moment solver in each, to show that it gives correct results in every regime.

For the streaming limit, $\chi_F$ and $\Delta t$ are chosen so that $c\chi_F\Delta t \ll 1$, and the Eddington factor along the direction of propagation is set to 1.0 and all others to zero, so that the energy equation becomes an advection at the speed of light ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §6.1; 2.10.5). A flux imposed at the left boundary starts a discontinuity of the radiation energy density at $t = 0$, and the square wave is followed across a grid of 100 zones, as in a problem of [Mihalas and Klein](#mihalas-klein-1982) (1982) and [Stone and Mihalas](#stone-mihalas-1991) (1991). The time step gives $c\Delta t/\Delta x = 2$, and the time centring $\theta$ of the right-hand side of the moment equations (2.10.6) is either $\theta = 1.0$, which is fully implicit, or $\theta = 0.5$, the Crank–Nicolson scheme (fig. 7). The analytic front lies at $ct = 7.5 \times 10^{16}$. Fully implicit differencing gives a smooth front that moves at the correct speed $c$, but is quite diffusive. Crank–Nicolson differencing diffuses less, but leaves unphysical oscillations in the wake of the front, which decay only slowly. [Mihalas and Klein](#mihalas-klein-1982) (1982) saw them too, undamped, as their scheme was conservative, and [Stone and Mihalas](#stone-mihalas-1991) (1991) describe a scheme for the time-dependent transfer equation that avoids them. In dynamical simulations, such oscillations would make the properties of the matter behind the front fluctuate strongly as well, so that the fully implicit scheme, although more diffusive, is clearly better. With it, a larger step, $c\Delta t/\Delta x = 4$, gives results that compare very well with those of fig. 7.

#### 2.12.2 The diffusion limits

In the diffusion limit, $c\chi_F\Delta t \gg 1$ and the radiation is isotropic, with $f_{ii} = 1/3$ and $f_{ij} = 0$ for $i \ne j$ ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §6.2). In the static diffusion limit, the diffusion time of the radiation, $\Delta t \sim (\Delta x)^2/4D$ with $D \approx c/3\chi_F$, is much shorter than the time $\Delta x/v$ in which the flow advects it. The test therefore starts a sharply peaked distribution of the radiation energy density, a Gaussian, in a fluid at rest, with these Eddington factors, and follows the pulse as it diffuses. For a Gaussian, an analytic solution gives the evolution, with an amplitude that the paper writes as $A(t) = A_0t^{-1/2}$, which makes the test quantitative. **[GK]** In two dimensions, the amplitude of a diffusing Gaussian falls as $t^{-1}$, with $t$ counted from the virtual origin of the pulse; $t^{-1/2}$ holds in one dimension.

The pulse starts at the origin, in a corner of a Cartesian grid of $20 \times 20$ zones with reflecting boundaries on all sides, and the evolution is followed over several diffusion times ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §6.2, fig. 8). With an effective diffusion constant $D \approx 1$, the run covers about one diffusion time, over which the amplitude falls by a factor of 4. The pulse stays circular and smooth, the flux is symmetric, and the amplitude follows the analytic evolution to within 4 per cent throughout, even on this coarse grid. With $D \approx 100$, the run covers about 100 diffusion times, after which the energy density varies over the grid by only $4.0 \times 10^{-3}$. Its contours take the pattern that the reflecting boundaries imply, and the flux is again smooth and symmetric everywhere. After about 1000 diffusion times the result is qualitatively the same, with a variation of only one part in $10^{10}$.

In the dynamic diffusion limit, advection is much faster than diffusion, which a Gaussian pulse carried by a flow tests ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §6.3, fig. 9). On a Cartesian grid of $64 \times 64$ zones, the pulse starts in the lower left corner and is advected to the upper right corner by velocities of equal magnitude in both directions, with opacities and velocities that make the diffusion time about $10^4$ times the advection time. With van Leer advection, the pulse becomes slightly square, but its contours stay smooth, and the flux is qualitatively right, closely tracking the gradients of the energy density. The squaring comes from the directionally split advection, which would affect any other advected variable in two dimensions alike. The amplitude falls substantially, from 4.10 to 2.20 in fig. 9, again through the diffusion of the advection, which is strong on so coarse a grid, with only five zones in the peak at first; PPA reduces the diffusion substantially. **[GK]** The paper gives the ratio of the time scales as $10^{-4}$, which would make diffusion the faster process, against the aim of the test stated in the same paragraph.

## 3. Software architecture

The software architecture draws on steps 2, 6, 11 and 17 of `plan.md`.

### 3.1 Data layout

Each variable is stored per zone, ghost zones included, and scalars and vector components share the indices of their zone, as 2.2.1 describes ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.1). In the index names of fig. 1, the ghost zones are $iim2$ and $iim1$ below $ii$ and $iop1$ and $iop2$ above $io$, and likewise in $j$. The domain boundary runs along the a-mesh lines $i = ii$ and $i = iop1$, and $j = ji$ and $j = jop1$. The active zones, which the evolution equations update, run from $ii$ to $io$ and from $ji$ to $jo$ for the zone-centred quantities, from $ii + 1$ to $io$ in $i$ for $v1$, and from $ji + 1$ to $jo$ in $j$ for $v2$. For a domain with stepped boundaries, the scalars $is$, $ie$, $js$ and $je$ hold the smallest and largest indices over the whole grid. The scale factors, their derivatives and the volume factors are kept like the coordinates, as one-dimensional arrays on both meshes, such as $g2a_i$ and $g2b_i$, and are computed once at the start when the mesh does not move (§4.2). With MHD, the field components are stored like the velocity components, and the EMF at the zone corners, with its two rows of ghost values at $iim2$ and $iim1$ and at $iop2$ and $iop3$ ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.1, §3.4). The radiation variables sit as 2.10.6 describes. The transfer solver keeps no array of the four-dimensional intensity, as the intensity of each tangent plane goes straight into the quadratures of the angular moments, whose weights are computed once, at the set-up ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §4.3, §5).

### 3.2 Program control

Each cycle of the main loop advances the solution by one time step ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.8, fig. 7). After the initialisation and the set-up of a problem, a cycle solves the Poisson equation (eq. 79), performs the source step in its three substeps (eqs. 30, 31, 35 to 37 and 40) and the directionally split transport step (eqs. 51 to 72), computes new grid velocities if the grid moves, and finds a new time step from the Courant condition. The loop repeats until one of several criteria stops it. It also handles the output, and an interrupt checker accepts several dozen commands during a run, to dump data, set flags and variables, or halt, resume and restart the run.

The code is meant to be modular and portable ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992a) 1992a, §4.8). Its Fortran follows the practice that [Winkler and Norman](#winkler-norman-1986) (1986) set out for writing efficient code free of bugs, and keeps as closely as possible to the Fortran 77 standard. Graphics rely on widely available software: vector graphics from the NCAR graphics library, raster images for animation in postprocessing tools on workstations, such as Imagetool and Datascope, and floating-point data in the HDF format of the NCSA. All of it is public, with source code, so that ZEUS-2D and its graphics can be compiled and run on any machine with Fortran and C compilers. The full code, with MHD and radiation, exceeds 30,000 lines of source, not counting the common blocks substituted into it. The C preprocessor CPP, together with the directories and utilities of Unix, manages its editing and compilation. CPP macros include or suppress the parts of the code that serve a physical process, a geometry or an output mode, so that an executable built without the macro for MHD carries no arrays or subroutines for it. The authors judge that without a precompiler a code of the complexity of ZEUS-2D could hardly be developed and applied.

Most of the MHD algorithms form self-contained modules, so that ZEUS-2D performs MHD by calling them at the right points of the control sequence ([Stone and Norman](#stone-norman-1992b) 1992b, §3.5, fig. 4). Substep 1 of the source step gains the gradients of the magnetic pressure (eqs. 26 to 29), and a module at its end, before substep 2, updates the toroidal field and the rotational velocity by the MOC. The MOC-CT update of the poloidal field (eqs. 11 and 12) opens the transport step, and the values $B1'$ and $B2'$ of the MOC at the zone corners update the velocities by eqs. 30 and 31. The transport step also advects $B_3$ (eqs. 49 to 52), the new time step uses the fast magnetosonic speed (eq. 53), and the output and graphics gain the new variables. As in the [first paper](#stone-norman-1992a), CPP macros switch the MHD variables and subroutines in or out, so that optimised executables result for hydrodynamics and for MHD alike.

The radiation module, too, consists of self-contained modules, complex inside but easy to build into ZEUS-2D ([Stone, Mihalas and Norman](#stone-et-al-1992a) 1992, §5, fig. 6). The moment solver of 2.10.6 replaces the third substep of the source step, which heated the matter by compression (2.3.3), and the transfer solver, called directly after it, updates the Eddington factors for the next cycle. The transport step advects the radiation variables (2.10.7), the set-up gains a routine for the quadrature weights, and the graphics the new variables. CPP macros mark all routines and variables of the module, so that optimised executables result with and without radiation. As the transfer solver yields the emergent intensity, the quantity closest to what a telescope on Earth observes, it can also serve as a post-processor that maps the emergent intensity of any simulation, even one computed without radiation. This motivates more elaborate solvers, with frequency-dependent transport in moving media, to compute line profiles for comparison with observations.
